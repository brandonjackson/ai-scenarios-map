# Disruption Swarm — extract

## Summary

In the Disruption Swarm, fast-follower competition spreads labour-replacing AI quickly (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”). Lower prices for AI and competition among providers coexist with substantial displacement and a difficult transition for affected workers (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”; “Five Major Policy Implications”). The author highlights episodes when diffuse technologies lifted productivity while wages and living conditions for a broad group stagnated for years or decades (“Disruption Swarm” historical examples).

## Key details

- **Source:** Titans, Swarms, or Human Renaissance? Technological Revolutions and Policy Lessons for the AI Age, Ramin Toloui, The Digitalist Papers, Vol. 2, 2025-12-11. `sources/toloui-titans-swarms-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full essay, including the typology, historical analogues, AI outlook, and five policy priorities.
- **Scenario set:** Two axes form four quadrants. Winner-take-all versus fast-follower market structure crosses labour replacement versus labour augmentation. The four quadrants are Titan’s Dominion, Copilot Empire, Disruption Swarm and Promethean Fire. The constructive Copilot row covers an expressly mentioned policy-dependent form of Copilot Empire (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Priority 3”).
- **Geography:** The policy agenda is US-centred, with international comparison, economic rivalry and historical examples from other countries (“Applying the Matrix to the Technological Past”; “Complicating Factors”; “Five Major Policy Implications”).
- **Authors' framing:** A typology and adaptable policy agenda for transformative AI, grounded in historical analogues. The quadrants are conceptual possibilities (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Conclusion”).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probabilities. The author finds evidence for all four combinations and says the outcome depends on interaction between the technology and institutions (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

### Timeline

No dated scenario storyline. Evidence used to motivate the axes includes ChatGPT’s late-2022 launch and China’s DeepSeek entering in early 2025 (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. Competition spreads a labour-replacing technology, and the author presents worker protection as a possible response (“Five Major Policy Implications”).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** No specific intervention is stipulated for the AI quadrant. Historical examples describe reforms that helped reverse stagnant real wages (“Disruption Swarm” historical examples).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* Prepare adjustable policy levers with different settings for different quadrants. (1) Shift the tax burden from hiring to capital or super-normal rents, revisit a public health insurance option and adjust wage subsidies or transfers if needed (“Priority 1”). (2) Back worker-augmenting research, procurement and training via federal R&D, NAIRR compute and data, SBIR/STTR prototypes, WIOA, apprenticeships and employer–college partnerships, and use adoption measures and voluntary certification (“Priority 2”). (3) Start with interoperable APIs, portable customer data, limits on exclusivity and cloud switching barriers, and expand DOJ/FTC capacity for stronger interventions if bottlenecks emerge (“Priority 3”). (4) Require reporting of advanced training and compute, independent evaluations, material incident reporting, disclosure, meaningful human review and appeals. Consider due-process withdrawal powers and updated tort standards with safe harbours (“Priority 4”). (5) Expand energy, grid and chip supply while protecting households and small businesses from utility cost shifts. Modernize lawful, privacy-protective data sharing, run regulatory sandboxes and extend diffusion assistance to small firms (“Priority 5”). The quadrant-specific focus is protecting displaced workers and maintaining social cohesion through rapid change (“Five Major Policy Implications”).

## Key dynamics explored

- Easy imitation, interoperability and competition lower prices and pass the technology quickly to more users (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”).
- Rapid diffusion can spread labour replacement faster than opportunities for workers can develop, so productivity may rise without corresponding wage growth (“Disruption Swarm” historical examples; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).
- The historical analogue of Engels’ Pause ended through a combination of capital accumulation, skilled and unskilled job creation elsewhere, cheaper food after Corn Law repeal, labour law, collective bargaining, public health and education (“Disruption Swarm” historical examples).

## State of technology

Knowledge spreads quickly, catching up is feasible, and switching costs are low. Alternative paths to continued capability gains include efficiency improvements, ongoing investment by multiple large firms, open models near the frontier, and specialized smaller models that are good enough for many uses (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

## State of adoption

*Applies across the set.* Integrating a general-purpose technology requires workflow and process redesign, skills and complementary investments. Institutional inertia and social resistance may slow replacement, though automating may be easier initially than developing effective augmentation. Organizational differences, task requirements and worker readiness mean adoption can vary sharply (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”). Competitive imitation and low switching costs drive rapid diffusion, elevating deployment, operations and complementary assets as sources of advantage (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

The author illustrates the risk of divergence between productivity and worker rewards through the early Industrial Revolution, when productivity rose while wages stagnated for decades (“Disruption Swarm” historical examples).

### Labour market dynamics

Autonomous systems may have an absolute advantage across enough tasks to reduce labour demand at prevailing wages. Fast advances shorten the adjustment period. Companies and AI suppliers may find replacement the easiest way to monetize investments. Early payroll evidence suggests lower employment for young workers in exposed occupations, especially where AI automates tasks. Examples include entry-level work in software, law and other professional services (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”). Historical analogues include handloom displacement and sustained losses for lower-skilled workers in industrial areas, with unresolved attribution between trade and technology in the more recent manufacturing case (“Disruption Swarm” historical examples).

### Winners and losers

The absence of a single AI monopolist does not ensure gains for labour. Capital owners captured most profits in the early British industrial analogue. Manufacturing-dependent localities in the later example experienced lower employment, worse prospects and deteriorating social indicators (“Disruption Swarm” historical examples).

### Household incomes and living standards

Britain’s early industrial example featured decades of stagnant working-class wages and worsening health. Real wages and living standards recovered in the second half of the 19th century through economic, technological and policy developments (“Disruption Swarm” historical examples).

### Government finances

*Applies across the set.* Accelerated depreciation and expensing expanded in 2025, and low effective taxes on profits, dividends and capital gains contrast with the income/payroll tax wedge and employers’ health-benefit costs. That mix can favour automation of workers even for small gains. Toloui suggests funding Social Security and Medicare payroll tax cuts with earmarked capital revenue, possibly focusing on super-normal AI rents if concentration dominates (“Priority 1”).

### International economic patterns

*Applies across the set.* US–China competition for AI leadership and global influence pushes towards rapid research or deployment. Misuse, misalignment and economic disruption favour caution. The US, EU and China approach regulation differently. Chip resilience depends on investment at home and on allied partnerships for critical inputs (“Complicating Factors”; “Priority 5”).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Fast followers constrain supplier pricing power and lower the unit cost of AI. The policy historical analogue of Corn Law repeal lowered food prices and supported real wages (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Disruption Swarm” historical examples).

*Applies across the set.* Larger supports could be required with strong displacement and unequal gains. Examples are an expanded earned income tax credit, wage insurance and transfers independent of employment. A public option for health insurance could lower nonwage hiring costs (“Priority 1”).

### A fair share of progress

A competitive producer market can coexist with gains accruing largely to capital if firms replace workers. The author proposes policy that can be scaled with the extent of dislocation (“Disruption Swarm” historical examples; “Priority 1”).

## Other dynamics

*Applies across the set.* Transformative AI can amplify misuse, bias, discrimination and misalignment. Incidents can also undermine public support for useful diffusion. Demographic decline and a shrinking labour force in many countries could offset some labour-saving effects (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”; “Complicating Factors”; “Priority 4”).
