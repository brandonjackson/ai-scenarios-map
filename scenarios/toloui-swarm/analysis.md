# Disruption Swarm — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Off.** Competition spreads a labour-replacing technology without any stipulated intervention. Worker protection is a possible response, not part of the quadrant. Historically, Engels' Pause ended partly through reforms (Corn Law repeal, labour law, collective bargaining, public health, education), but the scenario describes the period before those arrive.

## Big assumptions
1. **Capability commoditises and replaces.** Open models near the frontier, efficiency gains and good-enough specialised models make cheap AI capable enough to have an absolute advantage across many tasks.
2. **Adoption is fast.** Competitive imitation and low switching costs drive rapid diffusion, shortening the adjustment period.
3. **Markets pass gains to capital.** Prices fall, but productivity does not become wages; owners capture the profits.
4. **Politics lags.** The policy response arrives years or decades late.

## What must be true for this to occur
- Fast followers stay close to the frontier while capability reaches broad replacement.
- Firms find replacement the easiest way to monetise AI, and institutional inertia does not slow it.
- New industries built on cheap AI employ fewer people than they displace for a long period.
- Shrinking labour forces do not absorb the slack.

## Tensions and weak points
- The map previously placed this as strongly diffuse (x_labor 0.5). The extract says the absence of a monopolist does not ensure gains for labour and that capital captured most profits in the analogue, so it now sits at 0.25: diffuse among providers, not among workers.
- Cheap AI could lower the cost of living, but the extract gives no evidence that it reaches the displaced; the food-price fall in the analogue was a policy choice.
- The attribution of the recent manufacturing decline between trade and technology is unresolved, so the modern analogue is weaker than the British one.
- Close neighbours: `cfg-decentral`, `moores-law` and `gos-open-frontier`.
