# Work Transformed: The Promise and Peril of Artificial Intelligence — extract

## Summary

AI can complement workers, create new kinds of jobs and improve occupational safety, while automation can displace workers and widen inequality. Exposure and complementarity vary across occupations, countries and gender. The brief uses multiple published estimates and shows how different levels of complementarity and diffusion can produce different income outcomes (§§2–4).

## Key details

- **Source:** Work Transformed: The Promise and Peril of Artificial Intelligence, Sofia Gomez Tamayo, Andrea Petrelli, International Labour Organization, 2025-06. `sources/ilo-work-transformed-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** The brief is a standalone synthesis. It reports low complementarity, high complementarity with limited diffusion, and high complementarity with widespread diffusion as results from a cited study (§4.1).
- **Geography:** Global, with comparisons by income group, gender and occupation (§§2–3).
- **Authors’ framing:** ILO research synthesis and policy brief. The three income scenarios in §4.1 are attributed to Cazzaniga et al. (2024).
- **Authors’ view of likelihood:** The brief presents alternative outcomes that depend on the degree of exposure, complementarity and diffusion, with no probabilities (§§1, 4.1).

### Timeline

2025 — June research brief. It reviews studies with varying time horizons and gives no single scenario end date (§§1–4).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Mixed. Diffusion, complementarity and the impact on workers vary, and the brief recommends active labour and diffusion measures (§§2–4, boxes 1–3).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The income comparisons vary complementarity and diffusion without a common specified policy package (§4.1).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Support responsible diffusion, developing-country capacity and connectivity, AI and transferable skills, career transitions and equal access to reskilling. Maintain social dialogue and worker participation, audit for bias, and apply AI to occupational safety and labour inspection with safeguards (boxes 1–3).

## Key dynamics explored

- Exposure counts tasks AI could affect, while complementarity determines whether using it raises a worker’s value or reduces demand for their work (§§2.1–2.2).
- Infrastructure, education, resources and firm readiness determine which workers and countries can adopt AI, so exposure can translate into unequal gains (§§2.2–3.2).
- Automation can eliminate tasks and change job composition while new sectors and occupations appear. Hybrid technical and human skills complement AI (§§2.2, 3.2).
- Income distribution depends on relative productivity, deployment and capital returns. Worker participation, training and diffusion can influence the outcome (§4.1, boxes 1–3).

## State of technology

The brief covers generative AI, workplace decision systems, automated risk monitoring, sensor-based wearables, collaborative robots, inspection tools and training simulations. It points to potential for both substitution and assistance (§§2, 4.2–4.3).

## State of adoption

High-income economies are typically more exposed and better positioned to support uptake. Within countries, urban, educated, younger and formal-sector workers have greater opportunities to access AI. Skills shortages, geography and connectivity can hold back adoption, and workplace surveys show limited frequent use (§§2.2, 3.1–3.3).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Experimental evidence reviewed includes gains in customer service, coding and professional writing. In an income comparison attributed to Cazzaniga et al., low complementarity yields national income growth of 10%, while high complementarity with widespread diffusion yields 16% higher output (§§2.2, 4.1).

### Labour market dynamics

The brief distinguishes high automation potential from exposure and augmentation. Clerical and administrative work is highly exposed, and women are overrepresented in those roles. AI creates demand for technical and human skills and can help experienced and less experienced workers differently. Exposure also varies by education, income, firm readiness and geography (§§2–3).

### Winners and losers

Workers with complementary skills and access, firms with capital, and high-income countries can gain more. Women can face disproportionate high exposure but also show greater augmentation potential in the cited analysis. Low-complementarity and limited-diffusion configurations increase inequality through reduced labour incomes or unequal returns (§§3.1, 4.1).

### Household incomes and living standards

Under low complementarity, displacement reduces labour income, especially near the top, and capital income gains increase overall inequality. With high complementarity but limited diffusion, high-income workers benefit more and capital returns widen wealth inequality. With high complementarity and broad diffusion, total incomes rise across groups, by 2% for low-income workers and nearly 14% for high-income workers even as labour inequality increases (§4.1).

### International economic patterns

Skills, infrastructure and firm readiness create a divide between countries that can realise augmentation sooner and those that face barriers. International knowledge sharing and capacity building for developing countries are recommended (§§2.2–3.1, box 3).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

The brief warns that reskilling opportunities are least available to some of the workers most exposed to displacement. Inclusive lifelong learning, vocational preparation and career-transition assistance are proposed (§3.2, box 2).

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

The brief recommends measures to support workers through transitions and prevent disproportionate harm from AI deployment (boxes 1–2).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Workers should participate in selecting and implementing systems that affect their livelihoods. The brief proposes worker-centred design, dialogue, audit and confidential reporting on harms (§4.2, box 3).

### A fair share of progress

Broad diffusion and equal opportunities for complementary training can spread AI gains, but higher capital returns and unequal displacement risk can widen inequality (§§3.1, 4.1, boxes 1–3).

## Other dynamics

Algorithmic hiring, evaluation and surveillance can reinforce discrimination. Predictive inspections and sensors, robotics and simulation can reduce risks from hazardous work, while poorly designed equipment, constant alerts and monitoring can add strain and inequality (§§4.2–4.3).
