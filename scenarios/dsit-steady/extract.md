# AI Disappoints — extract

## Summary

AI capabilities improve modestly by 2030, but slow gains in accuracy and complex reasoning leave most jobs unaffected (§5.8, “Capability” and “wider context in 2030”). A 2025 AI bust consolidates the industry and sends investment towards fusion and quantum computing (§5.8, “Key turning points in 2025” and “Ownership, constraints, and access”). Businesses and governments begin reinvesting in human capital while AI delivers only marginal productivity improvements (§5.8, “wider context in 2030”). By 2030 AI is becoming more embedded in services with little fanfare and scrutiny, while technical safety has improved and misuse remains possible (§5.8, “Safety” and “wider context in 2030”).

## Key details

- **Source:** AI 2030 Scenarios: Helping policy makers plan for the future of AI (Annex C), Government Office for Science, Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (GOV.UK), 2025-04-28. `sources/go-science-ai-2030-scenarios-annex-c-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None.
- **Reading notes:** Full GOV.UK HTML annex. The scenarios were developed in 2023 for the November AI Safety Summit, the report is dated January 2024 on its cover, and the annex was published on GOV.UK on 28 April 2025 (§1.2; GOV.UK parent publication change history).
- **Scenario set:** Unpredictable Advanced AI, AI Disrupts the Workforce, AI “Wild West”, Advanced AI on a Knife Edge, and AI Disappoints. The five uncertainties are capability, ownership/access/constraints, safety, level and distribution of use, and geopolitical context (§§1.3–1.4).
- **Geography:** Global developments, with implications for the UK (§1.2).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory policy-planning scenario. “Scenarios are a tool to help policy makers explore uncertainty and identify a resilient course of action that can withstand a range of future conditions” (§1.2).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated (§§1.2 and 3.1).

### Timeline

- Early 2020s — public enthusiasm for the original generative systems gives way to disappointment with accuracy and functionality (§5.8, “wider context in 2030”).
- 2025 — rollout delays precipitate rapid disinvestment, a brief financial crisis, start-up failures and big-tech consolidation (§5.8, “Key turning points in 2025”).
- Late 2020s — safety research advances and international cooperation grows alongside higher concern about climate. Certain organisations manage to deploy advanced systems (§5.8, “Safety”, “Level and distribution of use” and “Geopolitical context”).
- 2030 — semi-autonomous agents are in use where deployment barriers have been overcome. AI is increasingly embedded in software and services without AI branding (§5.8, “Capability” and “wider context in 2030”).
- 2030s — a later breakthrough remains possible (§5.8, “Key policy considerations”).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. New policy interventions are left out to support stress testing (§1.2).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Governments begin investing more in human capital and other technologies as AI disappoints. The authors describe public calls for improved regulation and safety, without specifying an enacted response (§5.8, “wider context in 2030”).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Help organisations overcome skills and adoption barriers, including data and software engineering capacity. Consider maintaining strategic UK AI skills and infrastructure through disinvestment. Keep addressing disinformation and other safety issues even as products cease to be identified as AI. Improve public AI literacy, enable reporting of unsound decisions, and maintain international cooperation (§5.8, “Safety” and “Key policy considerations”).

## Key dynamics explored

- Accuracy setbacks delay major releases, trigger disinvestment and limit further progress. Media criticism of overpromising compounds weak demand and investment (§5.8, “Key turning points in 2025”).
- Legacy data systems, limited integration skills and inequitable access to training suppress gains from the available tools (§5.8, “Level and distribution of use”).
- Slow capability progress gives safety researchers space to achieve breakthroughs. Low public trust gives firms reasons to avoid AI branding, potentially reducing scrutiny (§5.8, “Safety”, “Level and distribution of use” and “Key policy considerations”).

*Applies across the set.* The report identifies AI skills, international cooperation and sustained attention to AI harms as considerations across the set. Five narratives vary those conditions to let policymakers compare their responses against differing plausible futures (§§1.2–1.3, 5.1 and 7.1).

## State of technology

Text generation improves, but factual errors, multi-step reasoning and high-quality video generation remain difficult, and close human oversight is usual. A limited set of implementers deploy semi-autonomous agents. Most deployed systems are simpler machine-learning models; narrow specialist tools can help in healthcare. Large labs lead, but data shortages, accuracy and quality problems slow them and let open-source developers catch up (§5.8, “Capability”, “Ownership, constraints, and access” and “wider context in 2030”).

## State of adoption

Higher-skilled people, start-ups and large firms have the highest uptake in this scenario, but even theirs is only medium on the source’s scale. Lower-skilled people, other SMEs and the public sector adopt less. Cognitive automation is medium, physical automation low, and UK and international adoption are both low. Bad publicity, poor data structures, shortages of skills and tools users find difficult limit uptake (§5.8, “Level and distribution of use” and “Frontier AI Adoption in 2030”).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

AI brings only marginal productivity improvement. Businesses and governments accept that it has done little to remedy stagnation and invest more in people and other technologies (§5.8, “wider context in 2030”).

### Labour market dynamics

Most jobs are unaffected because the systems are unreliable and unable to complete complex tasks. Those with suitable skills gain advantages using them; many others struggle to get training or to use the tools (§5.8, “Level and distribution of use” and “wider context in 2030”).

### Winners and losers

Large technology firms gain market share following start-up failures, while open-source developers gain space to catch up as progress slows. People with AI skills obtain more of the available benefits. Fusion and quantum research draw investment that had been going to AI (§5.8, “Ownership, constraints, and access”, “Level and distribution of use” and “Key turning points in 2025”).

### Household incomes and living standards

The authors describe productivity stagnation as adverse for living standards and limited AI-driven lifestyle changes for some groups. They give no household income estimate (§5.8, “Level and distribution of use” and “Key policy considerations”).

### Government finances

The authors describe long-term productivity stagnation as adverse for public finances. No changes to tax or benefits are specified (§5.8, “Key policy considerations”).

### International economic patterns

Growing willingness to cooperate on climate and AI contrasts with UK shortages of AI and other technical skills. The UK may need to attract skilled migrants, with possible consequences for international relations (§5.8, “Geopolitical context”).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability

Delayed rollouts trigger an AI bust and brief financial crisis in 2025. Investors move into quantum computing and fusion following early advances. Some researchers see AI problems as technically fixable with investment that is no longer available (§5.8, “Key turning points in 2025” and “wider context in 2030”).

Not addressed.

### Time for what matters

Less scrutiny of AI embedded in online services has implications including worsening online addiction and polarisation (§5.8, “wider context in 2030”).

### A say in your future

Public calls for safety and regulation follow prominent misuse. The report asks how people affected by inaccurate decisions can report them and get systems changed; the scenario does not specify the redress mechanism (§5.8, “Safety” and “wider context in 2030”).

### A fair share of progress

Unequal access to training means people with the right skills enjoy AI benefits while many others struggle (§5.8, “Level and distribution of use”).

## Other dynamics

Improved technical safety coexists with misuse targeting vulnerable people, biased decisions and errors. Climate change attracts growing concern. Compute demand grows slowly and efficiency improves, keeping the environmental footprint marginal; unreliable AI brings less help with sustainability than hoped (§5.8, “Safety” and “wider context in 2030”).
