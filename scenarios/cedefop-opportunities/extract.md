# Scenario A: a future of opportunities – technology-driven competition for talent — extract

## Summary

It is 2040. The EU labour force is shrinking because the population is ageing and migration flows are slowing, but the economy is booming. Technological innovation and AI create new jobs (pp. 9, 69). Skills supply and demand move in opposite directions, so skills shortages grow, recruitment gets harder and competition for talent becomes "fiercer than ever" (p. 69). AI automates repetitive and low-skilled tasks. Rather than displacing workers, it lets people in affected occupations upskill and reskill into higher-value roles, and human-centric skills take centre stage (p. 69). Most workers and organisations benefit. Purpose-driven careers and voluntary job changes increase. AI-enabled, more flexible and integrated skills development systems serve the workforce, although the authors stress risks from the digital divide, information overload and screen time (pp. 9, 69–71).

## Key details

- **Source:** Preparing for 2040: four AI-powered scenarios for the future of continuing skills development, Cedefop (European Centre for the Development of Vocational Training), Publications Office of the European Union, 2025-11. `sources/cedefop-preparing-2040-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None. The same report restates the scenario in its executive summary (p. 9). Results from the later scenario-exploration and vision-building phases are due to be published in 2026 (pp. 8, 18).
- **Reading notes:** Full text read: executive summary, Chapters 1–2 (method), Chapter 3 (Scenario A assumptions and semi-Delphi results), Chapter 7.1 (consolidated scenario), Chapter 8, Annexes 1, 3 and 4, and the Scenario A section of the Annex 5 questionnaire. Figure 1 (impact–uncertainty matrix) and Figure 2 (respondents) are images that are not in the text extraction. Locators are the report's printed page numbers.
- **Scenario set:** One of four Cedefop scenarios for continuing skills development by 2040. The others are B "left alone to ride the tide – navigating the AI shock waves on jobs", C "staying afloat – AI opportunities missed" and D "AI unleashed – dominating the world of work and societies" (pp. 9–11). The set is not built on a 2×2 grid. It comes from a five-step process: (1) identifying 21 trends with the STEEPV framework, (2) impact–uncertainty analysis, (3) morphological analysis of possible evolutions for each trend, (4) four scenario outlines built on four "evolution paths", and (5) validation in a semi-Delphi survey with 132 respondents (pp. 8, 19–28). AI is the central trend. In both rounds of trend analysis, experts ranked all AI-related trends as highly impactful and highly uncertain (pp. 8–9). Scenario A is built on evolution path 1: "AI has a positive impact on job creation, with most workers benefiting from it" (pp. 26–27, 88). Annex 4 compares the scenarios on AI uptake, benefits from AI, ability to cope with change, employment forms, labour relations, AI impact on jobs, skills most needed, inequality, learning landscape and sustainability (pp. 95–96).
- **Geography:** European Union / Europe (pp. 9, 29).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory planning scenario that feeds a stakeholder vision-building process. "These scenarios are not predictive but rather exploratory, aiming to identify areas that require attention, threats that need to be addressed and opportunities that should be leveraged" (p. 9). This is the "very positive outlook, where AI enhances human capabilities and social good" (p. 9) and "the most positive scenario" (p. 35).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated for the scenario as a whole. The survey's stated purpose was "not to see which scenario is more plausible" but to consolidate assumptions within each scenario (p. 98). Respondents supported the scenario's general direction but were "cautious regarding the magnitude of the likely change" (p. 35). For example, they judged a fully integrated learning system by 2040 "highly unlikely" (p. 32) and said further tightening of labour markets "may not happen" (p. 37).

### Timeline

- Horizon: 2040 (p. 9). The survey framed it as "the next 15-20 years" (p. 98).
- No dated events within the scenario narrative.
- Study timeline: trend identification Sept–Oct 2023; trend analysis Oct–Nov 2023 (webinar 9 Oct 2023; Brussels stakeholder meeting 8 Nov 2023); morphological analysis Dec 2023–Mar 2024; scenario outlines Apr 2024; expert workshop May 2024; semi-Delphi survey Oct–Nov 2024; scenario exploration Dec 2024–Jan 2025; vision building Feb–Dec 2025 (p. 18).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** On. Inside the scenario, sustainability becomes "the core of most policy areas", inclusiveness is a key factor in state, trade union and employer decisions, and social dialogue is strengthened through tripartite cooperation (pp. 14, 29, 89–90).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:**
  - Sustainability actions become the core of most policy areas, and sustainability is built into curricula and skills development (pp. 29–30, 69).
  - Inclusiveness in education and training rises substantially as a policy priority, because job creation and a shrinking labour force make untapped vulnerable and disadvantaged groups important. Per evolution path 1, inclusiveness is "a key factor in state, trade union and employer decisions (with most employers actively contributing)" (pp. 30, 90).
  - Employers, learning providers and policymakers cooperate more to improve skills utilisation as shortages grow (p. 31).
  - Learning and career professionals need substantial changes in management, organisation and skills development because AI disrupts delivery (p. 30).
  - Trade unions, employers' organisations and governments work together on technological change. Unions extend support to workers outside traditional contracts (p. 14).
  - Micro-credentials become increasingly recognised, and the education and training system becomes more flexible, accessible and responsive (p. 70).
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - For this scenario, people with limited digital literacy or poor digital infrastructure "may need more support" so that they also benefit (p. 70). The scenario's optimistic outlook on inequality holds "only if the digital divide is managed and opportunities are inclusive" (p. 13). Scenario A shows AI's potential "provided inclusivity and sustainability are prioritised" (p. 79).
  - *Applies across the set:* Stakeholders should co-create a vision that prioritises "lifelong and life-wide learning and continuing skills development, individual empowerment, equitable access to opportunities and robust governance of AI technologies" (p. 79). Integrated learning systems should leverage institutional, self-directed and workplace contexts (p. 79). Workplaces should be treated as lifelong learning spaces (pp. 7, 17). Social dialogue should be willing to "experiment with new forms of worker representation and social protection", prioritise lifelong learning and make sure all workers have a voice (p. 14). Technological advancement should be balanced with social equity and environmental responsibility (p. 79).

## Key dynamics explored

- A shrinking labour force (ageing, slowing migration) combined with AI-driven job creation widens the gap between skills supply and demand. Skills shortages and a very tight labour market follow (pp. 29, 69).
- AI automates repetitive and low-skilled tasks while creating demand for higher-level skills and jobs. This pushes upskilling and reskilling of an "ever-shrinking workforce" and raises the value of human-centric skills, which let workers complement AI (pp. 29–30, 69).
- Scarce skills push employers to pay more attention to loyalty, attracting and retaining talent, and workplace skills development. International competition for talent intensifies (p. 29).
- Skills gaps lead employers to turn to "previously untapped talent sources, such as disadvantaged groups", which requires greater inclusiveness. Survey respondents judged this link real but "not ... very strong" (pp. 29, 31).
- Abundant jobs give workers bargaining power and choice. Voluntary job changes rise, more people invest in purpose-driven careers, and employees' loyalty to employers "inevitably erodes" (pp. 30, 69). The ability to self-direct a career still depends on qualifications, skills and mindset, so only some workers make use of it (pp. 31–32, 36).
- Business disruption is moderate relative to the extent of transformation, so changes in tasks and skills are manageable and most companies adapt (pp. 29–30).
- AI-driven, digitally delivered learning makes the skills system more open. Boundaries between delivery modes and between formal and non-formal learning erode, and micro-credentials gain value (p. 70).
- *Applies across the set:* the "individual responsibility gap" is less pronounced in Scenarios A and C, where individuals are "better equipped to cope with changes in the job market" (pp. 12–13).

## State of technology

- AI and automation increasingly handle repetitive and low-skilled tasks (pp. 29, 69). The survey framed the scenario with AI capabilities that "expand and become more pervasive across industries" (p. 103). 28% thought AI would advance quickly enough to replace human-centric skills too, which the authors treat as a minority view (p. 34).
- In learning, AI creates tailored, adaptive learning environments and immersive augmented and virtual reality simulations for safe experiential learning (p. 70). AI-driven learning assistants use "advanced mapping techniques" to identify learners' skills and potential and to give personalised recommendations (p. 71).
- AI tools are built into basic and advanced guidance and counselling, "usually still under the supervision of qualified professionals" (p. 71).
- New technologies make sustainable practices easier and cheaper to implement (35% of respondents) and support new, innovative sustainability solutions (pp. 33, 69).
- Market structure, providers, compute, energy and pricing: Not addressed.

## State of adoption

- AI uptake is "Extensive" (p. 95). Its benefits "cut across all types and sizes of organisations, creating positive disruptions in all economic sectors" (p. 29). Benefits are "widely available; opportunities for most individuals/companies" (p. 95).
- Disruption is moderate in nature but significant in scale. Existing jobs are rarely destroyed, and most companies adapt reasonably well (p. 29). Evolution path 1: "Disruption is moderate and affects some industries and business models (medium difficulty to manage)" (p. 89).
- AI affects all segments of education and training. Use of AI in education and training "increases rapidly across all types of learning environments" (pp. 30, 92).
- Barriers mentioned: limited digital literacy and inadequate digital infrastructure in some areas (p. 70). Respondents noted regional and company-size disparities in adopting environmentally and socially responsible practices (p. 33). Established institutions and accreditation systems resist integration (17% of respondents) (p. 32).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

"The economy is booming and creating new jobs, fuelled by technological innovations and the impact of AI. Business activities thrive" (p. 69). Workers using human-centric skills to complement AI drive "innovation and growth in their organisations" (p. 69). The executive summary describes AI creating "new opportunities for economic growth, social mobility and environmental sustainability" (p. 11). Respondents cited longevity and productivity as factors that would ease shortages (p. 37). No quantitative growth figures.

### Labour market dynamics

- The labour force shrinks while jobs grow, producing a "very tight" labour market, deepening recruitment problems and growing skills shortages (pp. 9, 69). 85% of survey respondents agreed labour markets would be even tighter in 2040 (36% strongly, 47% somewhat). Even so, the consolidated conclusion is that "further tightening of EU labour markets may not happen". Automation (cited by 50%), longer working lives and healthcare (44%) and immigration (one third) could mitigate it, leaving "a relatively stable status quo" (pp. 34–35, 37).
- AI is "transformation, not replacement: opportunities in higher-value roles" (p. 95). Existing jobs are "rarely destroyed" (p. 29). The automation of repetitive and low-skilled tasks "free[s] up workers to pursue more fulfilling careers" (p. 69).
- Voluntary job changes increase as people pursue better opportunities (pp. 30, 69, 90). More workers use flexible work arrangements and provide services to several employers, even across countries (pp. 69–70). Flexibility and mobility are "preferred by individuals" (p. 95).
- Employers prioritise talent retention. 84% of respondents agreed (28% strongly, 56% somewhat), but 57% said retention depends mainly on company culture and turnover. Over two fifths said lower-value-added or routine sectors may not feel the pressure. Retention strategies therefore expand "moderately", mainly in higher value-added sectors with specialised skills (p. 34).
- Employers turn to disadvantaged groups to fill shortages (p. 29). 82% of respondents agreed skills shortages are positively linked to inclusive hiring, but 58% said employers will prioritise alternatives such as automation or outsourcing. The scenario therefore assumes a "more moderate expansion of inclusiveness and diversity" (pp. 31, 35–36).
- Skills: human-centric skills (social and emotional skills, creativity, innovation, complex problem-solving) "take centre stage" (pp. 69, 94–95). 88% of respondents agreed they remain indispensable (43% strongly, the highest "strongly agree" share in the survey). 45% said AI uptake creates jobs and raises demand for all types of skills (p. 33). Sustainability becomes increasingly important in curricula (p. 30).
- Wages, hours, labour share and unemployment rates: Not addressed.

### Winners and losers

- "Most workers and organisations benefit from AI advancements" (p. 9). Benefits are widely available to most individuals and companies (p. 95).
- Workers with higher qualifications, skills and the right personal attributes are best placed to self-direct their careers. 82% of respondents said this capacity depends on qualifications and skills, and 47% said personal attributes matter more than job availability (pp. 31–32).
- Higher value-added sectors with specialised skills invest more in their workforce. Lower value-added or routine sectors may not (p. 34).
- Disadvantaged groups gain from employers' search for talent and from personalised digital learning, which "greatly helps individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds" (pp. 29, 71). People with limited digital literacy or poor digital infrastructure risk missing out without support (p. 70).
- Inequality "Decreases; scale depends on managing the digital divide" (p. 95). It is "a thriving economy and declining inequalities, but only if the digital divide is managed and opportunities are inclusive" (p. 13).

### Household incomes and living standards

AI-driven opportunities lead to "improved quality of life for individuals, economies and societies" (p. 11). Workers seek "higher satisfaction and more meaningful work" (p. 69). The growing reliance on digital delivery raises concerns about excessive screen time and well-being (p. 70). Incomes and wages: Not addressed.

### Government finances

Not addressed.

### International economic patterns

International competition for talent intensifies and affects most sectors (pp. 29, 89). Workers provide services across different countries (p. 70). Irregular migration flows do not increase (p. 88), and slowing migration contributes to the shrinking labour force (p. 69). One third of respondents said the EU can address its declining native workforce through immigration, and "targeted migration" is among the mitigating solutions (pp. 35, 37). The survey's A1 options suggested outsourcing may no longer be an option amid a global skills shortage (p. 100).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Skills and talent become scarce (pp. 29, 69). Learning content becomes abundant, and people "may struggle to identify the most relevant and effective content" amid "the overwhelming abundance of options" (p. 70). Prices of goods, essentials and assets: Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Automation creates "opportunities for those in the occupations affected to upskill and reskill, transitioning into higher-value roles" (p. 69). The executive summary describes "a virtuous cycle of upskilling and reskilling, where workers can transition to higher-value roles and employers can benefit from a more skilled and engaged workforce" (p. 11). The ability to shape a career still "hinges on their individual qualifications, skills and mindset, regardless of favourable labour market conditions" (p. 36).

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

Social dialogue is strengthened, and trade unions expand support to workers outside traditional employment contracts, "ensuring that all workers have access to representation and protection" (p. 14). Labour relations are "Peaceful; high employment and skills development reduce tensions" (p. 95). Specific social protection or income support arrangements: Not addressed.

### Time for what matters

More people pursue purpose-driven careers, which the survey defined as aligning work with personal values and contributing to society in areas such as social justice, sustainability, education or health (pp. 69, 99). Only 30% of respondents thought skills shortages would empower most workers to put more emphasis on happiness, personal growth and meaningful work (p. 32). Work–life balance features in the purpose-driven careers trend (p. 20). Concerns about screen time and well-being in digital learning (p. 70).

### A say in your future

Social dialogue is "strengthened through collaborative efforts between trade unions, employers' organisations and governments" (p. 14). Labour relations are peaceful (p. 95). Democratic processes: Not addressed.

### A fair share of progress

Benefits from AI are "widely available; opportunities for most individuals/companies" (p. 95). AI is "leveraged to create new opportunities for economic growth, social mobility and environmental sustainability" (p. 11). Inequality declines, conditional on managing the digital divide (pp. 13, 95).

## Other dynamics

- **Environment:** Technology "does not solve the climate change crisis". The human impact on the environment is recognised by most, sustainability is at the core of most policy areas, eco-consumer values matter to most people, and sustainability practices become standard in most organisations and sectors (pp. 29–30, 69). 79% of respondents agreed this is plausible (25% strongly, 54% somewhat). 42% said businesses and governments will still prioritise short-term gains, and respondents noted that political and security challenges are pushing back the green agenda (p. 33). The conclusion stays "cautiously optimistic" (p. 33).
- **Education and training system:** Teachers' and trainers' roles are transformed. They scale up support but must master new technologies (p. 70). Many traditional and nontraditional stakeholders, including AI creators, produce learning content (pp. 30, 70). 68% of respondents agreed boundaries between formal and non-formal learning would greatly diminish (27% strongly, 41% somewhat), but 65% expected formal systems to adapt rather than disappear. The report concludes full integration "will remain an aspirational goal" (pp. 32, 36).
- **Guidance and counselling:** AI empowers guidance professionals to focus on higher-value tasks. With learners facing "an overwhelming array of choices", human guidance professionals become "more vital than ever" (p. 71).
- **Health and well-being:** Excessive screen time and the negative effects of VR/AR on well-being are flagged (p. 70).
- Safety, loss of control, geopolitics and information ecosystem: Not addressed for this scenario (security concerns noted only as pushing back the green agenda, p. 33).
