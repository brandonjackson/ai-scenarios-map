# The 2028 Global Intelligence Crisis — extract

## Summary

Agentic coding makes it cheap to build or replace software, while purchasing agents drive down the rents of businesses that depend on human search costs, inertia and intermediation. Firms maintain output with fewer workers and put labour savings into further automation. Falling high-income households' spending pulls the economy into recession even as AI productivity and investment remain strong. Private-credit losses spread to insurers, and the sudden impairment of white-collar incomes calls the prime mortgage market into question while tax receipts fall and the government debates relief. (“The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”; “The Daisy Chain of Correlated Bets”; “The Battle Against Time”)

## Key details

- **Source:** *The 2028 Global Intelligence Crisis: A Thought Exercise in Financial History, from the Future*, Citrini, Citrini Research, 22 February 2026. `sources/citrini-intelligence-crisis-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** [Companion pieces by Alap Shah](https://alapshah1.substack.com/p/the-global-intelligence-crisis), credited in the Preface as separately written parts of the same exercise.
- **Reading notes:** Full Citrini memo, including the Preface. Shah proposed the initial question and brainstormed the scenario with Citrini, as the Preface explains.
- **Scenario set:** Standalone.
- **Geography:** United States, with examples of divergent effects in Taiwan, South Korea and India. (“From Sector Risk to Systemic Risk”)
- **Authors' framing:** A financial-history thought exercise presented as a 30 June 2028 macro memo about an overlooked left-tail risk. (Preface; “Macro Memo”)
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability assigned. (Preface)

### Timeline

- Late 2025 — a jump in agentic coding makes it plausible to replace the core functionality of midsize software products. (“How It Started”)
- 2026 — white-collar layoffs first raise profits; SaaS renewals come under pressure; Q3 ServiceNow results expose the seat-based revenue problem. Job openings are below 5.5 million in October, when the S&P 500 approaches 8,000. Agents already undercut travel booking by Q4. (“The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”; “How It Started”; “When Friction Went to Zero”; “From Sector Risk to Systemic Risk”)
- 2027 — AI agents become routine. Weak card volume growth puts payments disruption in the open in April. The US is in recession by Q2. Private-credit software defaults mount in Q3, and a November market crash exposes how connected the risks are. (“When Friction Went to Zero”; “The Intelligence Displacement Spiral”; “The Daisy Chain of Correlated Bets”)
- June 2028 — unemployment reaches 10.2%; the S&P 500 is down 38% from its October 2026 high. Prime borrowers in technology-heavy areas are showing stress. A wider mortgage crisis remains a risk for the second half of 2028. (“The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”; “The Daisy Chain of Correlated Bets”)

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. Existing stabilizers pay out and the Fed acts, but structural relief remains under debate at the memo's horizon. (“The Battle Against Time”)
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The Fed cuts rates and considers its liquidity toolkit while Washington considers transfers financed by a compute tax and a public stake in AI infrastructure. Partisan arguments over transfers, innovation and distribution delay policy. (“The Daisy Chain of Correlated Bets”; “The Battle Against Time”)
- **Policies the source recommends:** Draws attention to the urgency of replacing a wage-based tax base and designing a timely response. The memo leaves the legislative proposals unresolved. (“The Battle Against Time”; “The Intelligence Premium Unwind”)

## Key dynamics explored

- A company can either build software in-house or credibly threaten to do so. In both cases incumbent vendors lose pricing power. Seat-based vendors lose more revenue when their customers reduce staff. (“How It Started”)
- Threatened incumbents cut their own payroll, buy AI with the savings and protect margins, accelerating the same substitution that damaged their business. Falling consumption later creates another margin incentive to automate. (“How It Started”; “The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”)
- Agents can search continuously and switch providers cheaply, reducing the value of brand loyalty, passive renewals, information asymmetry, booking platforms and card interchange. (“When Friction Went to Zero”)
- Job losses in high-income households reduce spending disproportionately. The next round of displacement reaches service workers through lower demand and more applicants. (“From Sector Risk to Systemic Risk”; “The Intelligence Displacement Spiral”)
- Private loans assumed recurring software revenue. Losses on those loans propagate through arrangements tying asset managers to insurers and can force sales as regulatory capital demands rise. Prime mortgage risk emerges separately through structurally lower professional incomes. (“The Daisy Chain of Correlated Bets”)

## State of technology

Agentic coding allows a competent developer to reproduce the core of a mid-market SaaS product within weeks. Persistent consumer agents monitor prices and renewals, aggregate data, transact on users' behalf, and often route payments over stablecoin networks. The scenario describes abundant cheap cognitive labour and a continuing need for GPUs, electricity and data centre capacity. (“How It Started”; “When Friction Went to Zero”; “From Sector Risk to Systemic Risk”)

## State of adoption

Enterprise procurement starts repricing contracts at 2026 midyear reviews, when budget owners have evidence from internal pilots that was unavailable at the prior budget cycle. Firms adopt AI even during the downturn because it substitutes for operating expense. By early 2027, consumer use is often invisible through phones and integrated services. Travel, insurance, property, delivery and payment firms face successive erosion, alongside software, consulting and finance. (“How It Started”; “When Friction Went to Zero”; “From Sector Risk to Systemic Risk”)

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Early nominal GDP prints run at mid- to high-single-digit annualized rates, and output per hour grows at rates absent since the 1950s. A smaller share of that output returns to the consumer economy, which initially accounts for 70% of GDP. The memo calls the disconnect “Ghost GDP” and dates the real-GDP recession to Q2 2027, after two negative quarters. (“The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”; “The Intelligence Displacement Spiral”)

### Labour market dynamics

Professional layoffs expand from software into finance, consulting, legal, marketing and other services. Firms also stop filling roles before headline unemployment rises sharply. White-collar workers made up half of employment and roughly three-quarters of discretionary spending. A displaced example goes from a $180,000 product-manager job to $45,000 in gig work. Workers crowd into service jobs and bid down wages, while those still employed work harder and save more. Initial claims reach 487,000 in Q3 2027 and unemployment reaches 10.2% by June 2028. Labour's share of GDP drops from 56% in 2024 to 46% four years later. (“From Sector Risk to Systemic Risk”; “The Intelligence Displacement Spiral”; “The Battle Against Time”; “The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”)

### Winners and losers

Compute owners accumulate wealth. Semiconductor, hyperscaler and power infrastructure businesses initially prosper while software, professional services, merchants built on intermediation, card issuers and labour lose income. Gig delivery platforms face new competition from services remitting more of each delivery fee to drivers. Higher-income households are exposed early, and wage pressure reaches lower-paid workers as former white-collar employees compete for their jobs. (“The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”; “From Sector Risk to Systemic Risk”; “When Friction Went to Zero”; “The Intelligence Displacement Spiral”)

### Household incomes and living standards

Falling real wages and insecure work drive precautionary saving, especially among still-employed professionals. High-income households account for a disproportionate share of consumer spending, so the aggregate impact exceeds the number of lost jobs. Borrowers initially maintain mortgage payments by drawing credit or retirement savings. Some households face doubled debt-to-income ratios as bonuses and salaries fall. (“The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”; “The Intelligence Displacement Spiral”; “The Daisy Chain of Correlated Bets”)

### Government finances

Federal receipts run 12% below the CBO baseline through Q1 2028 as both income- and payroll-tax receipts fall. Outlays rise as the displaced claim benefits, while a greater portion of output goes to capital and compute and generates less tax revenue per unit. States dependent on income taxes come under bond-market pressure. Washington considers a compute-funded transition and public participation in AI infrastructure. (“The Battle Against Time”)

### International economic patterns

Taiwan and South Korea benefit from strong demand for AI supply-chain inputs. India loses the cost advantage supporting more than $200 billion in annual IT services exports, a key offset to its goods trade deficit. The currency falls and the IMF is approached as outsourcing contracts erode. (“From Sector Risk to Systemic Risk”)

### Market pricing and scarcity

The memo reports software vendors losing the ability to raise renewal prices, and agents undermining the 15–20% premium associated with passive insurance renewals, the 5–6% residential real estate commission, and 2–3% card interchange. It sees cheap intelligence relative to still-scarce compute and energy. San Francisco, Seattle and Austin housing values fall 11%, 9% and 8% year on year by June 2028. (“How It Started”; “When Friction Went to Zero”; “The Daisy Chain of Correlated Bets”; “The Intelligence Premium Unwind”)

### Financial markets and stability

The 10-year Treasury yield falls from 4.3% to 3.2% over four months as bonds price weaker consumption. Private credit grows beyond $2.5 trillion by 2026, with exposure to software buyouts valued on durable subscriptions. Marks lag falling public comparables. Moody's downgrades $18 billion across 14 software issuers in April 2027. Zendesk misses covenants and its $5 billion direct-lending facility is marked at 58 cents. Long-duration locked-up funding initially limits forced selling, but affiliated life insurers hold the loans against annuity obligations, offshore arrangements complicate visibility, and higher regulatory capital requirements change the incentives to sell. Prime mortgage delinquencies are elevated in technology and finance centres. The memo distinguishes the already-realized 38% equity drawdown from the roughly 57% potential scenario if wider mortgage losses materialize. (“From Sector Risk to Systemic Risk”; “The Daisy Chain of Correlated Bets”)

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Previously secure professionals face diminished pay and promotion prospects. Some work more intensively with AI to remain employed. Well-qualified displaced workers move into lower-paid roles and add to competition for those jobs. (“The Intelligence Displacement Spiral”)

### Affordable essentials

Agents reduce certain prices and fees, including insurance and property commissions, while loss of employment makes it harder for households to meet existing mortgage payments. (“When Friction Went to Zero”; “The Daisy Chain of Correlated Bets”)

### Reliable safety net

Automatic stabilizers assume claimants return to work after temporary job loss. Structural displacement keeps outlays high while the receipts that finance benefits fall. The proposals under discussion have yet to close the gap. (“The Battle Against Time”)

### Time for what matters

The memo describes employed professionals working harder to keep their jobs and previously salaried workers taking gig work at much lower pay. (“The Intelligence Displacement Spiral”)

### A say in your future

Government debates a transition package but disagreements over redistribution, taxation and competition slow a response. The memo describes declining faith in government rescue and “Occupy Silicon Valley” protests over the distribution of AI gains. (“The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”; “The Battle Against Time”)

### A fair share of progress

AI-driven gains initially flow to compute and capital owners despite booming measured output per hour. The proposed public stake and transfers are the political response to that distribution. (“The Consequences of Abundant Intelligence”; “The Battle Against Time”)

## Other dynamics

The memo argues the value of scarce human intelligence underlay labour contracts, asset prices and tax arrangements, and the speed of the repricing leaves institutions little time to adjust. (“The Intelligence Premium Unwind”)
