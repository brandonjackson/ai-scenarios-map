# The 2028 Global Intelligence Crisis — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Off, then too late.** Existing stabilisers pay out and the Fed cuts rates, but no new policy shapes the crisis. By 2028 Washington is debating transfers funded by a compute tax and a public stake in AI infrastructure, and partisan fights hold them up. The memo's point is the lag: institutions built around scarce human intelligence cannot reprice as fast as the technology does. The authors draw attention to replacing a wage-based tax base but leave the proposals unresolved, so they do not count as policy in the scenario.

## Big assumptions
1. **Capability.** Agentic coding lets a competent developer rebuild the core of a mid-market SaaS product in weeks by late 2025, and persistent consumer agents shop, switch and pay on users' behalf.
2. **Adoption.** Firms move spending from payroll to AI within shrinking budgets and keep doing so in the downturn, because AI substitutes for operating expense. Threatened incumbents are among the fastest adopters.
3. **Markets.** High-income households drive a disproportionate share of spending, so job losses there cut demand by more than their number. Private credit (over $2.5 trillion), insurers' balance sheets and $13 trillion of mortgages all assume white-collar incomes hold.
4. **Politics.** Government sees the problem but argues about villains; no transition package passes within the horizon.

## What must be true for this to occur
- Agents reach enterprise-grade reliability across software, professional services and intermediation within about 18 months.
- Payroll savings are reinvested in automation rather than in new hiring, and displaced workers find no comparable new roles.
- Cheaper services from removed friction do not offset lost incomes.
- Hidden links between private credit, asset managers and life insurers amplify losses instead of absorbing them.
- Fiscal policy does not move before the crisis, despite clear warnings.

## Tensions and weak points
- The scenario counts lower fees and commissions as destroyed corporate value; for consumers, especially those still employed, they are a real gain the memo does not net off.
- The first hit falls on high earners. Under the worst-off rule the group that matters is lower down: service and gig workers whose wages are bid down as displaced professionals compete for their jobs.
- Strongly US-centred. India's loss of its IT-services export model, a falling currency and a call to the IMF show how AI can break labour-cost development paths; Taiwan and South Korea gain.
- The memo is precise about markets and vague about the state: it gives a revenue shortfall but no debt path or benefit numbers.
- Map position (−0.32, 0.58; −0.70, −0.55) sits with `tcb-displacement`, `wef-displacement` and `korinek-agi` on the labour plot and with `toloui-titans` and `wef-displacement` on the fiscal plot. That fits: it is the fastest and most financially detailed of these displacement stories, and less concentrated on the labour axis than `toloui-titans` because agents also pass some gains to consumers.

## Scoring notes
Scores are 1/2/1/2/2, all stated. Work is 1: white-collar displacement on this scale, a ten-point fall in the labour share and wage pressure reaching service work leave no route from effort to a better life for the worst-off. Essentials is 2 rather than 1 because some prices genuinely fall, but households with mortgages and debts lose the incomes to meet them. Safety net is 1: stabilisers designed for temporary job loss face structural displacement just as receipts fall. Government is 2: it is trying, but deadlocked. Time is 2: insecurity and longer hours, not free time. The weakest-link rule caps the headline at 2.
