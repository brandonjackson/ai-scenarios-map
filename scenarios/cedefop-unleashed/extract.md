# Scenario D: AI unleashed – dominating the world of work and societies — extract

## Summary

It is 2040. AI and automation have "taken over in all areas of life and work", transforming the fabric of society. Job losses are "unprecedented" across all sectors, industries and skill levels (pp. 10, 76). A few major players own and control AI, dominate the global economy and exert growing influence over policymakers. No regulation safeguards citizens' rights, democracy is undermined, and extremist and bottom-up anti-technology movements emerge (pp. 57, 76). Most human jobs are replaced. The remaining workers do non-standard work on tasks AI cannot yet do, such as domestic service or training machines. Companies invest only in the AI skills of a small elite, and individuals bear full responsibility for their own skills development while unions lose their power (pp. 76–77). Large parts of the population are pushed out of the labour market into unemployment, poverty and social exclusion. Environmental policy loses ground, and AI drives geographical polarisation and brain drain (pp. 57–58, 76–77).

## Key details

- **Source:** Preparing for 2040: four AI-powered scenarios for the future of continuing skills development, Cedefop (European Centre for the Development of Vocational Training), Publications Office of the European Union, 2025-11. `sources/cedefop-preparing-2040-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None. The same report restates the scenario in its executive summary (pp. 10–11). Results from the later scenario-exploration and vision-building phases are due to be published in 2026 (pp. 8, 18).
- **Reading notes:** Full text read: executive summary, Chapters 1–2 (method), Chapter 6 (Scenario D assumptions and semi-Delphi results), Chapter 7.4 (consolidated scenario), Chapter 8, Annexes 1, 3 and 4, and the Scenario D section of the Annex 5 questionnaire. Figure 1 (impact–uncertainty matrix) and Figure 2 (respondents) are images that are not in the text extraction. Locators are the report's printed page numbers. The consolidated scenario (Ch. 7.4) differs from the initial assumptions (Ch. 6.1) on education. After the survey, the claim that AI fully replaces teachers and formal education was dropped. Both versions are recorded below.
- **Scenario set:** One of four Cedefop scenarios for continuing skills development by 2040. The others are A "a future of opportunities – technology-driven competition for talent", B "left alone to ride the tide – navigating the AI shock waves on jobs" and C "staying afloat – AI opportunities missed" (pp. 9–10). The set is not built on a 2×2 grid. It comes from a five-step process: (1) identifying 21 trends with the STEEPV framework, (2) impact–uncertainty analysis, (3) morphological analysis of possible evolutions for each trend, (4) four scenario outlines built on four "evolution paths", and (5) validation in a semi-Delphi survey with 132 respondents (pp. 8, 19–28). AI is the central trend. In both rounds of trend analysis, experts ranked all AI-related trends as highly impactful and highly uncertain (pp. 8–9). Scenario D is built on evolution path 4: "AI disrupts economies and societies, replaces almost all jobs and serves only the few controlling it / a few powerful elites; social inequalities grow, and people are left alone/unsupported" (pp. 27, 88). Annex 4 compares the scenarios on AI uptake, benefits from AI, ability to cope with change, employment forms, labour relations, AI impact on jobs, skills most needed, inequality, learning landscape and sustainability (pp. 95–96).
- **Geography:** European Union / Europe, with global framing ("dominant force in the global economy"; "threatening the stability of societies worldwide") and cross-country migration effects (pp. 57, 76–77).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory planning scenario, presented as the dystopian end of the set. "These scenarios are not predictive but rather exploratory..." (p. 9). It is the "dystopian future, where AI is deployed primarily for control and profit maximisation and where most jobs are lost, with those that remain often precarious, low-paying and lacking in benefits" (p. 9). The scenario shows how "unchecked AI advancement can lead to a concentration of power and wealth, exacerbating social and economic inequalities and undermining democratic institutions" (p. 11).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated for the scenario as a whole. The survey's stated purpose was "not to see which scenario is more plausible" (p. 98). Respondents agreed with the democratic-erosion assumption (63%) and the human-centric skills assumption (72%). They strongly rejected the assumptions that formal education disappears and that AI replaces teachers (pp. 60–64). Even respondents who did not foresee harm to democracy "tend to acknowledge potential negative consequences for jobs and worker protection" (p. 65).

### Timeline

- Horizon: 2040 (p. 10). Human-centric skills are hardest for AI to replace "in a short time span (15 years)" (p. 57). The survey framed it as "the next 15-20 years" (p. 98).
- No dated events within the scenario narrative.
- Study timeline: trend identification Sept–Oct 2023; trend analysis Oct–Nov 2023; morphological analysis Dec 2023–Mar 2024; scenario outlines Apr 2024; expert workshop May 2024; semi-Delphi survey Oct–Nov 2024; scenario exploration Dec 2024–Jan 2025; vision building Feb–Dec 2025 (p. 18).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. Policymakers are "caught unprepared or unwilling" to address AI's legal and ethical impacts, no regulation safeguarding citizens' rights is implemented, and public institutions come under the dominance of those who control AI (pp. 57, 60, 76).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:**
  - "No regulation safeguarding citizens' rights in light of AI developments is implemented" (pp. 57, 76).
  - AI owners progressively influence policymakers, who are "unable (or unwilling due to their dependence on AI and other technologies) to respond". This leads to "the rise of authoritarian powers at the service of (or controlled by) the few" (p. 57). Those controlling AI "exert their full dominance over public institutions and decision-makers, who rely on these technologies to run all aspects of societies and economies" (p. 60).
  - Policymakers cannot offer retraining that keeps up with AI-driven change "without the support of those owning and controlling these technologies" (pp. 57, 77).
  - Inclusiveness and equal access "are not a priority for any stakeholder" (p. 59). Evolution path 4 in Annex 3 lists both "a key factor in state, trade union and employer decisions" and "not a priority for any of the stakeholders" (p. 90). The scenario text takes the latter (p. 59).
  - Policymakers have "less leverage for (and interest in)" sustainability and greening policies, which may hinder competitiveness and growth (pp. 58, 76).
  - "Some policymakers view the replacement of human jobs by AI/machines as a counterbalancing factor to the rising demographic challenges" (p. 76).
  - Access to employment and skills development relies on fully automated processes with "no checks and limitations" against bias, discrimination, data misuse or privacy breaches (p. 77).
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - For this scenario: "findings stress the importance of developing policies that address the potential impacts of AI on job displacement and worker protection and ensure that the benefits of AI are shared equitably, and its negative consequences are mitigated" (p. 67). Oversight is needed "to ensure that the increasing role of nontraditional stakeholders in content generation does not worsen existing inequalities". Policymakers and education, training and guidance institutions should develop "a more nuanced and balanced approach to the integration of technology into education" that prioritises the well-being of students and practitioners (pp. 67–68). Respondents stressed that Member States and policymakers "have a responsibility to mitigate the effects of AI on citizens and democracy", which requires addressing the growing power of big tech and "a more nuanced and context-specific approach to AI governance" (p. 61).
  - *Applies across the set:* Stakeholders should co-create a vision that prioritises "lifelong and life-wide learning and continuing skills development, individual empowerment, equitable access to opportunities and robust governance of AI technologies" (p. 79). Integrated learning systems should leverage institutional, self-directed and workplace contexts (p. 79). Social dialogue should be willing to "experiment with new forms of worker representation and social protection", prioritise lifelong learning and make sure all workers have a voice (p. 14). Technological advancement should be balanced with social equity and environmental responsibility (p. 79).

## Key dynamics explored

- A few individuals, companies or organisations own and control AI and use it "individualistically to pursue growth, profit and wealth". Their market power becomes political influence, and policymakers grow dependent and captured. The result is authoritarian drift and the erosion of democracy and social cohesion (pp. 57, 60, 76).
- Policymakers cannot keep pace with private-sector AI, so no protective regulation exists. Citizens are disempowered, and polarised and extremist movements grow. Algorithmic manipulation creates "filter bubbles" and erodes trust (pp. 60, 76).
- AI replaces most human jobs, which shrinks labour demand and weakens workers' position. Large segments of the population are left behind and vulnerable (p. 59).
- Jobs are seen as replaceable, so companies have "little incentive to offer retraining opportunities for tasks/jobs that can be taken over by machines". Workplace skills development "drop[s] sharply", and responsibility shifts entirely to individuals (pp. 57–59, 77).
- Tasks relying on technical skills are fully automated because AI has not yet mastered human-centric skills. Human-centric tasks become more sought after (p. 77), yet workers have fewer chances to use and develop those skills at work (p. 77).
- Traditional, unionised employment declines and task-based contracts become pervasive. Trade unions lose power and can no longer safeguard workers (pp. 66, 77).
- Small and medium-sized enterprises lack the resources to keep up and "may therefore be pushed out of the labour market, resulting in further job losses and rising inequalities" (p. 66).
- The AI-fuelled race for growth crowds out environmental policy. Individuals "prioritise their own survival over environmental concerns" (pp. 58, 76).
- People migrate towards places where limited opportunities remain. Low-skilled workers concentrate in less AI-dominated economies and high-skilled workers in AI-advanced ones, causing brain drain and social tensions (pp. 58, 76–77).
- *Applies across the set:* the "individual responsibility gap" is "particularly pronounced" in Scenarios B and D, "as workers are left to fend for themselves" (p. 12).

## State of technology

- "AI, technological advancements and automation increase in all areas of life and work" (p. 57). AI "increasingly inserts itself into all human activities" with "fast-moving developments in generative AI technologies" (p. 57). AI technologies "dominate all aspects of work and life" (p. 60).
- Capabilities: AI replaces most human jobs at all skill levels. Tasks relying on technical skills are "fully automated". "AI technologies have not yet perfected AI-generated versions of human-centric skills" (pp. 59, 77). Remaining human tasks include personal services, such as domestic staff in private households, and training machines (p. 77). 21% of respondents envisaged AI developing human-centric skills too (pp. 61, 67).
- Market structure: "A few major players own and control AI technologies and have emerged as the dominant force in the global economy" (p. 76). They "harness the power of AI to drive growth and expand their market shares" (p. 76).
- Energy and resources: companies adopt rapid AI upgrades "despite high levels of energy consumption and waste generation" (pp. 58, 76). Few invest in AI-based sustainability solutions (p. 76).
- Automated decision systems: access to employment and continuing skills development relies on "fully automated processes" without checks against bias (p. 77).
- In learning: AI use grows rapidly across all learning environments, with VR/AR tools. AI powers guidance tools "to fully AI-powered practitioners" (p. 78). Initial assumption: AI tutors "are able to fully replace teachers and trainers", and AI offers advanced career guidance autonomously (pp. 59, 93).
- Open versus closed models, compute constraints and pricing: Not addressed.

## State of adoption

- AI uptake is "Extensive" (p. 95). AI has "taken over in all areas of life and work" (p. 76).
- Companies "prioritise the adoption of swift AI/technological advancements and upgrades" to stay competitive and profitable, at the expense of sustainability and social inclusion (pp. 58, 76).
- Public institutions and decision-makers "rely on these technologies to run all aspects of societies and economies" (p. 60).
- Disruption is "fast-paced and happens across many industries and business models (hard to manage)" (p. 89). Jobs and tasks change "at a scale and speed that cannot be effectively addressed" (p. 58).
- Uneven adoption: SMEs lack the resources to keep up (p. 66). Countries differ in how pervasive AI is, with "countries with less AI-dominated economies or those in the early stages of AI adoption" (pp. 64–65, 76–77).
- Resistance: bottom-up anti-technology movements arise (p. 76). 37% of respondents expected the secondary effects of AI tools in education (isolation, anxiety, health issues) to spark grassroots opposition (p. 64).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

AI and technology "fuel the race for economic growth and profit" in a system "controlled by the few" (p. 58). Dominant AI owners drive growth and expand market share (p. 76). The transformation has "significant implications for the economy, the environment and human well-being" (p. 76). No quantitative growth figures.

### Labour market dynamics

- "Unprecedented job losses across all sectors, industries and skills levels" (p. 76). "Most human jobs are destroyed and replaced by AI" (p. 59). Annex 4: "Massive job losses across the board; extensive replacement" (p. 95). Per evolution path 4, AI "predominantly brings job destruction" (p. 88).
- "Large segments of the population are completely pushed out of the labour market and increasingly experience unemployment, poverty and social exclusion" (pp. 57–58).
- Remaining work: workers are "increasingly employed under non-standard forms of employment contracts to perform those tasks where AI has not yet been able to replace humans (e.g. personal service jobs such as domestic staff in private households and/or jobs training machines)" (p. 77). Companies "resort increasingly to gig workers" (pp. 58, 77). Annex 4: "Non-standard forms of employment become the norm" (p. 95). The executive summary calls remaining jobs "often precarious, low-paying and lacking in benefits" (p. 9).
- Recruitment "is limited to highly AI-skilled individuals" (p. 77). Over 40% of respondents expected companies to invest in skills development for only a limited share of their high-skilled workforce, related to generative AI (pp. 61–62).
- Job quality: "aspects of workplace well-being and good-quality jobs (e.g. skills development, challenging/complex jobs, autonomy, contributions to organisational decision-making) lose ground" (pp. 59, 77).
- Careers: individuals face "frequent and involuntary career changes due to circumstances beyond their control" and lose the ability to pursue purpose-driven careers or self-directed trajectories, "competing with many others and with machines for a reduced number of jobs" (pp. 59, 77).
- Skills: human-centric skills (critical thinking, leadership, emotional intelligence) rise sharply in importance across almost all jobs because they are hardest to replace (pp. 57, 61, 67). 72% of respondents agreed (54% somewhat, 18% strongly) (p. 61). Annex 4 nonetheless lists "Highly developed AI-related skills" as most needed (p. 96). 23% of respondents feared AI would limit opportunities to develop and use human-centric skills, which would then "be mastered by a select few at their own expense" (p. 61). Skills development focuses on tasks AI cannot yet do, "regardless of job satisfaction values or workers' well-being" (p. 59).
- Labour supply: the labour force declines through ageing and lower fertility. Some policymakers see AI replacement as a counterbalance (pp. 58, 76). 15% of respondents said displacement could help address demographic challenges (p. 62).
- Hours: 25% of respondents thought automation would lead to "people working fewer hours rather than people being fired" (p. 62).
- New opportunities: 5% of respondents expected new jobs in healthcare, education and environmental care (p. 62).
- International competition for talent "loses ground, as demand for human jobs is reduced", except in sectors AI cannot yet replace (p. 58). Regular migration is shaped by competition for AI-skilled talent (48% of respondents) (p. 65).
- Wages and labour share: Not addressed beyond "low-paying" remaining jobs (p. 9).

### Winners and losers

- **Winners:** the "few individuals, companies or organisations" owning and controlling AI (pp. 57, 76). Benefits are "Limited to a few entities that own and control AI technologies" (p. 95). A small number of highly AI-skilled workers get company investment and recruitment (p. 77). High-skilled workers are drawn to AI-advanced nations (pp. 65, 76–77).
- **Losers:** the entire workforce faces pressure at all skill levels (p. 59). Large segments are pushed into unemployment, poverty and exclusion (pp. 57–58). Gig and non-standard workers lose security and benefits (pp. 10, 77). SMEs are pushed out (p. 66). Marginalised and vulnerable groups face worse access to education and training (p. 67). Low-skilled workers concentrate in less AI-advanced countries (p. 65). Some regions suffer brain drain (p. 77). Local populations face social tensions from migration (p. 58).
- Respondents expected "differentiated treatment of workers, particularly in terms of investments in skills development and hiring processes" (p. 61).
- Inequality "Drastically deepens" (p. 96). Only 11% of respondents thought AI's economic benefits would be equitably redistributed and improve social welfare (p. 62).

### Household incomes and living standards

Unemployment, poverty and social exclusion spread, and "dissatisfaction among citizens grows" (p. 58). "Widespread disillusionment and social unrest" (p. 76). Individuals prioritise "their own survival" (pp. 58, 76). The executive summary describes remaining jobs as "precarious, low-paying and lacking in benefits" (p. 9). Income levels: Not addressed.

### Government finances

Not addressed.

### International economic patterns

- Irregular migration into the EU "increases as individuals are pushed to move towards places where they see the remaining limited opportunities", causing social tensions (p. 58). Per evolution path 4, irregular migration flows "increase significantly" (p. 88).
- AI affects both regular and irregular migration. Geographical polarisation leaves low-skilled workers concentrated in less AI-dominated or emerging economies and high-skilled workers in AI-advanced nations, with brain drain in some regions (pp. 76–77). 55% of respondents agreed AI exacerbates geographical polarisation, 48% that competition for AI-skilled talent shapes regular migration, and 16% that irregular migration falls because of AI-driven job shortages (p. 65).
- AI owners are "the dominant force in the global economy" (p. 76).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Human jobs become scarce, and people compete "with many others and with machines for a reduced number of jobs" (p. 77). Highly AI-skilled workers are in demand (p. 77). Prices of goods, essentials and assets: Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Workers bear "full responsibility for the upskilling and reskilling needed in order to try to cope" (p. 59). They have fewer opportunities to use and develop skills at work (p. 77). Companies see little incentive to develop "a disposable human labour force" (p. 77). "Human agency is eroded" and careers are fragmented by involuntary changes (p. 77).

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed. Poverty spreads (p. 58), but the cost of essentials is not discussed.

### Reliable safety net

Respondents agreed job losses are "not counterbalanced by measures safeguarding workers' rights and ensuring equal access to opportunities in the labour market and training" (D3 statement, p. 122; pp. 61–62). "Individuals are left alone and unsupported" (p. 59). Trade unions "no longer have the power to exert their influence and to safeguard workers' rights and interests" (pp. 60, 77). Policymakers cannot support equal access without AI owners' cooperation (p. 77). Only 11% of respondents expected equitable redistribution contributing to social welfare improvements (p. 62). Non-standard workers have "limited access to skills development, job security and benefits" (p. 10).

### Time for what matters

"Pervasive job losses may lead to more people seeking purpose through activities like volunteering, community engagement and spending time with loved ones" (p. 62). 25% of respondents expected fewer working hours rather than dismissals (p. 62). Purpose-driven careers stop gaining momentum (pp. 77, 90). AI tools in learning bring risks of "increased isolation, anxiety and health issues" (pp. 64, 67).

### A say in your future

"Democracy is increasingly undermined and extremist movements, fuelled by anger and frustration, begin to emerge" (p. 76). Authoritarian powers rise at the service of AI owners (p. 57). 63% of respondents agreed with this assumption (43% somewhat, 20% strongly). Confidence: 52% somewhat and 18% very confident (p. 60). Algorithmic manipulation creates "filter bubbles" (p. 60). "Citizens are left disempowered", but "some bottom-up anti-technology movements arise". 55% of respondents expected such movements (pp. 60, 76). A minority foresaw AI strengthening democracy through fair, transparent automated decision-making (pp. 60–61). Social dialogue is severely weakened, and negotiations prioritise employers and corporations (p. 14). Labour relations are "Extremely tense; power of trade unions drops significantly" (p. 95). A quarter of respondents saw potential for bottom-up diversity and inclusivity movements (p. 62).

### A fair share of progress

Benefits are limited to the few entities that own and control AI (p. 95). The scenario shows "concentration of power and wealth" (p. 11). Corporations are "free to prioritise profits over people" (p. 76).

## Other dynamics

- **Democracy and power concentration:** see above (pp. 57, 60–61, 76).
- **Environment:** "Environmental mitigation policies and sustainability practices are imperilled" (p. 76). Climate change is acknowledged, but firms prioritise growth despite energy use and waste (p. 76). Most respondents (59%) rejected the idea that eco-conscious practices lose importance. 54% said parts of the population and activist groups will keep valuing them, and 41% that irreversible climate change will force recognition (p. 64). The authors call this a "sustainability paradox". Current green investments, legal obligations and ESG compliance may keep climate policy relevant (p. 68). Respondents who strongly agreed with democratic erosion also expected sustainability to lose importance for all (pp. 65–66). Annex 4: "At risk / losing traction; individuals prioritise economic survival" (p. 96).
- **Education and training (initial assumptions):** "AI completely takes over education and training, and formal education as we know it ceases to exist". AI tutors replace teachers and trainers, AI tools provide advanced guidance, and traditional roles no longer exist (p. 59).
- **Education and training (consolidated after the survey):** AI "radically transform[s] skills development", dissolving boundaries between formal and non-formal provision. Established institutions form alliances with private stakeholders to stay relevant. Content comes increasingly from AI, peers and users. New AI-powered roles emerge, "but the human roles of teachers, trainers and career guidance professionals are not completely replaced" (p. 78). Nearly 70% of respondents said formal education will continue, and 53% expected public–private hybrid approaches (p. 62). 65% said AI tools such as VR/AR will enhance rather than replace methods. 60% expected public–private collaboration, and 34% expected private stakeholders to lead content generation (p. 63). Respondents "strongly reject[ed]" the idea of AI replacing teachers and guidance professionals (p. 63). The report notes that "in the field of education and training, the impact of extensive AI take-up is generally seen as positive" (p. 67).
- **Health and well-being:** isolation, anxiety and health issues from AI tools (pp. 64, 67). Erosion of workplace well-being (p. 77).
- **Information ecosystem:** algorithmic manipulation, fake news and populism, filter bubbles (p. 60).
- **Data and privacy:** discrimination, data misuse and privacy issues in automated access to jobs and training (pp. 62, 77).
- Safety and loss of control in the technical sense: Not addressed.
