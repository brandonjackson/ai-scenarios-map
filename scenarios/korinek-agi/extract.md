# Full AGI Scenario — extract

## Summary

With human task complexity bounded, AI reaches the point where it can perform all human work tasks in either about 20 years or about five years (pp. 31–32, Chart 1). In both cases, the model’s output rises far faster as labour scarcity ceases to constrain production (pp. 32–33, Chart 2). Competitive wages rise initially and then collapse near AGI as abundant machines substitute for scarce labour. Institutions could compensate workers and share output gains, while social choices could retain human labour in particular jobs (p. 33).

## Key details

- **Source:** Scenario Planning for an A(G)I Future, Anton Korinek, IMF Finance & Development, 2023-12. `sources/korinek-agi-scenario-planning-2023/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full print article, pp. 30–33, including the two charts on p. 32. The publisher PDF supplies the full text behind the condensed IMF landing page.
- **Scenario set:** Three technology scenarios. I: traditional business as usual, with an unbounded distribution of task complexity. II: baseline AGI in 20 years, with a finite ceiling on human task complexity. III: aggressive AGI in five years, with the same ceiling and faster progress. The catalogue’s augmentation row maps to I and its full-AGI row covers both II and III (pp. 31–33, Charts 1 and 2).
- **Geography:** General macroeconomic model without a named national calibration (pp. 31–33).
- **Authors' framing:** Two bounded-task-complexity cases used to stress-test economic policy. Chart 2 illustrates possibilities rather than precise forecasts (pp. 32–33).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Highly uncertain. At publication the author gave each of the three scenarios more than a 10% chance (p. 32).

### Timeline

- Starting year — efficiency gains from partial automation raise both output and wages (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).
- Year 5 — the aggressive case reaches full task automation. Output has accelerated and competitive wages fall sharply from their initial rise (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).
- Year 20 — the baseline case reaches full task automation. Aggregate output has grown rapidly and competitive wages have fallen from an earlier peak (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. The illustrated paths assume competitive returns in an efficient economy and show the market outcome before any compensatory policy (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The author does not put particular transfer, tax or deployment rules into the model. The article separately allows for social decisions to reserve some jobs for people (p. 33).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* Use a portfolio of scenarios and stress-test economic and financial institutions against all three. Reform taxation and expand safety nets where needed, potentially starting with a small basic income designed to scale. Monitor task benchmarks, AI R&D, talent and chip investment, deployment by sector, productivity and labour-market trends. Commission experts to revisit scenario probabilities as evidence changes (pp. 31–33). Design institutions to compensate labour-income losses and share AGI gains, as the same substitution mechanism drives rising output and falling wages (p. 33).

## Key dynamics explored

- If humans face a finite upper limit on task complexity, sufficiently advanced AI can reach that limit. The baseline case allows 20 years to automate even the most complex tasks, while the aggressive case shortens this to five (pp. 31–32, Chart 1).
- Before full automation, scarce human tasks still command wages. Near full automation, relatively abundant machines can do those tasks, removing scarcity as an output constraint and reducing the competitive market wage (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).
- Technical possibility and deployment can diverge. Organizational and regulatory frictions, capital accumulation and chip supply, or collective preferences for human roles can sustain demand for people (p. 33).

## State of technology

AGI can understand, learn and perform any intellectual task a human can. The scenarios assume a bounded distribution of human task complexity and reach a point of complete work-task automation. The timing differs across the two variants (pp. 31–32).

## State of adoption

*Applies across the set.* The author distinguishes benchmarks, resources committed to AI investment, use in production and eventual macroeconomic effects. Monitor each separately, across sectors. Organizational friction, regulation, capital accumulation or chip supply bottlenecks, and societal choices can make deployment lag technical capability (p. 33). Society may retain people in some technologically automatable positions, including priests, judges and lawmakers (p. 33).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Output accelerates in both AGI cases because labour scarcity no longer constrains production. In the illustrative chart, output reaches four times its initial index level around year 20 in the baseline case and year 10 in the aggressive case (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).

### Labour market dynamics

Competitive market wages initially rise and then collapse once the remaining tasks that require scarce labour become automatable. The chart shows the aggressive wage path peaking around year 3 and the baseline around year 10, then both reaching their starting level around their respective AGI year. The plot ends those traces at index 100 and does not show the eventual wage level. In practice, functions society wants performed by a human could sustain labour demand indefinitely (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).

### Winners and losers

Market wages fall near AGI even as output takes off. The article flags returns to capital and inequality among the indicators that can differ markedly across scenarios, and leaves the sharing of gains to institutional choices (pp. 32–33).

### Household incomes and living standards

The illustrated competitive wage decline implies labour-income losses for workers. The author says institutional design could compensate workers and convert AGI-driven growth into shared prosperity (p. 33).

### Government finances

*Applies across the set.* The author lists fiscal sustainability, taxation and the social safety net as areas to stress-test across markedly different trajectories. Monetary policy, financial regulation, industrial policy and development strategies must also be able to adapt (pp. 32–33).

### A fair share of progress

The author says falling wages and rapidly rising output stem from the same substitution of relatively abundant machines for scarce labour. Institutions could therefore share gains and compensate for lost labour income (p. 33).
