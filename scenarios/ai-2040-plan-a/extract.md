# AI 2040: Plan A — extract

## Summary

White-collar work increasingly revolves around supervising AI agents by 2028, and a US–China agreement prevents an uncontrolled leap to superintelligence. Companies around the world share frontier research and scale models only within agreed limits. Rapid adoption and robotic production drive explosive growth after 2032, with industrial output capped by tradable permits whose proceeds fund a large Citizen’s Dividend. Governments hold models at top-expert ability from 2035 while preparing for a gradual handover to aligned superintelligence around 2040. (Foreword; “2028: AI on the Ballot”; “2032: Controlled Explosive Growth”; “2033: The Citizen’s Dividend”; “2035: Pause at Top Expert AI”; “2040: Passing the Torch to AIs”)

## Key details

- **Source:** *AI 2040: Plan A*, Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Brendan Halstead, Eli Lifland, Ryan Greenblatt and Daniel Kokotajlo, AI Futures Project, July 2026. `sources/ai-2040-plan-a-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** [Economics supplement](https://ai-2040.com/supplements/economics-of-plan-a), cited by Appendix X for the mechanisms and model behind the growth estimates.
- **Reading notes:** Official complete PDF, including the 2030–40 chapters absent from the initial website text and the economic appendix. Page numbers follow printed pagination in the PDF.
- **Scenario set:** Plan A is the narrated path. The Foreword contrasts it with Plans B, C, D and S as alternative responses. The authors' counterfactual here is fully automated AI R&D and superintelligence in 2030 without the agreement. (Foreword; “2029: Choose a Path”)
- **Geography:** Global, centred on the US and China and the Consortium of countries implementing the agreement. (Foreword; pp. 9–13, 17–19)
- **Authors' framing:** A policy proposal stress-tested as a scenario. The depicted developments after implementation are the authors' conditional expectations. (Foreword)
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Successful implementation is not the authors' baseline expectation. They chose 2030 for uncontrolled acceleration within this story; the Foreword emphasizes uncertainty about timelines. (Foreword)

### Timeline

- 2027–28 — Americans and AI agents both perform substantial computer-based work. Congress passes the AI Transparency Act, most white-collar roles are disrupted, and the presidential election turns on AI. (pp. 5–8)
- 2029–30 — the US and China agree to a slowdown, verifiable limits and Total Research Transparency; the Consortium takes shape and develops conditions under which frontier research can resume. (“2029: Choose a Path”; “2030: Plan A is Established”)
- 2031 — developers assemble safety cases while constrained progress feels rapid outside frontier AI research. (“2031: Safety Cases”)
- 2032–34 — robots and AI contribute rapidly growing shares of output. Industrial caps and tradable permits generate revenue and fund the Citizen's Dividend. Consortium members also share revenue abroad. (pp. 16–22)
- 2035 — developers pause at top-human-expert capability; robots and agents continue to spread and be replicated. (pp. 23–24)
- 2036–39 — most Americans no longer have paid jobs, while public access to consumer AI and transparent research expands. Alignment practice and safety evidence improve, and some regulators begin to defer more decisions to trustworthy systems. (pp. 24–32)
- 2040 — more institutions delegate control to AIs, capability scaling resumes and caps loosen in space while tightening on Earth. (pp. 32–33)

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** On. Multilateral coordination and redistribution are central to the outcome. (Foreword; pp. 16–19)
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The 2027 US transparency law precedes a joint slow-down agreement. Frontier developers disclose research, cooperate with monitoring, and justify progress through safety cases. Industrial expansion is restricted to monitored special economic zones and limited with tradable compute and robot permits. The US channels a growing portion of receipts to equal shares in a Compute Dividend Corporation and shares part of the revenue abroad. The 2035 pause ends in 2040 under new guardrails. (pp. 5–6, 9–19, 23–24, 32)
- **Policies the source recommends:** Begin negotiations promptly; prioritize transparency, closing the gap between internal and public deployments, government technical expertise, verification, safety research, and coordination to diffuse power across countries and firms. Treat the particular dividend and technical details as scenario choices where the authors give alternatives or caveats. (Foreword; “2027: The Writing on the Wall”; pp. 18–19; Appendix Z)

## Key dynamics explored

- Growing agent competence and uptake produce field data that helps companies enter successive occupations. (p. 7)
- An uncontrolled rush to automate AI R&D risks a rapid capability jump and extreme concentration of power. Research transparency and joint limits let more actors catch up at a controlled pace. (Foreword; pp. 9–15)
- Digital labour makes ideas and designs abundant. Investment flows to physical bottlenecks until machines can manufacture more machines, which accelerates industrial expansion. (p. 16; Appendix X)
- Production caps restrain the speed of expansion so institutions and verification can adapt. Auctions turn scarcity rents into public resources used for redistribution. (pp. 16–19)
- Loss of economic leverage threatens voters' political leverage. Time freed from paid work and transparent, broadly available AI advice help citizens deliberate and exercise political influence. (pp. 27–28)

## State of technology

Early agents work across computer-based occupations but their work often requires review. Improvements spread through public research, better training and discoveries, including advances in alignment. By 2032 roughly 60 million continuously running agents at around 20× human speed collectively do work comparable to billions of humans, while robotics is catching up. The authors' model has AI and robots capable of performing about 95% of human cognitive labour by 2035 and physical labour by 2036. Systems remain near top-human-expert level during the pause, even as the number and speed of copies rise. Eventually more widely distributed developers offer systems reflecting different public specifications and values. (pp. 5, 16, 19, 23–25, 32; Appendix X)

## State of adoption

The 2027 US workforce includes 165 million humans and millions of agent instances. Buyers pay roughly $10 billion per month for agents capable in principle of almost any desktop task. White-collar workers increasingly manage agents. Trust and regulation preserve some human jobs, and labour shifts into physical construction before robotics expands. Research transparency allows dozens of AI companies to reach the frontier. Special economic zones channel scaling into monitored industrial areas, while consumer AI and robotics diffuse more broadly. (pp. 5, 7, 16–19, 24–27)

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

The narrative gives roughly 50% real GDP growth in 2032, and Appendix X estimates growth of around 100% on average from 2032 to 2037 with the controls. Reproducible labour lets additions to capital directly expand the AI-and-robot workforce, which recursively expands production. Regulatory caps bring the effective doubling time to about six months. In the very uncertain uncapped counterfactual the authors explore one-month doubling by 2033, with gains potentially concentrated in self-replicating industrial output. Rapid relative-price changes complicate intertemporal comparisons of real GDP. (pp. 16–17, 70–71)

### Labour market dynamics

Agent management replaces much of the content of white-collar jobs. People losing those jobs initially find new work in more trusted functions or physical construction, but robot manufacture eventually automates much of that work as well. The share of work done by AIs and robots, weighted by economic value, rises from about 20% in 2032 to about 85% in 2035. US employment falls from about 62% in 2027 to 26% in 2036 and 12% in 2040. (Foreword metrics; pp. 7, 16, 18, 24, 27, 32)

### Winners and losers

Some former white-collar workers briefly benefit from high demand for physical labour. Once most tasks are automated, scarce remaining human work may be well paid but income from it is concentrated. Highly invested households retain greater incomes than the dividend floor. The US and China receive outsized gains; the US compensates other countries in part through international transfers. (pp. 16–19, 31; Appendix X)

### Household incomes and living standards

The US Citizen's Dividend is illustrated at around $45,000 per adult in 2032 and roughly $1 million by 2035. The income distribution illustration gives an approximately $10 million floor in 2040. Amounts are in real 2025 dollars and should be interpreted alongside major relative-price shifts. Malnutrition, lack of medicine and homelessness become rare, many illnesses are cured and people have access to better education, travel and entertainment. (pp. 16–19, 27)

### Government finances

Federal personal income and payroll taxes together started at roughly ten times corporate taxes. Employment falls and firms re-invest, expensing or depreciating new capital and leaving little taxable income under the old tax code. US compute and robot permit auctions raise about $50 trillion in FY2032 and $180 trillion in 2034 in the model. The Compute Dividend Corporation has a legally assigned share of permit proceeds. The narrative also uses some of the resources for international sharing and major public health and resilience investment. (pp. 17–19, 31)

### International economic patterns

The US and China are the initial parties and negotiate industrial capacity shares with other Consortium countries. Transparency supports frontier catch-up across countries. Shared permit revenue finances US distributions abroad starting around $1,200 per adult annually outside China in 2032 and reaching about $10,000 in 2035, in the narrative’s rounded figures. Bargaining over shares and foreign aid continues later. Under the final regime, new industrial expansion shifts toward space. (pp. 9–13, 17–19, 31–32)

### Market pricing and scarcity

AI pushes many goods and services much lower in price. Existing land and other positional goods are supply-constrained and rise in relative price, as do goods requiring scarce human input. Housing can become cheap for people flexible about location where governments let robots build abundant new units. Robot and chip permits are scarce and costly early in the industrial expansion. (pp. 16–17, 25; Appendix X)

### Financial markets and stability

The appendix illustrates real interest rates around 100% during rapid growth. Nominal stability might take large-scale money creation, while leaving the money supply unchanged could entail massive deflation. Prioritizing reinvestment can be rational for firms when returns on new AI and robotic capital are very high. The scenario does not supply an investment-return or default path. (p. 17 n. 44; Appendix X)

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Early human work remains where physical labour or credibility is needed. The material basis of living standards shifts to the dividend as the employment rate falls. The narrative describes the stigma of leaving paid employment fading and meaningful contributions via politics, volunteering and giving. (pp. 16, 27–28)

### Affordable essentials

AI and robots cheapen food, medicine, durable goods and many services, while the income floor nearly eliminates malnutrition and homelessness. High-demand historic city land remains expensive. The alternative in jurisdictions with flexible planning is cheaper large housing in new developments. (pp. 16, 25–27; Appendix X)

### Reliable safety net

Permit receipts create a common income floor as jobs disappear, with the dividend growing enough to support almost all living expenses for most people. Some permit receipts are shared abroad. Health and resilience investments are additional protections. (pp. 18–19, 27)

### Time for what matters

Most people have more time for family, romance, learning, hobbies, competition and volunteering. Some miss feeling useful through paid work, and political participation offers one avenue to contribute. (pp. 27–28)

### A say in your future

The public forces AI onto the election agenda. Later, transparent systems with strong track records help voters interpret policy and assess officials. Although weak labour leverage creates a risk of techno-oligarchy, the 2036 election features better-informed voters and candidates committed to stewardship. Power over frontier systems is more widely distributed by 2040, even as humanity relinquishes more control to AIs. (pp. 7–8, 19–20, 27–28, 32–33)

### A fair share of progress

Open research allows a wider set of firms and countries to develop frontier models. US adults share auction rents; an additional share goes abroad, with lower initial payments than in the US. The narrative still has a long upper tail of incomes held by people with large investments. (pp. 18–19, 31)

## Other dynamics

The decade gives society time to work on safety, public health resilience and the risks of manipulation. People adopt AI-curated news and advice and argue about surveillance, diversity of values and political influence. The 2040 handover leaves unresolved issues about what authority humans should retain, how AIs follow public values and how expansion in space is administered. (pp. 19–20, 27–33; Appendix AC)
