# Will A.I. Become the New McKinsey? — summary

*Ted Chiang, The New Yorker, 4 May 2023*

**The scenario.** Chiang's argument is about *who AI serves*, not what it can do. AI, like McKinsey, is "capital's willing executioners": a tool for doing what management wants without management taking the blame. "As it is currently deployed, A.I. often amounts to an effort to analyze a task that human beings perform and figure out a way to replace the human being." The danger isn't paperclip-maximising superintelligence but "A.I.-supercharged corporations destroying the environment and the working class in their pursuit of shareholder value."

## Hard work pays
Broken, and getting worse. AI makes layoffs easier, as McKinsey "helped normalize the practice of mass layoffs as a way of increasing stock prices." Augmentation isn't enough: personal computers were pure augmentation (they replaced typewriters, not typists) yet US median household income lagged far behind per-capita GDP, which nearly doubled after 1980. "The claim that better technology will necessarily improve people's standard of living is no longer credible."

## Affordable essentials
Already broken, and AI won't fix it. "Real-estate prices, college tuition, and health-care costs have all risen faster than inflation... In 1980, it was common to support a family on a single income; now it's rare." People would trade streaming and online shopping for owning a home and avoiding medical bankruptcy.

## Reliable safety net
Treated with suspicion. Chiang likes universal basic income in principle but sees AI leaders' support for it as a way "to pass the buck to the government". This is accelerationism: make things bad enough that the state has to step in, accepting "significant pain and suffering in the short and medium term."

## Responsive government
Weak against capital. The only routes to a fairer outcome are "taming" (regulation) and "resisting" (unions, activism) capitalism. Chiang asks whether AI could empower unions or worker cooperatives, and cites the 1976 Lucas Plan, but finds no existing "labor-consulting firm" for workers.

## Time for what matters
The ideal is a future where "no one is forced to work at any job they dislike", reachable only if labour becomes *more* powerful than capital, which AI currently prevents.

## Bottom line
A future in which AI doesn't need to be very capable to break the social contract. It only needs to be useful to employers as cover for decisions they already wanted to make.
