# Will A.I. Become the New McKinsey? — extract

## Summary

Companies deploy AI to cut labour costs and increase shareholder returns, with the tool playing a role like that of a management consultancy (¶1–7). Faster or cheaper work can leave employees worse off when owners capture the savings, even when the software augments workers (¶18–22). Chiang imagines a future with more power for labour and asks whether technologies could strengthen unions, worker-owned cooperatives and regulation (¶17, 23–29).

## Key details

- **Source:** Will A.I. Become the New McKinsey?, Ted Chiang, The New Yorker, 2023-05-04. `sources/chiang-new-mckinsey-2023/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text. Paragraph locators count from the opening paragraph, excluding the headline, byline and captions.
- **Scenario set:** Standalone
- **Geography:** The United States is the principal reference point, with a UK example of worker-led industrial planning (¶18–25).
- **Authors’ framing:** A critical essay and conditional warning about corporate incentives (¶1–7, 17–19).
- **Authors’ view of likelihood:** No probability is stated. Chiang expects lower labour costs and higher corporate profits, while treating rising median income as conditional on distribution policy (¶20–22).

### Timeline

Next few decades — the balance of power could move towards either management or labour. One hundred years out — an idealised future leaves everyone free to spend time on personally fulfilling activities (¶17–18).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off in the main warning. Existing market incentives favour management and redistribution depends on political action (¶5–9, 20–24).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** No existing universal basic income for workers displaced by AI. The author treats government intervention as uncertain and says deploying on the assumption of eventual mitigation could cause prolonged harm (¶9–12).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Use economic policy to distribute technological gains and ask whether AI can support regulation, grassroots action, unions and worker-owned cooperatives; the essay poses these as directions without specifying detailed instruments (¶21–26).

## Key dynamics explored

- Consulting-style tools can legitimise choices that management wanted to make and diffuse accountability (¶1–7, 23).
- A firm can choose systems that maximise shareholder value over systems restricted to pro-social choices (¶5–8).
- Worker augmentation improves personal productivity but does not ensure sharing of the resulting gains; Chiang uses word processors and spreadsheets as historical examples (¶18–22).
- Relying on future UBI after displacement deepens existing inequality while leaving workers exposed in the interim (¶9–12).

## State of technology

AI is considered both as a collection of cost-cutting technologies and as semi-autonomous, obedient problem-solving software. In either case it can serve harmful objectives chosen by human customers (¶5–8).

## State of adoption

Management selects applications that make tasks cheaper or replace employees. Chiang contrasts that with still-hypothetical applications for labour organisations and worker-owned firms (¶5–8, 23–25).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Computers and the internet increased output and worker productivity, while median incomes lagged per-capita GDP. Chiang uses that historical comparison to question whether future AI augmentation raises the median income (¶18–22).

### Labour market dynamics

AI reduces labour costs and makes it easier to lay people off. Workers can also become more productive without receiving commensurate pay, depending on firms’ incentives and distribution policy (¶7–9, 20–23).

### Winners and losers

Shareholders benefit from lower labour costs, while workers bear risks of replacement, weakened bargaining power and inadequate sharing of productivity gains (¶7–12, 20–23).

### Household incomes and living standards

The author points to lagging US median household income, above-inflation increases in housing, college and health-care costs, and the declining prevalence of single-income households. He argues worker augmentation alone will not ensure better living standards (¶18–22).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

The author questions the ability of individual productivity gains to lift worker pay or living standards when economic policy permits gains to concentrate (¶18–22).

### Affordable essentials

As an historical comparison, US real estate, higher education and health care became less affordable despite the conveniences of information technology (¶18–21).

### Reliable safety net

UBI is discussed as a possible response to displacement, but Chiang objects to assuming it will follow only after conditions become severe (¶9–12, 22).

### Time for what matters

An idealised future lets people avoid jobs they dislike and devote time to what they find most fulfilling. Chiang associates a path towards that ideal with more labour power (¶17–18).

### A say in your future

The essay asks how AI might aid regulation, grassroots activism, labour unions and worker-owned cooperatives, and cites the Lucas Plan as a worker-led attempt to redirect production (¶23–26).

### A fair share of progress

Technological progress raises living standards only with economic policies that distribute benefits appropriately, in the author’s argument (¶18–22).

## Other dynamics

Chiang’s warning includes environmental damage from firms pursuing shareholder value. The essay compares corporate incentives today to a post-war corporate culture that placed a higher priority on employment security (¶12–13, 26–27).
