# Business as Usual — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Off.** The outcome comes from market structure (monopolistic AI pricing) and from workers finding new tasks. The simulation assumes no fiscal response beyond the Federal Reserve cutting rates and transfers at their usual level. The authors' options (taxing, regulating or nationalising AI monopolies; closing intellectual-property profit shifting; moving the tax base off labour) are labelled "illustrative rather than recommendations" and sit outside the scenario.

## Big assumptions
1. **Capability.** AI and robots can replace essentially all existing jobs in the long run, as a pure substitute for labour with no productivity gains beyond holding output constant.
2. **Adoption.** Full diffusion, with no timeline. The simulation displaces 10% of workers in a single quarter of 2025.
3. **Markets.** Closed-weight models keep a performance lead, so a few firms per task market can price above input cost. Meanwhile the economy generates new human tasks paying the same average wages as the jobs lost.
4. **Politics.** None modelled. Corporate profits stay onshore and taxable.

## What must be true for this to occur
- New remunerated work (social and emotional labour, jobs not yet imagined) absorbs displaced workers at similar average pay within about five years.
- Closed-weight models keep a sizeable lead over open-weight ones, sustaining pricing power.
- AI firms' profits are booked and taxed in the United States rather than shifted abroad.
- Lower prices for automated tasks do not shrink market size so much that aggregate profits fall.

## Tensions and weak points
- The scenario is built for revenue, not welfare: the five-year 14.2% unemployment spell and the unknown pay spread across the new jobs are given only in passing, so the social-contract reading is thin.
- Its "business as usual" label fits public finances, not workers or market structure. The map places it in replacement-and-concentration because existing jobs are all replaced and profits pool in a few firms, even though labour income is restored on average.
- "Gains" here are monopoly profits partly recycled through corporate tax. Who holds the equity is not discussed, so `x_labor` reads concentration from the profit share alone.
- The 10% simulation's 2035 nominal GDP ($43.0 trillion) is slightly *below* baseline even though revenue shares are above it, a reminder that the model is not built for extreme shocks.
- Neighbours: `anthropic-substantial` (moderate replacement, concentrated, finances holding) and `shumer`. Within the RAND set it sits directly above `rand-fr-hyperconcentration` on the labour axis and left of `rand-fr-price-shocks` on distribution.

## Scoring notes
Scores are 2/–/3/–/–. Work is 2: the worst-off are the displaced tenth of the workforce, who face a five-year spell of unemployment before re-employment at similar average pay. The long-run picture alone would be 3. Safety net is 3: transfers are assumed to cover 60% of prior consumption through the spell, and revenue rises, so existing capacity is not overwhelmed. Essentials, government and time are not addressed. With only two promises scored, the scenario has no headline rating. Map: `y_fiscal` +0.3 rests on the authors' statement that this is the only scenario where revenue rises; `x_fiscal` −0.05 reflects similar average wages after a long transition with an unspecified pay distribution.
