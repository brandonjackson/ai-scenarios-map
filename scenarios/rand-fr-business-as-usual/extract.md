# Business as Usual (Scenario 1: monopolistic AI pricing, new tasks for humans) — extract

## Summary
AI replaces human labour in existing industries, but humans find other economically valuable tasks that pay the same wages, on average, as their prior jobs, so after disruption the labour market stabilises with people working in new industries (p. 11). AI resources are concentrated in a small number of firms within each market (and possibly across markets), which collect profits above the cost of inputs (p. 11). Labour-tax revenue is preserved and corporate profits rise, so this is the only scenario in which the authors expect federal revenue to increase, absent productivity gains (pp. 11, 13–14). In the nearer-term simulation, 10 percent of the workforce is displaced, unemployment sits at 14.2 percent for five years before returning to baseline, and by 2035 personal and corporate income tax revenue are both slightly above baseline (pp. 16, 29, 31).

## Key details
- **Source:** Federal Revenue When AI Replaces Labor: An Examination of Economic Scenarios with Highly Capable Artificial Intelligence (RR-A4980-1), Carter C. Price and Akshaya Suresh, RAND Corporation (Education and Employment Program, RAND Education, Employment, and Infrastructure; part of the Social and Economic Policy Rethink project), 2026. Catalogue date August 2026; the revision history dates revision 2.0 to May 8, 2026 (p. 33). `sources/rand-federal-revenue-ai-labor-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** The same analysis was first published by RAND as a working paper in August 2025; revision 2.0 added a discussion of open versus closed economies and clarifying language (p. 33). That earlier version was not read. Otherwise none.
- **Reading notes:** Full text read (front matter, Summary pp. iv–vii, body pp. 1–23, Appendixes A–C pp. 24–33). Figures 1, 2, 3 and A.1 are images; only their titles, axis labels and captions survive in the text file, so the Figure 3 scenario landscape is known from the surrounding prose and Tables 2–3. Page locators use the report's printed page numbers.
- **Scenario set:** Four extreme cases built from two framework questions: does AI adoption create new remunerated work for humans (new tasks vs no new tasks), and how are AI systems priced (monopolistically vs at or near the cost of inference) (pp. 7–10, Fig. 3). Siblings: 1 Business as Usual (monopolistic + new tasks), 2 Hyperconcentration (monopolistic + no new tasks), 3 Price Shocks (at cost + new tasks), 4 System Breaks (at cost + no new tasks) (pp. 11–13). Each is examined in two cases: a longer-term case in which essentially all existing jobs are displaced (qualitative, with revenue-vulnerability shares) and a nearer-term case in which 10 percent of the workforce is displaced, simulated with a FRB/US-based model over 2025–2035 (pp. iv, 6, 15–17).
- **Geography:** United States; federal revenue only. The framework implicitly assumes a closed economy (p. 22).
- **Authors' framing:** Thought experiment / planning scenarios bracketing the range of fiscal outcomes. "analyzing the hypothetical question of how the economy would function with sharply reduced demand for human labor can provide useful indicators for policymakers to track" (p. 1). The four are "extreme cases"; "examining the extremes provides an indication of the range of plausible outcomes" (pp. 9–10). The authors call their assumptions conservative and the analysis "likely pessimistic" because AI is treated as a pure substitute for labour with no total factor productivity gains (pp. 1, 10).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability assigned. "it is likely that the ultimate outcome will differ depending on the relevant market for each type of task" (pp. 9–10). None of Nordhaus's six early-warning indicators of an economic singularity is triggered at present (pp. 19–20). A sizable performance gap between closed- and open-weight models would make the monopolistic scenarios more likely (p. 20); heterogeneous effects, with some jobs replaced and others augmented, "could make scenarios 1 and 3 more plausible" (p. 22). Heterogeneity in AI's labour effects "could make scenarios 1 and 3 more plausible" (p. 22).

### Timeline
- Long-run full-displacement case — no horizon year; "We do not make any assertions about the timeline for these scenarios," best thought of as far enough ahead that automation has fully diffused (p. 10).
- 10-percent simulation (Q1 2025–2035; snapshots reported for 2027, 2030 and 2035) (pp. 15–16, 28, 31):
  - 2025 Q1 — AI displaces 10 percent of the workforce across the income distribution; unemployment rises from the 4.2 percent baseline to 14.2 percent; TFP is raised 11 percent for ten years to hold output constant (p. 29).
  - 2025–2030 — unemployment stays at 14.2 percent for five years (p. 29).
  - 2027 — real GDP growth 2.2 percent; corporate income tax 3.4 percent of GDP vs 2.7 baseline; inflation 1.8 percent (p. 31).
  - 2030 — displaced workers find new jobs and unemployment returns to baseline (p. 29); real GDP growth 2.0 percent; inflation 1.4 percent (pp. 31–32).
  - 2035 — real GDP growth 2.0 percent vs 1.8 baseline; nominal GDP $43.0 trillion vs $43.9 trillion baseline; personal income tax 14.2 percent of GDP vs 14.0; corporate income tax 3.6 percent vs 2.8; interest rate 3.4 percent; inflation 1.7 percent (pp. vi, 16).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Off — the scenario unfolds through markets and existing institutions; the simulation assumes no fiscal response, and the authors' policy options are offered as preparation outside the scenario (pp. 17, 21).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** No fiscal response to the employment shock; the Federal Reserve lowers interest rates under its maximum-employment mandate; the model assumes unemployed people receive transfers sufficient to sustain on average 60 percent of their prior consumption (p. 17). Otherwise not addressed.
- **Policies the source recommends:** For concentrated AI generally (scenarios 1 and 2), the authors point to regulating, specially taxing or nationalising AI systems if windfall profits emerge, and to eliminating tax avoidance such as shifting intellectual property abroad, which could otherwise erode the corporate revenue that this scenario relies on (pp. vii, 9, 21).
  *Applies across the set.* The authors present these as "illustrative rather than recommendations"; the right combination depends on which scenario emerges (pp. vii, 21–22). Some of the more aggressive options "may require changes to the U.S. Code or even the Constitution" (pp. vii, 21).
  - Reduce the federal debt so the balance sheet can absorb larger shocks and the government has room for fiscal intervention (pp. vii, 21).
  - Shift the tax code away from labour taxes toward corporate and excise taxes, since 84 percent of federal revenue comes from individual income or payroll taxes (pp. vii, 20).
  - Explore AI excise taxes on model inputs (computer hardware, electrical power generation), AI queries or computational time, especially during the transition (pp. 20–21).
  - Eliminate tax avoidance schemes such as moving intellectual property to foreign subsidiaries, via IRS rules on intellectual property transfers, international tax treaties or legislation (pp. vii, 21).
  - Maintain price stability / stabilise nominal demand if at-cost AI causes deflation; deliberately inducing inflation could preserve capital-gains revenue but hurts fixed-income households, stagnant-wage workers and holders of unindexed assets such as fixed-rate mortgages; tools should be used cautiously to avoid raising the debt burden (pp. vii, 21).
  - Plan or pilot a universal income or asset program in scenarios where humans do not find new jobs; it needs a robust funding source because paying it from federal funds would worsen the fiscal situation (pp. vii, 21).
  - Regulate, specially tax or nationalise concentrated AI systems if windfall profits emerge; this would let government steer AI's effects and raise revenue (e.g. for universal income) but would change innovation incentives and distort the market (pp. vii, 21).

## Key dynamics explored
*Applies across the set*

- Federal revenue depends heavily on labour income: in 2024, 84 percent came from individual or payroll taxes and about 66 percent is due directly to labour, so labour-displacing AI could disrupt most of it (pp. iv, 1, 8).
- AI's direct effect is a shock to the labour- and capital-income channels between individuals and businesses, which has second-order effects on the other revenue channels (government–individual and government–business flows) (pp. 6–7, Fig. 2).
- Whether displaced workers find new remunerated tasks determines how much labour-tax revenue disappears; if new industries fully replace lost labour demand, personal and payroll revenue could be largely unchanged in the long run, higher or lower depending on the new income distribution (pp. 7–8).
- How AI is priced determines capital and business revenue: monopolistic pricing yields substantial taxable net income; at-cost access yields little or no profit and hence no extra taxable income (p. 8). Pricing depends on the industrial organisation of each task market (pp. 8–9, App. A).
- Lost labour income reduces consumption (68 percent of GDP in 2024), which in turn hurts business and capital income and further reduces federal and state revenue (pp. iv, 8).
- Corporate-tax gains from AI profits would not offset lost personal-income-tax revenue, because business marginal rates are lower than individual rates, unless corporate rates were roughly doubled to parity with labour (pp. iv–v, 18).
- Because income is concentrated among high-paid white-collar workers, automating those jobs would have an outsize revenue effect relative to their share of employment (pp. iv, 17–18).
- In the 10-percent simulation, the employment shock leads the Federal Reserve to lower interest rates to spur investment; if investors see higher risk-adjusted (possibly monopolistic) returns the economy keeps growing, but competitively priced AI can produce deflation and much lower nominal GDP. Because debt grows with deficits and interest rates rather than inflation, lower nominal GDP makes debt service harder (p. 17).
- Nominal output hinges on AI pricing, not competition: AI produces the same quantity as replaced labour by assumption, so real output is essentially unchanged and nominal output is lower with at-cost AI (p. 17).

*This scenario*

- Model-owning firms charge above input costs (electricity, water, amortised chips and hardware); where a single firm dominates a task market and AI input costs are below human costs, profits are higher than today (p. 11).
- Because humans find new tasks, labour income does not collapse in the long term, so labour-tax revenue is maintained (p. 11; Table 2, p. 14: personal, payroll, excise and customs taxes "0", business income taxes "+").
- Macroeconomic dynamics and shifts in the distribution of consumption may shrink a market's size if lower prices are not offset by higher quantity, so aggregate profits from that market could decline (p. 11).
- Net effect: plausibly higher federal revenue in the long term if new-job labour income matches what AI replaced and AI firms' higher profits generate more revenue (p. 11).
- Higher profits raise federal revenue from corporate taxation and capital gains, barring tax avoidance such as shifting intellectual property to foreign subsidiaries (p. 9).
- In the simulation, nominal GDP reaches $43.0 trillion in 2035 vs $43.9 trillion baseline (p. 16); after the Fed cuts rates, investors who see higher risk-adjusted returns in firms, possibly from monopolistic returns, keep the economy growing (p. 17).

## State of technology
*This scenario.* AI capability is concentrated in a small number of firms in each market and possibly across markets, with pricing above input cost (p. 11). Monopolistic scenarios become more likely if closed-weight models keep a sizable performance lead over open-weight models (p. 20).

*Applies across the set.* "Highly capable" AI that substitutes for, rather than complements, human labour; the report does not distinguish intellectual from physical labour, so these systems "would necessarily include robots" (pp. 1, 6). AI produces goods and services "in the exact quantity and quality" of the replaced labour, with no total factor productivity gains beyond what is needed to hold output constant (pp. v fn. 2, 1, 10). Labour-replacing automation is "at present, hypothetical" (p. 2).

Pricing framework (App. A): the price of a task is set relative to the input price pI (mainly electricity plus amortised chips and hardware), the monopolist's profit-maximising price pM and the human price pH. For tasks where open-weight models suffice the price is min(pH, pI); where a closed-weight model dominates it is min(pH, pM) provided pM > pI; if pM < pI humans keep the task (pp. 24–25). Marginal cost of AI equals input cost, so supply is constrained only by demand given adequate compute; pI could be much higher for physical work requiring robots (pp. 24–25). Markets may be finer than industries, e.g. the market for producing and filing a will (p. 10 fn. 23). The authors are agnostic whether there is one AI monopolist across all markets or one per knowledge-work sector (p. iv fn. 1).

Task types: satisficing tasks (e.g. filing taxes) are expected to be dominated by the lowest-cost narrow model; decelerating tasks may support several models at different price points; accelerating (winner-takes-all) tasks favour the highest-performing model. No inherent reason for a natural monopoly across all tasks, but there may be one within a task type. With equal performance the lower-priced, usually open-weight, model dominates unless a closed model adds value through bundling or cross-subsidy (pp. 26–27, Fig. A.1). Extreme concentration means a single supplier behind high entry barriers (proprietary data, high compute requirements, specialised algorithms); the opposite extreme resembles open-weight models running on personal devices (p. 9). Open-weight examples: Gemma (Alphabet), Llama (Meta), Qwen (Alibaba) (p. 9 fn. 21).

Indicators to watch: pace of algorithmic advancement and model efficiency (smaller models moving to the edge, relevant to at-cost pricing); breadth of model capabilities (benchmarks may be unreliable); the gap between open- and closed-weight models; and robotics performance, unit price and production (p. 20).

## State of adoption
*This scenario.* AI fully displaces existing jobs in the long-run case; humans move to new industries. Disruption occurs before the labour market eventually stabilises (p. 11).

*Applies across the set.* In the long-run case automation has "fully diffused through the economy"; the authors make no timeline assertions and do not model rates of change, sequencing or industrial-organisation details (pp. 10–11). Displacement will "likely be gradual and job by job" because AI is rolled out incrementally and robots take time to build (p. 15). Non-price factors (AI speed, human preferences for or against dealing with humans) also shape deployment (p. 24). In the simulation, 10 percent of workers from across the income distribution are replaced in a single year (from Q1 2025), after which the economy rebalances with no further shocks; the 10 percent level is arbitrary, chosen to be measurable without breaking model assumptions (pp. 15, 28). A variant considers the top 10 percent of earners displaced first, e.g. a radiologist fully replaced or a junior attorney doing a senior partner's tasks with AI assistance; higher-paid jobs may in practice be harder to automate, and Hinton's 2016 radiologist prediction had not materialised nine years later (pp. 17–18, fns. 34–35). Some intellectual fields may be slower to automate because of tacit knowledge (pp. 22–23).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
*This scenario.* 10-percent simulation: real GDP growth 2.2 / 2.0 / 2.0 percent in 2027 / 2030 / 2035 against a baseline of 2.1 / 1.9 / 1.8; nominal GDP $32.5 / $36.2 / $43.0 trillion against $32.5 / $36.5 / $43.9 trillion (p. 31). TFP is raised 11 percent for ten years so that output is unchanged by the displacement, and the displaced workers then produce in new jobs (p. 29). In the long-run case no extra TFP growth is needed for AI to be revenue neutral (p. 15).

*Applies across the set.* AI is assumed to add no productivity beyond replacing labour; in the simulations TFP is raised only so that the net output change from replacing labour is zero, which "does not represent productivity growth from AI complementing" the workforce (pp. vi, 1, 16, 28–29). If AI raised productivity, real growth would be proportionally higher (p. v fn. 2). Real GDP growth is similar across scenarios while nominal GDP differs sharply (pp. vi, 16).

### Labour market dynamics
*This scenario.* Long run: AI replaces labour in existing industries, but humans find other economically valuable tasks paying the same average wages; disruption gives way to stabilisation in new industries (p. 11). New work might draw on social and emotional labour (DeLong's "smiles"), and may be as hard to conceive today as a social media influencer was 30 years ago; about 60 percent of workers in 2018 held job titles that did not exist in 1940 (p. 8). Simulation: unemployment rises from 4.2 to 14.2 percent for five years, then returns to baseline as humans find new jobs (p. 29). If the new income distribution shifts higher, labour revenue could be higher, or lower if it shifts down; new industries may pay differently even with a similar distribution (pp. 7–8).

### Winners and losers
*This scenario.* AI-model-owning firms that dominate task markets gain higher profits (p. 11). Workers keep similar average wages after moving to new tasks (p. 11). The distribution of income across workers in the new industries is not specified (pp. 7–8). If the top 10 percent of earners are displaced first, monopolistic AI firms could extract profits whose corporate taxes offset up to half of the lost labour income (p. 18).

### Household incomes and living standards
*This scenario.* Labour income does not collapse in the long term because displaced workers find new tasks paying the same average wages (p. 11). During the simulated five-year unemployment spell, the model assumes transfers support on average 60 percent of prior consumption (pp. 17, 29). Even with reemployment at similar compensation, the shock would feel like a recession (p. iv).

### Government finances
*This scenario.* Long run: 0 percent of federal revenue directly vulnerable (p. 14, Table 3). Expected direction (Table 2, p. 14): personal income taxes 0, payroll 0, business income +, excise 0, customs 0, other 0; net effect +. "the only scenario in which we would expect an increase in federal revenue is scenario 1" (p. 13). No additional TFP growth is needed for revenue neutrality (p. 15). 10-percent simulation: personal income tax 12.9 / 15.0 / 14.2 percent of GDP in 2027 / 2030 / 2035 (baseline 13.0 / 15.1 / 14.0); corporate income tax 3.4 / 3.7 / 3.6 percent (baseline 2.7 / 2.7 / 2.8); real corporate revenue $0.85 / $0.96 / $1.05 trillion (baseline $0.66 / $0.69 / $0.80 trillion); average interest rate on federal debt 3.4 percent in 2035 vs 3.5 baseline (pp. vi, 16, 31). Corporate gains depend on avoiding profit shifting abroad (pp. 9, 21).

*Applies across the set.* 2024 revenue shares: personal income 47 percent, payroll 37, business income 10, excise 2, customs <2, other <2 (p. 5, Table 1). About 60 percent of personal income tax is labour-derived and 21 percent capital-derived (capital gains about 13 percent of taxable personal income, business income 8 percent), with 19 percent from other sources, mainly transfers (p. 5). Labour-related taxes are roughly 65 percent of revenue (37 payroll + 28 from wages in personal income tax) (p. 8); the Summary gives about 66 percent and the introduction two-thirds (pp. iv, 1). Business taxes are 10 percent and the capital component of individual taxes about 7 percent (lower than the 9 percent implied by income shares because capital is taxed at lower rates) (p. 9, fn. 22). Since 1950 revenue has run 16–20 percent of GDP with spending a few points higher; the mix shifted from 50/50 business-and-excise vs personal-and-payroll in 1950 to 84 percent personal and payroll (pp. 2, 6). With high and rising debt the government may be unable to provide a strong safety net in a large labour disruption; once the Social Security trust fund is exhausted, benefits shrink to match payroll revenue absent a law change (p. 6). Unemployment insurance spending would spike temporarily; demand for other safety-net programs would rise but is not analysed (p. 6). The top 10 percent of earners received 49 percent of pretax national income in 2022 and paid 72 percent of individual-tax revenue (34 percent of all federal revenue; about 25 percent from their labour income). Displacing them would be a shock slightly larger than a typical recession's revenue dip: with monopolistic AI, corporate taxes could offset up to half the lost labour income and the dip would likely be smaller than a typical recession; with at-cost AI there is no offset and the drop would be significantly larger than a typical recession (p. 18). Even with reemployment at similar pay, the fiscal shock would be equivalent to a recession, though revenue could eventually rise (p. iv). Across the 10-percent simulations, revenue is about one-fourth lower when AI is at cost than when priced monopolistically, and higher when displaced workers find jobs (pp. vi, 16). Tax provisions tied to nominal values (capital-gains cost basis, some deductions and credits, backward-looking transfer thresholds) make deflation matter for revenue (pp. 13, 21, 24). No fiscal response to the employment shock is assumed in the simulation (p. 17).

### International economic patterns
*Applies across the set.* The framework implicitly assumes a closed economy. If the United States leads in AI there may be revenue from AI trade, though profits must be large enough to offset lost labour income; if non-U.S. firms lead, rents accrue abroad and business revenue falls. Mass U.S. unemployment alongside stronger labour markets elsewhere could prompt substantial emigration, shrinking the tax base (p. 22). Shifting intellectual property to foreign subsidiaries could erode revenue from concentrated AI (pp. 9, 21). Future work: scenarios where U.S. firms export AI, and where a foreign-owned concentrated AI displaces U.S. labour without raising U.S. capital-tax revenue (p. 22).

### Market pricing and scarcity
*This scenario.* AI prices sit at the monopolist's profit-maximising price (min of human price and pM, if pM exceeds input cost) (pp. 11, 25). Prices for automated tasks may fall; if quantity does not rise enough, market size and aggregate profits in that market could decline (p. 11). Simulated core inflation 1.8 / 1.4 / 1.7 percent in 2027 / 2030 / 2035 vs 2.0 baseline (pp. 31–32). Closed-weight dominance should produce higher nominal GDP than open-weight models, barring general equilibrium effects (p. 25).

### Financial markets and stability
*This scenario.* Average interest rate on existing federal debt 3.7 / 3.4 / 3.4 percent in 2027 / 2030 / 2035 vs 3.8 / 3.5 / 3.5 baseline (p. 31). Investors seeing higher risk-adjusted, possibly monopolistic, returns keep the economy growing after the Fed cuts rates (p. 17). Otherwise not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Displaced workers find new economically valuable tasks paying the same wages, on average, as their prior jobs (p. 11). Not otherwise addressed.

### Affordable essentials
Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net
*This scenario.* The simulation assumes transfers sustain the unemployed at 60 percent of prior consumption during the five-year unemployment spell, with no new fiscal response (pp. 17, 29).

*Applies across the set.* With high and rising debt the federal government may not be able to provide a strong safety net in a large labour disruption; Social Security benefits would shrink once the trust fund is exhausted absent a law change; unemployment insurance is temporary and other programs cover specific goods or have requirements beyond need (p. 6).

### Time for what matters
Not addressed.

### A say in your future
Not addressed.

### A fair share of progress
*This scenario.* AI gains accrue as higher profits to the small number of model-owning firms, while workers' average wages are maintained in new jobs (p. 11). The authors' option to regulate, specially tax or nationalise concentrated AI if windfall profits emerge applies here (pp. vii, 21).

## Other dynamics
*Applies across the set.* Economic-singularity indicators (Nordhaus 2021): elasticity of substitution between capital and labour above one; rising productivity growth; rising capital share; accelerating capital-output ratio growth; rising share of information capital; hidden productivity growth from mismeasurement. The authors judge none triggered: elasticity likely below one, productivity growth not rising, after-tax corporate profit share at a record but on a multi-decade trend and flat since COVID-19, no acceleration in capital-output ratio, no substantial rise in information capital (lagged data), little evidence of mismeasurement (pp. 19–20). The authors note there would "likely be broad social changes" from these AI systems but do not examine them (p. 22). Limitations: the FRB/US-based model is built for monetary policy, is not designed for extreme shocks, uses fixed relationships last re-estimated in 2017 and treats firms and individuals as homogeneous, so distributional effects are hard to isolate (pp. 28, 32). Unexplored: policy interventions and their trade-offs, safety-net costs, and different paths, sequencing and timing to labour-replacing AI (pp. 22–23). AI could also help tax collection: the IRS estimated $542 billion lost in 2021 to underreported income (p. 2).
