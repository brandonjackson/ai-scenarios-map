# Keep the Future Human: the AGI race — extract

## Summary

Leading companies race to develop autonomous, broadly capable AI to replace labour and capture a share of economic activity. States see a strategic reason to race as well, which makes it harder for any participant to slow down (pp. 22–25). As AI systems become more capable, competitive pressures push firms and governments to delegate to them, shifting power away from workers and ultimately from human decision-makers (pp. 26–36).

## Key details

- **Source:** Keep the Future Human: Why and How We Should Close the Gates to AGI and Superintelligence, and What We Should Build Instead, Anthony Aguirre, Keep The Future Human, 2025-03-05. `sources/keep-the-future-human-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** Two paths in the essay are the unchecked AGI race and coordinated limits paired with controllable Tool AI. The dividing line is the combination of high autonomy, broad generality and superhuman capability (pp. 3–8, 14–17, 47–48).
- **Geography:** Global, with Silicon Valley firms, the United States and China prominent in the discussion (pp. 22–25, 31–34).
- **Authors’ framing:** A warning about the trajectory the author sees in corporate and national competition (pp. 2–6, 22–36).
- **Authors’ view of likelihood:** Aguirre calls large-scale risks nearly inevitable if racing dynamics continue uninterrupted. He says views vary about how many months or years it will take to cross thresholds, and does not assign a numerical probability (pp. 4–6).

### Timeline

March 2025 — the essay describes an approaching threshold and a race already underway. Over a short period after AGI, many cognitive jobs can be automated and authority delegated; physical displacement follows with improved robotics. More capable AI could accelerate its own improvement (pp. 4–6, 26–36).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off in the baseline. No effective coordinated limits interrupt the race (pp. 22–36).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** States and companies continue following economic and strategic incentives, with no binding interruption to continued capability and autonomy gains (pp. 22–36).
- **Policies the source recommends:** The essay separately proposes limits on compute and unsafe capabilities, liability, tiered requirements, international agreements and continued development of Tool AI (pp. 37–56).

## Key dynamics explored

- Companies pursue labour substitution because businesses pay more to replace a salary than households would pay for optional productivity tools (pp. 22–24).
- A feared first-mover advantage pushes nations towards competitive development and raises geopolitical tensions (pp. 24–25, 31–34).
- A participant who retains human decision-making can lose out to rivals whose systems are faster and more capable. Humans initially approve more decisions, then lose practical ability to understand or control the systems (pp. 33–36).
- More capable systems can support their own improvement, which can widen the gap with their users and make loss of control harder to reverse (pp. 34–36).

## State of technology

AGI combines autonomy, generality and high capability across cognitive domains. Systems operate at speeds and scales people cannot match, and their reasoning and actions are difficult to inspect. Robotics eventually extends the exposure to physical jobs (pp. 14–21, 26–36).

## State of adoption

Initially adopted to match competitors, reduce labour costs and improve decisions. Delegation spreads from individual assistants to corporate and state decisions and institutions. A small number of giant developers and their counterparties can concentrate control (pp. 22–28, 33–36).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

The economic prize is capturing a fraction of global output by lowering or eliminating labour costs. The essay gives no modelled aggregate growth rate and warns of disruption to the institutions that support existing production (pp. 22–28).

### Labour market dynamics

Rapid cognitive-task automation may raise inequality, unemployment or underemployment faster than society can adapt. Screen-based jobs without in-person or external legal obligations are especially exposed. Physical work is exposed later as robotics improves (pp. 26–28).

### Winners and losers

Control initially concentrates in giant companies and possibly governments. Workers and ordinary citizens lose economic and political leverage. The author expects increasingly autonomous systems to absorb power over time (pp. 26–36).

### Household incomes and living standards

Job loss and income inequality can increase, while social disruption limits adjustment. The essay’s basic-income proposals concern the alternative path (pp. 26–28, 50–51).

### Government finances

Not addressed.

### International economic patterns

Potential first-mover advantages motivate strategic competition. Aguirre argues that national contests can favour systems that escape practical oversight even if leaders initially believe they will gain national power (pp. 24–25, 31–36).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Activities previously limited by their cost can become cheap enough to overwhelm institutions designed around scarce human effort (pp. 26–28).

### Financial markets and stability

AI-operated firms and financial markets could move at speeds and complexity beyond human comprehension or regulatory control, amplifying harmful externalities (pp. 27–28).

### A say in your future

Synthetic media, surveillance, dependence on AI advice and eroded confidence in elections weaken people’s ability to understand and govern institutions. In the extreme case humans lose the capacity to steer the future at all (pp. 26–36).

### A fair share of progress

The article warns that benefits can concentrate in companies and states even before autonomous systems gain effective control (pp. 26–36).

## Other dynamics

Aguirre anticipates an information environment with plausible false material and pervasive persuasion, weakening trust and human cultural production. He also discusses geopolitical instability and catastrophic misuse at a high level (pp. 26–36).
