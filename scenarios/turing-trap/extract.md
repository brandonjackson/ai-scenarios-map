# The Turing Trap — extract

## Summary

AI increasingly imitates and automates economically valuable tasks (pp. 272–275). Productivity rises while many workers lose market value and the ability to bargain over wages and conditions; those who own AI and scarce complementary assets can capture a greater share of output (pp. 274–277). With economic resources concentrated, political power also concentrates. If the people who lose power depend on owners’ choices for income and cannot change the terms, the resulting equilibrium becomes a trap (pp. 274, 276–277, 282).

## Key details

- **Source:** The Turing Trap: The Promise & Peril of Human-Like Artificial Intelligence, Erik Brynjolfsson, Dædalus / American Academy of Arts & Sciences, 2022-04-19. `sources/brynjolfsson-turing-trap-2022/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** [The Turing Trap (author preprint)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04200)
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** Two paths distinguished by whether AI primarily substitutes for people in existing tasks (the Turing Trap) or complements them to expand what they can do (escaping the Turing Trap). Both can raise productivity, with different consequences for income and power (pp. 272–276, 281–282).
- **Geography:** General economic argument with US evidence and tax examples (pp. 275–276, 280–281).
- **Authors' framing:** Warning about a possible equilibrium and an argument for changing the incentives that steer AI (pp. 272–274, 280–282).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated

### Timeline

The author expects machine intelligence to grow more powerful and pervasive in the coming decade from the 2022 publication. The two paths have no dated scenario milestones (pp. 273–274, 281–282).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. Existing incentives continue to favour automation (pp. 277–282).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Current US-style tax rules favour capital relative to labour. An automated economy can in principle redistribute output, though beneficiaries have limited bargaining power to enforce a continuing share (pp. 274, 280–281).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Rebalance capital and labour tax incentives, consider expanding the earned income tax credit, support education and retraining and change technical benchmarks to reward what humans and machines can do together. Build institutions resilient to the growing power of AI (pp. 280–282).

## Key dynamics explored

- As AI becomes more capable of doing existing human tasks, it becomes a closer substitute for labour. Substitution weakens wage bargaining and shifts income to scarce inputs and owners (pp. 272–277).
- Useful knowledge is dispersed and inalienable while embodied in people. Once codified in machines, its ownership can be transferred and concentrated, allowing its holders to control both income and decisions (pp. 276–277).
- Talented researchers get a well-specified challenge from replication, managers find labour costs and measurable replacement targets attractive, and the tax system underprices automation compared with employing people (pp. 277–281).
- Income concentration can become political concentration and make redistributive commitments hard to enforce. Costs of displacement can extend to local communities and social health (pp. 276, 282).

## State of technology

*Applies across the set.* AI is a general-purpose technology. Human-like capabilities include image and speech recognition, diagnosis and autonomy, while many useful systems perform quite differently from humans. Intelligence has multiple dimensions, and the feasible combinations of people and machines span a wider task space (pp. 272–275, 277–278 and Fig. 1).

## State of adoption

Businesses can swap machinery for measurable tasks with few initial workflow changes. Replacing every task in a job is harder; in one cited assessment of 950 occupations, machine learning could perform some tasks in most occupations and every task in none (pp. 278–280).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Automation raises output per labour-hour and can raise aggregate wealth. In the limiting arithmetic where a given output needs almost no labour, measured labour productivity grows without bound. Replicating existing output has limits on its benefit, illustrated by the thought experiment of fully automating ancient Greek production (pp. 274–275, 278–279).

### Labour market dynamics

Automation first removes some dangerous, dirty or dull tasks. As it substitutes for wider groups, less easily replaced talents become relatively more valuable, while many workers face lower wages, a weaker bargaining position and a declining share of income (pp. 274–276).

### Winners and losers

Owners, entrepreneurs, inventors and workers with rare complementary assets can capture more of the gains. Workers without such assets face declining value of their contribution and reduced power (pp. 275–277).

### Household incomes and living standards

Average living standards can rise with productivity. The income of people whose labour becomes dispensable depends on what the people who control technology decide to share (pp. 274–276).

### Government finances

Under the example of two firms earning identical profits, the firm without employees generates no payroll or labour-income taxes, whereas its labour-using rival and employees pay them. The source points to differences in labour and long-term capital-gains rates, deferral, and the step-up in basis at death (pp. 280–281).

### International economic patterns

Free trade is used as a historical parallel. Aggregate wealth can rise while regions and occupations lose, and promised compensation can prove difficult to sustain after the winners gain power (p. 282).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Automating an existing product can drive its price close to zero. This does not create new goods and services, and the author argues that such cheapness alone cannot replicate the changes in living standards brought by new products (pp. 278–279).

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Once workers lack indispensable assets or skills, they have less capacity to gain from effort or demand better terms (pp. 274–277).

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

A fully automated economy could support transfers to economically unnecessary workers. The author questions recipients’ ability to prevent those transfers from being reduced or terminated (p. 274).

### Time for what matters

Automation could increase leisure by reducing the labour needed for a given volume of output (pp. 272, 274, 278–279).

### A say in your future

Loss of indispensable knowledge and work weakens political influence, while the concentration of technological and economic power can translate into concentrated political power (pp. 276–277, 282).

### A fair share of progress

Productivity and wealth can increase while technological gains are allocated to a shrinking group. The contractual and political power to secure a share matters (pp. 274–277, 282).

## Other dynamics

When opportunities for paid work decline, effects can spill over to community well-being and health. The historical trade example also illustrates the potential for a political backlash (pp. 276, 282).
