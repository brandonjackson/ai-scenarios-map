# AI as Normal Technology — extract

## Summary

AI applications become powerful and widespread through decades of complementary investment, testing and organisational change (Part I). Firms and public institutions reorganise work around AI, with people spending less time on existing tasks and more time on oversight, coordination and newly valuable work (Parts I–II). Risk and gains vary by use and institution. Governments adapt liability, safety rules and public investment as experience accumulates (Parts III–IV).

## Key details

- **Source:** AI as Normal Technology, Arvind Narayanan, Sayash Kapoor, Knight First Amendment Institute at Columbia University, 2025-04-15. `sources/narayanan-kapoor-ai-normal-technology-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** Standalone
- **Geography:** General framework, illustrated primarily with US institutions and evidence (Parts I and IV).
- **Authors' framing:** A description of current AI, a prediction about its foreseeable future and a prescription for how to treat it (Introduction).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** The authors describe this as their median outcome and give no numerical probability (Introduction).

### Timeline

The main horizon is decades. Methods may improve discontinuously, while applications and adoption compound unevenly over much longer periods (“Economic impacts are likely to be gradual”).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** On. The authors expect institutions to evolve and propose ongoing public action on diffusion, safety, distribution and resilience (Part IV).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Existing institutions and rules of liability continue to matter, and oversight can evolve as automation becomes more capable (Parts II–IV).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Support research, evaluation and incident reporting to reduce uncertainty; invest in resilience, workforce training, AI literacy, open government data and energy infrastructure; adapt rather than freeze regulations; clarify liability and strengthen social safety nets (Part IV).

## Key dynamics explored

- Method invention enables applications, which must then diffuse. Diffusion and innovation feed back into one another, but changing organisations, infrastructure and institutions takes time (Part I, Fig. 1).
- Real-world reliability, evaluation and tacit knowledge limit what benchmark improvements yield in practice. Safety-critical settings require oversight and evidence (Part I).
- When production of a task becomes cheap, its economic value falls and people shift to tasks that newly command a premium, changing the target of comprehensive automation (“Economic impacts are likely to be gradual”).
- Human control can shift from checking every action to selecting rules, sampling decisions, monitoring outcomes and intervening or redesigning systems when conditions change (“Control comes in many flavors”).

## State of technology

Increasingly general AI methods make some kinds of application development easier. High-stakes uses such as autonomous driving face an enduring capability–reliability gap. The authors view intelligence as distributed and multidimensional, and argue that game-based comparisons offer a poor picture of superiority over society’s accumulated expertise and institutions (Parts I–II).

## State of adoption

Initial AI use and economy-wide transformation are different events. Reliability, workflow redesign, skills, infrastructure, legal clarity and trust shape diffusion. Safety-critical applications empirically lag technical innovation, and effective organisational change may take decades. Firms and countries differ in their capacity to diffuse useful innovations (Part I; “Realizing the benefits of AI”).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Productivity gains depend on complementary reorganisation, illustrated by factories redesigned around electricity and production lines. Aggregate gains can be large over time while arriving unevenly by sector. The article gives no GDP point forecast (Part I).

### Labour market dynamics

Workers increasingly oversee and coordinate systems, retain judgement in context-specific tasks and move into new roles. The economic value of many automatable tasks falls, even if existing work does not disappear at once. The authors urge workforce training and literacy in both public and private sectors (Parts I–II; “Realizing the benefits of AI”).

### Winners and losers

Affected workers may need compensation. Arts and journalism are already facing harms. The authors identify a bargaining-power imbalance between AI firms and media publishers and discuss mandatory negotiation with oversight (Part IV, “Realizing the benefits of AI”).

### Household incomes and living standards

Not addressed.

### Government finances

Governments could tax AI firms to fund arts and journalism, support displaced workers and invest in shared complements such as open data and reliable electricity. The essay offers no budget forecast (Part IV, “Realizing the benefits of AI”).

### International economic patterns

Countries differ in their ability to diffuse innovation, with consequences for economic growth and geopolitical power. Highly concentrated production and controls can create weaknesses in global systems (Part III; “Realizing the benefits of AI”).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Automating existing tasks can sharply lower their cost and value relative to human labour, redirecting work and demand to tasks that remain difficult or newly possible (“Economic impacts are likely to be gradual”).

### Financial markets and stability

The 2010 Flash Crash illustrates system-level hazards in interacting algorithms and the role of sector-specific regulation. It is historical evidence in the essay (“AI diffusion in safety-critical areas is slow”; “History suggests normal AI may introduce many kinds of systemic risks”).

Not addressed.

### Affordable essentials

### Reliable safety net

Stronger social safety nets can compensate people who lose from automation and lower public anxiety about AI (“Realizing the benefits of AI”).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Keeping the public sector capable of adopting AI can preserve accountability for basic public functions. Premature deployment can undermine trust and legitimacy. The authors favour institutional and human control appropriate to the application, including accountability for oversight (Part II, “Control comes in many flavors”; “Realizing the benefits of AI”).

### A fair share of progress

Complementary public investment, skills and retraining, and compensation can expand access and redistribute benefits. Regulation can both aid trust and clarify responsibility when designed around actual tasks and supply chains (Part IV).

## Other dynamics

The paper treats accidents, competitive pressure, misuse, misalignment and systemic risks individually. It advocates downstream defensive capacity, evidence-gathering and resilient institutions, and treats catastrophic loss of control as speculative (Parts III–IV).
