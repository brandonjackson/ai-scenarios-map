# Transformation Economy — extract

## Summary

From 2029 AI automates most remote work, supports major scientific advances and greatly improves productivity (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, opening and Capability). Two US companies dominate the frontier as the UK becomes increasingly dependent on imported AI and compute (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Distribution and model access, Adoption and Global cooperation). Large-scale job substitution pushes unemployment to recession levels. Lower demand and overseas profit flows offset most of the UK growth upside, while lower labour taxes and higher welfare spending squeeze public finances (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Labour displacement).

## Key details

- **Source:** AI Scenarios 2030: Helping policymakers plan for the future of AI, Government Office for Science, Government Office for Science (GOV.UK), 2026-06-15. `sources/go-science-ai-scenarios-2030-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None.
- **Reading notes:** Full GOV.UK HTML report, including the scenario narrative, the outlook to 2040, and the cross-cutting findings.
- **Scenario set:** Slow Burn and Open Frontier (trajectory 1), Augmented Growth and Transformation Economy (trajectory 2), and Take-Off (trajectory 3). The six uncertainties are capability, distribution and model access, security, adoption, labour displacement, and global cooperation (Executive summary, “Methodology” and “Scenarios overview”; ch. 2).
- **Geography:** Global developments, with implications for the UK (ch. 1, “Project scope”).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory policy-planning scenario. “They are not predictions, and the future may involve elements from all scenarios” (Foreword).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated. The 2040 outlook is more uncertain than the 2030 narrative (ch. 3, “Guide to the scenario narratives”).

### Timeline

- From 2029 — models automate most remote work and firms rapidly substitute agents for labour while adoption and scientific breakthroughs accelerate (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Capability and Labour displacement).
- 2030 — two US firms dominate the frontier, while unemployment reaches recession levels in the UK alongside reduced consumer spending and a worsening fiscal outlook (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Distribution and model access and Labour displacement).
- 2030s to 2040 — low-wage reallocation could stabilise employment while deepening inequality. Physical automation may catch up later, and further capability leaps or US nationalisation are possible (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Outlook to 2040).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off for economic adjustment policy. The scenario assumes US policy influence over frontier companies and existing limits on some automation, while leaving new policy answers to job and tax-base losses outside the scope (ch. 3, “Scenario limitations” and “Transformation Economy”, Distribution and model access, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The US government influences the leading companies through policy levers, which remain privately owned in the scenario. Existing rules prohibit some forms of automation. The 2040 outlook considers possible US nationalisation and access restrictions, and says fiscal and labour outcomes could worsen without reform (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Distribution and model access, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).
- **Policies the source recommends:** The report invites policymakers to monitor signs of each future, stress-test plans across the set, assess impacts from other perspectives and identify outcomes the UK could influence. It proposes backcasting, policy stress testing and considering shocks as methods (ch. 4, “How to use the AI 2030 scenarios” and “Three methodologies for applying scenarios to policy making”).

## Key dynamics explored

- Capital, compute, proprietary data and distribution advantages concentrate the frontier in two US companies. Market share further strengthens their control over inputs and downstream routes (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Distribution and model access).
- Lower model costs and competition from AI-native entrants encourage firms to take legal risks and automate. Lower transactional friction strains institutions and undermines firms whose business is intermediation (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Distribution and model access and Adoption).
- Substitution cuts wages and employment, weakening consumer demand and offsetting most expected growth gains. Limited domestic compute and a weak AI value chain push profits overseas (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Adoption and Labour displacement).
- Entry-level hiring collapses while experienced experts remain in demand, creating future shortages as they leave the workforce (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Labour displacement).

*Applies across the set.* The report treats workforce displacement and augmentation as concurrent possibilities across the set. Routine execution changes even in slower futures. Concentration at the frontier and commoditisation behind it can coexist. Uneven adoption and reliance on US- and China-centred ecosystems affect how productivity gains are distributed (ch. 4, “Cross-cutting implications across the AI 2030 scenarios”).

## State of technology

AI matches average humans at most cognitive tasks, exceeds expert performance in verifiable work, writes all code and completes projects that could take experienced people at least a month. Humans initially set high-level direction, while coordinated agents plan, learn, communicate and execute autonomously. AI contributes to advances in biotechnology, materials and energy. It automates much AI R&D with diminishing returns due to compute and data. Robots handle certain variable settings, mainly in industry. Two US companies can finance frontier training; open weights lag roughly 18 months. Energy and compute supply and efficiency gains make most applications cheap, while compute-intensive frontier uses remain costly (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Capability and Distribution and model access).

## State of adoption

Rapid economic integration changes business, public services and consumer transactions. Firms gain access to expert skills and technical advice. AI-native entrants disrupt laggards; software-as-a-service firms and knowledge brokers can collapse as intermediation becomes less valuable. UK energy prices and planning constraints make local compute supply lag, and organisations increasingly rely on European clouds. Agents gain considerable autonomy, and adoption of AI into payment and critical systems adds security exposure (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Adoption and Labour displacement).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Productivity jumps but unemployment and wage declines reduce demand enough to offset nearly all expected economic growth gains. Profits flowing overseas further reduce UK gains, and deflationary pressure later threatens recession (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, opening, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

### Labour market dynamics

Most firms automate many remote roles from 2029, and large enterprises can operate with minimal staff. About half of displaced people are re-employed, frequently at lower pay and with less security. Entry-level hiring collapses while demand persists for experts. Physical and face-to-face work is relatively stable, but robotics and autonomous vehicles increasingly displace people in sectors such as manufacturing. Roles requiring manual skill, human presence and social contact, or protected by regulation, expand. Costs and compute limit automation at the margin. People give agents end-to-end responsibility, retain final strategic decisions and override agents less as time passes. People may increasingly find lower-paid work in care, construction and hospitality before wider physical automation becomes viable (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

### Winners and losers

A minority of highly skilled professionals and asset owners captures most gains. Countries and organisations that adopt quickly pull ahead. Displaced workers, entrants and lagging firms lose out. A future two-tier economy could entrench a high-productivity asset-owning elite and low-paid precarious work for a majority (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Adoption, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

### Household incomes and living standards

Falling wages and recession-level unemployment reduce purchasing power and demand, while unemployment and more insecure replacement work widen income inequality. Healthcare access and outcomes improve through transformed public services (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Adoption and Labour displacement).

### Government finances

Lower labour income tax revenues and higher welfare expenditure strain public finances by 2030. Without economic reform, later growth could coexist with further revenue declines (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

### International economic patterns

The UK relies more on US systems and imported European compute, and profits from UK financial and professional services flow overseas. Technology trade imbalances reduce UK bargaining leverage, while access to advanced systems depends in some cases on geopolitical alignment and limits autonomy elsewhere. The UK could become a net importer of AI services and exporter of lower-value labour. US nationalisation could deepen dependence and US strategic advantage (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Adoption, Labour displacement, Global cooperation and Outlook to 2040).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Most AI is affordable and AI substitutes cost substantially less than workers. Leading models are still expensive for compute-heavy work. High UK energy prices and planning constraints leave compute in short supply. Unemployment and falling wages threaten later deflation (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Distribution and model access, Adoption and Outlook to 2040).

### Financial markets and stability

Frontier firms that cannot compete lose customers, collapse or pivot. AI integration into payments raises their exposure to security incidents; the report gives no asset-price or credit trajectory (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Distribution and model access and Adoption).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Young people lose entry points and many displaced workers face lower pay and insecure work. The 2040 outlook says the exchange of human labour for resources may need a fundamental rethinking (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

### Affordable essentials

AI-assisted diagnosis, treatment and personalised care improve access to healthcare and its outcomes (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Adoption).

### Reliable safety net

Rising welfare expenditure and declining tax from work create fiscal pressure. The report describes the risk of further labour and fiscal deterioration without reform, without identifying a new benefit in the scenario (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Large protests and anti-AI campaigns make AI a decisive political issue. Human sign-off persists, but people intervene less in decisions. Technology-importing countries lose autonomy and leverage (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Adoption, Labour displacement and Global cooperation).

### A fair share of progress

A small share of highly skilled workers and asset owners monopolises gains despite widespread access to expert capabilities and improvements in healthcare (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Adoption).

## Other dynamics

Systems generally act as intended and are better understood, but global safeguards are difficult to enforce and misuse persists. A future rapid acceleration in AI R&D could outpace alignment and human understanding. Human influence can erode even if systems remain secure (ch. 3, “Transformation Economy”, Security and Outlook to 2040).
