# Scenario B: left alone to ride the tide – navigating the AI shock waves on jobs — extract

## Summary

It is 2040. "Successive waves of AI transformation" have fundamentally reshaped the workplace and affect sections of the workforce "in radically different ways" (p. 71). The state and employers have stepped back from workforce skills development. Working-age adults are largely left to keep their skills relevant on their own, and a "two-tier workforce" results. Tier 1 embraces individual responsibility and thrives. Tier 2 struggles with it and falls behind (p. 71). Most jobs are transformed rather than replaced: augmentation marginally prevails over replacement, and companies choose replacement "when economically advantageous" (pp. 38, 72, 95). Traditional employment "has become a privilege of the few" and gives way to micro-tasks, project work and temporary contracts. The learning landscape becomes a tech-platform-dominated ecosystem. "Disparity spirals", "opportunity deserts" and rising mental health problems deepen inequality (pp. 72–73).

## Key details

- **Source:** Preparing for 2040: four AI-powered scenarios for the future of continuing skills development, Cedefop (European Centre for the Development of Vocational Training), Publications Office of the European Union, 2025-11. `sources/cedefop-preparing-2040-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None. The same report restates the scenario in its executive summary (pp. 9–10). Results from the later scenario-exploration and vision-building phases are due to be published in 2026 (pp. 8, 18).
- **Reading notes:** Full text read: executive summary, Chapters 1–2 (method), Chapter 4 (Scenario B assumptions and semi-Delphi results), Chapter 7.2 (consolidated scenario), Chapter 8, Annexes 1, 3 and 4, and the Scenario B section of the Annex 5 questionnaire. Figure 1 (impact–uncertainty matrix) and Figure 2 (respondents) are images that are not in the text extraction. Locators are the report's printed page numbers.
- **Scenario set:** One of four Cedefop scenarios for continuing skills development by 2040. The others are A "a future of opportunities – technology-driven competition for talent", C "staying afloat – AI opportunities missed" and D "AI unleashed – dominating the world of work and societies" (pp. 9–11). The set is not built on a 2×2 grid. It comes from a five-step process: (1) identifying 21 trends with the STEEPV framework, (2) impact–uncertainty analysis, (3) morphological analysis of possible evolutions for each trend, (4) four scenario outlines built on four "evolution paths", and (5) validation in a semi-Delphi survey with 132 respondents (pp. 8, 19–28). AI is the central trend. In both rounds of trend analysis, experts ranked all AI-related trends as highly impactful and highly uncertain (pp. 8–9). Scenario B is built on evolution path 2: "AI disrupts most jobs and businesses; it's impact is hard to manage and only some segments of the population benefit" (pp. 27, 88). Annex 4 compares the scenarios on AI uptake, benefits from AI, ability to cope with change, employment forms, labour relations, AI impact on jobs, skills most needed, inequality, learning landscape and sustainability (pp. 95–96).
- **Geography:** European Union / Europe (pp. 9, 38).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory planning scenario that feeds a stakeholder vision-building process. "These scenarios are not predictive but rather exploratory, aiming to identify areas that require attention, threats that need to be addressed and opportunities that should be leveraged" (p. 9). The scenario is offered as an example of AI's "more nuanced and complex" impact, "where the benefits and impacts of AI are unequally distributed" (p. 11). Its closing moral: "technological progress does not automatically translate to societal progress" (p. 73).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated for the scenario as a whole. The survey's stated purpose was "not to see which scenario is more plausible" (p. 98). Respondents strongly agreed (92%) that workers will (have to) take more personal responsibility for skills development but doubted that they are ready (p. 42). Confidence was higher for predictions about human behaviour than for technology-dependent changes (p. 43).

### Timeline

- Horizon: 2040 (p. 9). The survey framed it as "the next 15-20 years" (p. 98).
- No dated events within the narrative, which describes "successive waves of AI transformation" leading up to 2040 (p. 71).
- Survey premise (B1): in 15–20 years, Generation Z (born about 1997–2012) and Generation Alpha (born from about 2013) make up more than 60% of the workforce, alongside some Millennials and possibly some Gen X (p. 106).
- Study timeline: trend identification Sept–Oct 2023; trend analysis Oct–Nov 2023; morphological analysis Dec 2023–Mar 2024; scenario outlines Apr 2024; expert workshop May 2024; semi-Delphi survey Oct–Nov 2024; scenario exploration Dec 2024–Jan 2025; vision building Feb–Dec 2025 (p. 18).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. The state and employers "step back from workforce skills development". Neither prioritises inclusiveness, regulation fails to address quality, privacy and ethics concerns, and sustainability policies weaken for fear of undermining competitiveness (pp. 39–41, 71).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:**
  - The state steps back from workforce skills development, and individuals bear responsibility for it (pp. 9, 71).
  - "Neither the state nor employers prioritise inclusiveness and equal access to opportunities". Diversity and equality initiatives are left to civil society, and trade unions are too weak to contribute substantially (p. 41). Per evolution path 2, inclusiveness "is not a priority for any of the stakeholders" (p. 90).
  - Concerns over quality, privacy, ethics, inclusiveness and access in the learning landscape exist, but "regulations fail to address these concerns" (p. 40).
  - Policies fail to address the rising energy and resource use of AI "for fear of undermining competitiveness". The AI race dominates policy cycles at the expense of sustainability, and sustainability policies "decrease in importance" (pp. 38–40, 88).
  - Social dialogue shifts from tripartite to "a more fragmented and reactive model of social coordination, characterised by company-specific negotiations and limited to basic framework conditions", focused on short-term gains and damage control (p. 14).
  - Survey arguments mention that regulatory frameworks and ethical considerations favour augmentation, though cost-effectiveness may drive replacement (pp. 42–43).
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - For this scenario: the gap between responsibility and readiness "highlights the critical importance of building capability alongside increasing responsibility, rather than simply shifting responsibility without ensuring capability" (p. 44). The quality and sustainability of adaptation depend on "the availability and effectiveness of support structures for those requiring guided adaptation" (p. 44). Learning-conduciveness in AI-transformed workplaces "depends on conscious organisational choices regarding how AI is implemented and managed" (p. 43). Paths forward should be sustainable and "respect human limitations while maintaining necessary progress" (p. 44).
  - *Applies across the set:* Stakeholders should co-create a vision that prioritises "lifelong and life-wide learning and continuing skills development, individual empowerment, equitable access to opportunities and robust governance of AI technologies" (p. 79). Integrated learning systems should leverage institutional, self-directed and workplace contexts (p. 79). Social dialogue should be willing to "experiment with new forms of worker representation and social protection", prioritise lifelong learning and make sure all workers have a voice (p. 14). Technological advancement should be balanced with social equity and environmental responsibility (p. 79).

## Key dynamics explored

- AI transforms most existing jobs through augmentation, where tools enhance human capabilities, or redefinition, where jobs evolve with new tasks and skills requirements. Some jobs are replaced and some new jobs are created (p. 38). This changes job quality, meaning at work, autonomy, competence, relatedness and well-being (p. 38).
- Fast, cross-industry disruption is hard to manage, especially for companies that fail to keep pace with domestic and global competition (p. 39).
- Firms step back from comprehensive employee development and compete globally for top talent while "investing minimally in their broader workforce". The result is "a self-reinforcing cycle where skilled workers become more valuable while other workers risk obsolescence" (pp. 71–72).
- Responsibility shifts to individuals, so outcomes depend on prior advantage. Workers with better education, financial resources, location and social capital navigate well, while those starting disadvantaged fall further behind and socioeconomic divides deepen (p. 71).
- Augmentation versus replacement depends on economics. Cost-effectiveness may push organisations to replace workers despite a stated goal of augmentation (pp. 42–43, 45, 72).
- The "new digital divide" is about the ability to leverage AI tools effectively, not just access. People who cannot use them "move towards low-skilled/low-quality jobs, rather than unemployment, as labour markets are tight" (p. 72).
- Occupational polarisation produces "disparity spirals": "self-perpetuating cycles where a disadvantage in one area compounds difficulties in others" (p. 72).
- Remote work opens global opportunities for some but creates "opportunity deserts" in regions lacking technological infrastructure or good educational institutions. Urban innovation centres pull ahead (p. 73).
- Mental health problems compound disadvantage. Anxiety, stress and feelings of inadequacy impair learning and adaptation, pushing those affected into unemployment and inactivity (p. 73).
- Technology-driven growth clashes with the sustainability values of younger generations. Young workers avoid employers with poor environmental records and prefer temporary, value-aligned project work, which fragments the labour market (p. 72).
- A two-tier adaptation pattern: most workers can achieve "basic functional adaptation" with support, but "higher-order adaptive capacity" (autonomous learning, proactive skills development) is harder to achieve. "The challenge is not whether workers will adapt at all but rather how they will adapt and how sustainable their adaptation will be" (p. 44).
- Speed–adaptation tension: accelerating change exceeds "comfortable human capacity", raising cognitive load, stress and burnout (p. 44).
- *Applies across the set:* the "individual responsibility gap" is "particularly pronounced" in Scenarios B and D, "as workers are left to fend for themselves" (p. 12).

## State of technology

- "Fast-moving AI technological advancements" arrive in successive waves (pp. 38, 71). AI tools enhance human capabilities in augmented jobs (p. 38). AI creates demand for roles that manage and interpret AI systems and oversee its ethical and regulatory side (p. 38).
- Businesses need people for micro-tasks "better suited to humans than computers", for example improving and testing the accuracy of machine learning algorithms (p. 39).
- In learning and guidance, AI is used for data analysis and content generation. It powers guidance tools with in-depth analysis, pattern identification and personalised suggestions (pp. 40–41). Survey respondents had the lowest confidence in how AI-transformed learning will unfold (2.97/4.0) (p. 42).
- Growing AI development and use increases energy and resource consumption (p. 39). Companies do not apply responsible sustainability practices across the AI life cycle, "from design and development to deployment and disposal" (p. 39).
- Algorithmic management is a concern: "the spectre of algorithmic management and its potential to dehumanise workplace relationships looms large" (p. 45).
- The survey's B5 options mention blockchain or smart contracts as a nontraditional employment form (p. 110).
- Market structure, providers, compute and pricing: Not addressed. Tech companies lead learning-content generation as "new formal stakeholders" (p. 40).

## State of adoption

- AI uptake is "Extensive" (p. 95). "AI disrupts most jobs and businesses in Europe" (pp. 10, 106).
- Integration "varies dramatically across organisations and sectors". Some companies augment and others replace (p. 72). 54% of respondents expected 50–79% of businesses to use AI mainly for augmentation, 27% expected fewer than half, and 19% expected 80% or more (p. 42). Annex 4: "augmentation marginally prevails over replacement" (p. 95).
- Adoption is driven by technology-driven growth and global competition. Most businesses prioritise it over sustainability (pp. 38–39).
- Barriers and enablers: individuals' ability to use AI tools effectively (p. 72); regional technological infrastructure (p. 73); economic factors, regulatory frameworks and sector-specific conditions (p. 43); organisational choices about implementation (p. 45).
- In education and training, AI use "increases rapidly across all types of learning environments" (p. 92). 76% of respondents expected nontraditional stakeholders to dominate skills development (p. 42).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Most businesses prioritise "technology-driven growth" (p. 38). "Fast-moving AI technological advancements disconnect from sustainable economic growth and competitiveness" (p. 38). AI tools let people "perform tasks more efficiently or effectively" (p. 38). "While we have the tools for unprecedented advancement, their benefits remain unevenly distributed" (p. 73). No quantitative growth figures.

### Labour market dynamics

- The EU labour force declines "or stagnates at best" (p. 39). Labour markets remain tight, so people who cannot use AI tools move to low-skilled, low-quality jobs rather than unemployment (p. 72). Those with mental health problems are "pushed into unemployment and inactivity" (p. 73).
- Most existing jobs are transformed (augmented or redefined). Some are replaced and new ones created (p. 38). The value of non-routine and cognitive tasks increases (p. 38).
- Employment forms: "Traditional employment has become a privilege of the few, replaced by an ever-changing tapestry of micro-tasks, project-based work and temporary employment relationships" (p. 72). Micro-tasking and online micro-jobs rise, and employers promote nontraditional relationships (p. 39). Annex 4: "Nontraditional employment outpaces full-time, long-term employment" (p. 95). 51% of respondents expected traditional full-time, long-term employment to fall below 40% (confidence 3.08/4.0), with traditional and new forms coexisting and varying by sector and region (p. 42).
- Job mobility: individuals adopt a "freewheeling" approach "whether by choice or out of necessity", changing jobs, careers and employers often, both voluntarily and involuntarily (micro-tasking, short-term or precarious contracts, frequent lay-offs) (p. 39). Loyalty erodes (pp. 39–40).
- Workplace stratification: some workers are in "highly dynamic, tech-enabled environments rich with growth opportunities"; others are "confined to routine, lower-skilled roles with limited advancement possibilities" (p. 72).
- Skills: individuals must acquire AI-related technical skills and, "more importantly", human-centric skills such as problem-solving, creativity and adaptability (p. 38). Success requires "meta-learning abilities (i.e. learning how to learn), adaptability and resilience, not just technical skills" (pp. 72–73, 96). Per evolution path 2, human-centric skills are "extremely significant across most/all jobs" (p. 89). New roles in managing and interpreting AI and in AI ethics and regulation (p. 38).
- Workplace skills development: many companies neglect employee skills development and leave it to employees. Its importance in the workplace "falls because of AI/machines" (pp. 39, 90).
- Ability to cope: 70% of respondents expected 50–79% of the workforce to cope with rapid change, 18% expected less than 50%, and 12% expected 80% or more (confidence 3.06/4.0) (p. 41). 53% expected a majority to be ready for autonomous learning, while 41% expected less than half (p. 42).
- Global labour competition: with remote work, EU workers can work for international companies without relocating, and individuals "compete with the workforce globally" (pp. 39–40).
- Wages, hours and labour share: Not addressed. Better compensation in high-carbon sectors is mentioned (p. 72).

### Winners and losers

- **Winners (tier 1):** workers who "fully embrace individual responsibility and thrive" (p. 71), who start with better education, financial resources, location and social capital (p. 71), who master meta-learning (pp. 72–73), and who use AI tools to advance rapidly (p. 72). Top talent is competed for globally (p. 72). Urban centres with strong innovation ecosystems "pull further ahead" (p. 73).
- **Losers (tier 2):** those who "struggle with increased individual responsibility and fall behind" (p. 71). They are smaller in number than those who benefit (pp. 10, 38) and are pushed towards routine, lower-skilled roles, or into unemployment and inactivity if mental health suffers (pp. 72–73). Regions lacking infrastructure become "opportunity deserts" (p. 73). Companies that fail to adapt struggle (p. 39).
- The adaptation gap "is not necessarily age-related, as many might assume, but strongly correlates with educational background, access to resources, mental health and previous learning experiences" (p. 73).
- Generational divide: younger workers avoid high-carbon employers despite better pay. Companies struggle to attract young talent (p. 72).
- Inequality "Deepens among individuals and locations with 'opportunity deserts' in certain areas" (p. 96). Benefits from AI are "Not widely available; skills-based polarisation and social divide" (p. 95). "Those who are already privileged are able to leverage AI to further enhance their positions, while those who are marginalised are left behind" (p. 11).

### Household incomes and living standards

Those struggling to stay employed suffer harm to their "mental, physical and socioeconomic well-being" (pp. 10, 38). Socioeconomic disparities intensify across the EU workforce (p. 38). "Technological alienation" profoundly affects "mental health, social connections and overall well-being" (p. 40). Incomes: Not addressed.

### Government finances

Not addressed.

### International economic patterns

EU businesses compete for talent within the EU and globally. Remote work lets EU workers serve non-EU firms without relocating, deepening international competition (p. 39). International competition for talent is "significant, affecting most sectors" (p. 89). Irregular migration is "n/a" for this evolution path (p. 88). Cross-country gaps: Not addressed beyond regional "opportunity deserts" (p. 73).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Skilled workers become more valuable and top talent is scarce (p. 72). Pricing of goods, essentials and assets: Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Workers with prior advantages invest in skills, take "calculated career risks" and bounce back, while those starting disadvantaged "increasingly struggle to keep pace" (p. 71). "Initial advantages or disadvantages become more pronounced and harder to overcome" (p. 73). Responsibility is given to people for "something many are not capable of managing effectively" (p. 42). "We are all riding the AI tide, but some are surfing while others are struggling to tread water" (p. 73).

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

The state and employers step back (p. 71). "Traditional unionisation is weakened and cannot effectively support those lagging behind" (p. 72). Diversity and equality initiatives are left to civil society (p. 41). Support structures for those needing guided adaptation are flagged as decisive, but their presence is not described (p. 44). Social protection arrangements: Not addressed.

### Time for what matters

Rapid change brings "increasing cognitive load, stress and potential burnout" (p. 44). Rising mental health problems: "increased anxiety, stress and feelings of inadequacy" (p. 73). Some segments pursue purpose-driven careers and self-directed paths (p. 40). Younger workers prefer value-aligned project work (p. 72). Maintaining "human purpose in increasingly automated environments" is a concern (p. 45).

### A say in your future

New forms of worker representation emerge, especially in fluid-employment sectors and among younger workers who prefer "more flexible, cause-oriented forms of collective action" (p. 72). 80% of respondents agreed representation would be transformed, with "evolution rather than dissolution" (confidence 3.01/4.0) (p. 43). Labour relations are "Tense" (p. 95). Social dialogue becomes fragmented, reactive and company-specific (p. 14). Democratic processes: Not addressed.

### A fair share of progress

"Changes are hard to manage and only some parts of the population benefit" (p. 10). Benefits from AI are not widely available, with "skills-based polarisation and social divide" (p. 95). "Technological progress does not automatically translate to societal progress" (p. 73).

## Other dynamics

- **Environment:** Most businesses prioritise technology-driven growth over sustainability, and only a minority are driven by sustainability values (pp. 38–39). Eco-conscious consumer values gain prominence among most of the population, especially new generations, and clash with the AI-fuelled development model (pp. 38, 40, 72). Businesses implement AI "often at significant environmental cost" (p. 72).
- **Education and training:** The learning landscape is "a complex ecosystem dominated by tech platforms and AI-powered training systems" (p. 72). New nontraditional stakeholders include peers, users and tech companies offering AI-powered platforms. Tech companies lead content generation, and formal and non-formal education are fully integrated (p. 40). Individuals struggle to navigate the array of opportunities (p. 40). Teachers and trainers join multidisciplinary teams with tech experts to train AI models and develop content (p. 41).
- **Guidance:** AI-driven career guidance particularly benefits specific segments of the workforce and significantly transforms counsellors' roles (p. 41).
- **Health:** Rising mental health problems, technological alienation and burnout (pp. 40, 44, 73). The executive summary warns of "increased isolation, heightened anxiety and adverse effects on mental and physical health" from over-reliance on platform-dominated learning (p. 12).
- **Human agency:** Uncertainty over whether organisations will use AI tools "in ways that enhance human agency rather than diminish it". AI integration is "a complex socio-technical transformation" (p. 45).
- Safety, loss of control, geopolitics and information ecosystem: Not addressed.
