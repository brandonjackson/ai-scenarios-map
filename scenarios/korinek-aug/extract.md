# AI Augments Workers — extract

## Summary

AI automates a growing range of cognitive tasks, while workers move to new, on average more productive jobs (p. 32). The traditional case extends the historical pattern of automating simpler tasks and reallocating workers toward more complex ones (pp. 31–32, Chart 1). Modelled output keeps growing along a familiar trajectory and competitive wages keep rising while labour remains scarce (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).

## Key details

- **Source:** Scenario Planning for an A(G)I Future, Anton Korinek, IMF Finance & Development, 2023-12. `sources/korinek-agi-scenario-planning-2023/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full print article, pp. 30–33, including the two charts on p. 32. The publisher PDF supplies the full text behind the condensed IMF landing page.
- **Scenario set:** Three technology scenarios. I: traditional business as usual, with an unbounded distribution of task complexity. II: baseline AGI in 20 years, with a finite ceiling on human task complexity. III: aggressive AGI in five years, with the same ceiling and faster progress. The catalogue’s augmentation row maps to I and its full-AGI row covers both II and III (pp. 31–33, Charts 1 and 2).
- **Geography:** General macroeconomic model without a named national calibration (pp. 31–33).
- **Authors' framing:** Business-as-usual technology path used for policy planning. Chart 2 illustrates possibilities rather than precise forecasts (pp. 32–33).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Highly uncertain. At publication the author gave each of the three scenarios more than a 10% chance (p. 32).

### Timeline

The curves start at an index of 100 in year 0. The traditional paths for output and competitive wages both rise through the chart’s 30-year window. The article sets no calendar date for an endpoint (p. 32, Chart 2).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. The illustrated path comes from technological progress and market reallocation (pp. 32–33).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** No specific new intervention is built into the economic model (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* Use a portfolio of scenarios and stress-test economic and financial institutions against all three. Reform taxation and expand safety nets where needed, potentially starting with a small basic income designed to scale. Monitor task benchmarks, AI R&D, talent and chip investment, deployment by sector, productivity and labour-market trends. Commission experts to revisit scenario probabilities as evidence changes (pp. 31–33).

## Key dynamics explored

- Automation removes tasks from the lower end of the complexity distribution, leaving human workers to perform more of the higher-complexity tasks (pp. 31–32, Chart 1).
- Workers affected by automation move to new jobs with higher average productivity. Labour remains scarce enough to earn a rising competitive wage (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).

## State of technology

AI systems continue automating cognitive tasks. This case assumes no upper bound to the complexity of tasks humans can perform. Chart 1 places new human tasks in the open-ended tail beyond the expanding automation frontier (pp. 31–32).

## State of adoption

*Applies across the set.* The author distinguishes benchmarks, resources committed to AI investment, use in production and eventual macroeconomic effects. Monitor each separately, across sectors. Organizational friction, regulation, capital accumulation or chip supply bottlenecks, and societal choices can make deployment lag technical capability (p. 33).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Modelled output grows along the trajectory typical of past decades. Chart 2 rises from 100 initially to roughly 225 by year 30 (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).

### Labour market dynamics

Some workers are displaced from particular jobs. AI creates opportunities for them to move into jobs with higher average productivity. Labour remains scarce and competitive market wages rise to just under twice their initial level after 30 years (pp. 32–33, Chart 2).

### Household incomes and living standards
