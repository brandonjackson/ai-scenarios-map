# Income Inequality Increases — summary

*Scenario #2 of four in "How Might Fiscal Policy Respond to the Rise of Artificial Intelligence?" by Karen Dynan, Douglas Elmendorf and Louise Sheiner (Brookings Institution, August 2026), written for the Brookings–University of Utah conference on America's fiscal future. The four scenarios are layered thought experiments for US federal fiscal policy over CBO's 30-year horizon, not predictions; the authors assign no probabilities. Read from the paper's full text; see `extract.md`.*

**The scenario.** "AI is again a rising tide, but it lifts only some boats." Productivity growth rises by the same half point a year as in Scenario #1 and GDP ends about 16% higher by 2056, with no fall in employment. But all the extra income, capital and labour alike, goes to the top fifth of earners; incomes elsewhere are unaffected. Because that income is taxed at the top quintile's average federal rate (25.9% rather than 20.6%), federal debt ends 49 points of GDP below CBO's baseline, more than under shared gains, even though more people stay eligible for Medicaid and CHIP. On top of Scenario #1's choice about the size of government, policymakers must decide whether to make taxes and transfers more progressive.

## Hard work pays
Stalls for most. Nobody loses a job, but the extra labour income goes only to workers in the top quintile, and labour earnings shift up the distribution. For the other four fifths, effort pays what it would have without AI.

## Affordable essentials
Not addressed. The extract lists options (wider health-insurance subsidies, broader housing assistance, universal pre-school, eldercare and public transport) but says nothing about prices in the scenario itself.

## Reliable safety net
Holds by default. The existing tax and transfer system "redistributes automatically": because lower-income households do not get richer, more of them stay eligible for Medicaid and CHIP than in Scenario #1. Some see that as enough; others want more progressivity to raise incomes outside the top quintile.

## Responsive government
Under strain, not broken. The budget improves, but the authors cite the worry that greater inequality "hinders democracy by reducing social solidarity and giving some individuals the resources to exercise disproportionate political power". Recent history cuts both ways: past expansions of Medicaid, ACA subsidies and tax credits reduced inequality, while the 2017 and 2025 tax cuts and the 2025 benefit cuts increased it. Universal services "might engender wider support from voters" than means-tested ones.

## Time for what matters
Not addressed.

## Bottom line
Faster growth with nothing for most people. Nobody is worse off than without AI, and the Treasury does better than under shared gains, but the gap between the top fifth and everyone else widens sharply and the case for redistribution becomes a political fight.
