# Income inequality increases — extract

## Summary
AI is again a rising tide, "but it lifts only some boats". Average annual total factor productivity (TFP) growth in the US nonfarm business (NFB) sector rises from CBO's projected 1.1% to 1.6% over 30 years, as in Scenario #1. GDP is about 5% higher after a decade and 16% higher after three. But all the extra national income, capital and labour alike, goes to people in the top income quintile, and incomes elsewhere are unaffected. The result is "a sizable increase in income inequality" (pp. 2, 10; Tables 1, 3). Under current law, federal debt falls by 49 percentage points of GDP against the baseline after 30 years, more than in Scenario #1. The extra income is taxed at the top quintile's higher average rate, though more people remain eligible for Medicaid and CHIP (p. 11). Beyond the Scenario #1 choice about historical budget-to-GDP relationships, policymakers must decide whether to make taxes and transfers more progressive (pp. 11–12; Table 3).

## Key details
- **Source:** How Might Fiscal Policy Respond to the Rise of Artificial Intelligence?, Karen Dynan, Douglas Elmendorf and Louise Sheiner, Brookings Institution, August 2026. The paper was written for the Brookings Institution–University of Utah conference on "The Forces Shaping America's Fiscal Future" in June 2026 (cover). `sources/dynan-elmendorf-sheiner-fiscal-ai-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text (pp. 1–39, including references). Figure 1, the chart of debt paths, is image-only in the text extraction. Its values are taken from the prose and tables (p. 7).
- **Scenario set:** Four illustrative scenarios: #1 A rising tide lifts all boats proportionally; #2 Income inequality increases (this one); #3 Jobs are lost and new jobs emerge; #4 Capital wins amid major permanent job displacement. They are built from three drivers: the increase in annual TFP growth (0.5 pp in #1–#3, 1 pp in #4), the reduction in the number of workers (none, none, 2%, 6%) and the distribution of the extra income (proportional to income without AI; proportional to capital and labour but all to the top quintile; the same; all to owners of capital in the top quintile). "By construction, this second scenario is simply the first scenario plus a shift of income upward in the distribution" (pp. 1–3, 11; Table 1).
- **Geography:** United States, federal budget only. The authors exclude effects on other countries, international trade and financial flows, and state and local budgets (p. 1).
- **Authors' framing:** Thought experiment and planning scenario for fiscal policy. "The scenarios are not intended to be specific predictions, but rather illustrations of the types of effects that have garnered scholarly and popular attention in recent months" (p. 1). The scenarios are additional to the AI effects already in CBO's projections (p. 1).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned: "We do not attempt to assign probabilities to the different scenarios, which seems to us exceedingly difficult" (p. 1). *Applies across the set:* the four scenarios "include only a subset of the possible effects of AI" (p. 3). The conclusion says "immense uncertainty remains" (p. 32).

### Timeline
- Horizon: 30 years, matching CBO's long-term projections to 2056 (pp. 1, 6).
- 2022 — latest year of CBO's average federal tax rates: 20.6% for all households, 25.9% for the top quintile (p. 11).
- 2026 — federal debt held by the public is 101% of GDP in CBO's extended baseline (p. 6).
- Coming decade — CBO's projections already include AI adding 0.1 pp to annual NFB TFP growth (p. 1).
- After 10 years — GDP is roughly 5% higher than without AI (p. 10).
- 2056 (after 30 years) — GDP is 16% higher, and debt is 49 pp of GDP lower than the baseline's 175% (pp. 6, 10–11).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Off. The scenario's budget results assume no significant changes in fiscal policy, under current law and CBO's projection methodology. Responses are discussed as options, not built into the scenario (pp. 1, 4, 11; Table 3).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Current law and CBO's projection methodology, with the authors' extended-baseline treatment of discretionary spending: it rises with inflation for 10 years, then with GDP after a five-year transition (pp. 5–6). The existing tax and transfer system redistributes automatically (p. 11).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *This scenario:* options, not a single recommendation (Table 3, p. 10; pp. 11–12):
  - The Scenario #1 choice: take no action, or "raise spending and cut taxes relative to current law and projection methodology to maintain closer historical relations to GDP".
  - Increase the progressivity of the tax and transfer system, for example by returning to some tax rules of the mid-2010s or earlier and using the funds for people outside the top quintile (p. 12). Two routes: (a) expand means-tested benefits, such as wider eligibility for health-insurance subsidies or broader housing assistance; (b) expand low-cost or no-cost public services for everyone, such as pre-school, post-secondary job training, personal care for older people and public transport. The latter "might engender wider support from voters and less stigma for participants" (Lasky-Fink and Linos 2022) (p. 12).
  - Some argue the existing system's automatic redistribution is enough, since taxes and transfers aim to reduce market-income inequality "to a chosen extent" rather than reach a set after-tax distribution (pp. 11–12).

  *Applies across the set:* given uncertainty, "policies that are robust to different scenarios would be especially valuable" (p. 1). Waiting lets policy be targeted, but problems can become entrenched first, as with the China shock (p. 32). An alternative is to adopt insurance-like policies now, such as a modernised Trade Adjustment Assistance (TAA) on a limited scale or modest public equity purchases through a sovereign wealth fund. These could be scaled up later or switched on by automatic triggers tied to unemployment or the capital share (p. 32). Their costs "might not be large" (pp. 32–33). Table 6 catalogues all responses (p. 31). Combinations may suit (Marinescu 2025; Sperling 2026; Korinek and Lockwood 2026) (p. 31).

## Key dynamics explored
*This scenario*

- All extra income goes to the top quintile, and incomes elsewhere are unaffected (p. 10; Table 3).
- An upward income shift raises personal income tax revenue because the tax is progressive. It lowers payroll tax revenue because more earnings sit above the Social Security taxable maximum (p. 11).
- Federal benefit payments are higher than in Scenario #1 because lower-income people do not get the gains that would have reduced their eligibility. The authors assume the fall in Medicaid and CHIP spending relative to GDP is one-third smaller than in Scenario #1 (p. 11).
- The extra GDP is taxed at the top-quintile average federal rate of 25.9%, not the economy-wide 20.6% (p. 11).
- Social Security benefit effects of the earnings shift would be very small within 30 years, because productivity gains cumulate slowly and affect benefits only after retirement. They are not estimated (p. 11, n. 5).
- Rationales for more progressivity: declining marginal utility of income, well-being that depends partly on relative consumption, and worry that inequality "hinders democracy by reducing social solidarity and giving some individuals the resources to exercise disproportionate political power" (p. 12).
- History cuts both ways. Medicaid and CHIP expansions, ACA marketplace subsidies and refundable tax credits reduced inequality. The large tax cuts of 2017 and 2025 and the large benefit cuts of 2025 went the other way (p. 12).

*Shared with Scenario #1 (growth mechanics, repeated here)*

- Faster NFB TFP growth (NFB is about 75% of the economy) directly adds 0.38 pp to annual GDP growth. Investment and other adjustments lift GDP further (p. 4).
- Growth raises revenue relative to GDP slightly through bracket creep (p. 5). It lowers non-interest spending relative to GDP a lot, through Social Security price indexation for existing retirees, CBO holding Medicare and Medicaid flat in the first decade, unresponsive other mandatory spending and slower discretionary growth (pp. 5–6). It shrinks existing debt relative to GDP, and it raises interest rates, partly offsetting the other effects (p. 6).
- The improvement "may prove at least partly illusory" if policymakers keep historical budget-to-GDP relationships (p. 8).

*Applies across the set*

- Ramsey-style analysis gives no clear guidance on whether AI justifies larger or smaller deficits in any scenario (pp. 8–9).
- Faster labour-market disruption would raise public pressure for policy responses (p. 3).

## State of technology
AI is represented only as an increment to NFB TFP growth: 0.5 pp per year beyond the 0.1 pp in CBO's projections for the coming decade (pp. 1, 10). AI capabilities, providers, market structure and pricing are not addressed. *Applies across the set:* an AI boom might spur ongoing investment in computing capacity and energy production (p. 8).

## State of adoption
Not addressed for this scenario. *Applies across the set:* the authors do not explore a low-impact case in which public resistance dampens AI's spread. Pew (2025) found 50% of Americans more concerned than excited, 10% the opposite (p. 3).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
NFB TFP growth averages 1.6% a year instead of 1.1%. GDP is roughly 5% higher after a decade and 16% higher after three decades, as in Scenario #1 (pp. 2, 10). In Scenario #1 the paper also gives about 10% after two decades (p. 4).

### Labour market dynamics
No reduction in the number of workers (Table 1, p. 3). The extra labour income goes only to workers in the top quintile, and labour earnings shift upward in the distribution (pp. 2, 11).

### Winners and losers
The top quintile gains all the extra capital and labour income. The other four quintiles see no gain against the no-AI path ("Higher incomes increased and other incomes unaffected", Table 3, p. 10).

### Household incomes and living standards
Incomes outside the top quintile are unaffected by AI. Lower-income households do not get the gains that, in Scenario #1, would have made some ineligible for Medicaid and CHIP (pp. 10–11).

### Government finances
- Debt held by the public is 49 pp of GDP lower than the extended baseline after 30 years, against 39 pp in Scenario #1. The baseline rises from 101% of GDP in 2026 to 175% in 2056 (pp. 6, 11; Table 3; Figure 1, p. 7, dashed line).
- Method: the Scenario #1 effects plus the income shift. The top-quintile average tax rate of 25.9% (2022) is applied to the GDP increment instead of 20.6%. These CBO rates cover personal income, payroll, corporate income and excise taxes, roughly 90% of federal revenue, and exclude estate and gift taxes (p. 11).
- Personal income tax rises, payroll tax falls, and benefit spending is higher. The Medicaid/CHIP reduction relative to GDP is one-third smaller than in Scenario #1 (p. 11).
- Shared growth mechanics from Scenario #1: in that scenario the 39 pp splits into 30 pp from lower non-interest spending, 2 pp from revenue and 7 pp from GDP and interest effects on existing debt. Debt would be lower by a further 12% of GDP if interest rates were unchanged (pp. 7–8). Discretionary spending falls from 6.1% of GDP (2025) to 4.5% (2056) under +0.5 pp TFP, on the authors' approach (p. 6).
- *Applies across the set:* even at face value, "federal debt would continue to rise significantly relative to GDP in the scenarios we analyzed" (p. 30).

### International economic patterns
Not addressed. The authors exclude international effects (p. 1). *Applies across the set:* foreign capital inflows could hold down interest rates (p. 8).

### Market pricing and scarcity
Not addressed for this scenario.

### Financial markets and stability
Not addressed for this scenario. *Applies across the set:* CBO expects interest rates to rise by roughly the TFP increment, and the actual effect is uncertain (p. 8).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Not addressed.

### Affordable essentials
Policy options include extending eligibility for health-insurance subsidies and making housing assistance more broadly available to lower-income households. Universal services could include pre-school, personal care for older people and public transport (p. 12).

### Reliable safety net
The existing tax and transfer system redistributes automatically: more people stay eligible for Medicaid and CHIP than in Scenario #1 (p. 11). Some see that as sufficient. Others want more progressivity to boost incomes outside the top quintile (pp. 11–12). Shared with Scenario #1: the authors ask whether falling eligibility and retirees' relative income decline would look like a weaker safety net or a breach of "the generational social contract" (pp. 7–8).

### Time for what matters
Not addressed for this scenario. *Applies across the set:* the leisure-choice alternative is not analysed (p. 4).

### A say in your future
Greater income inequality may hinder democracy "by reducing social solidarity and giving some individuals the resources to exercise disproportionate political power" (p. 12). Universal services might win wider voter support than means-tested benefits (p. 12).

### A fair share of progress
Gains from AI go only to the top quintile (p. 10). *Applies across the set:* the authors expect "considerable pressure to ensure that gains from AI would be shared broadly across society" (pp. 30–31).

## Other dynamics
The paper excludes AI's effects on the environment, social interactions, national security, AI regulation, other countries, and state and local and foreign fiscal policy (p. 1). *Applies across the set:* alternatives not explored are small AI effects (public resistance; Jones 2026 on stable 150-year growth), AI reducing inequality by raising productivity more outside the top quintile (Bloom et al. 2024; Cruces et al. 2026), and the leisure case (pp. 3–4). AI "may become one of the central questions of our time" (p. 33).
