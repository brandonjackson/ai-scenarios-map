# Situational Awareness — extract

## Summary

Aschenbrenner extrapolates growth in compute, algorithmic efficiency and the ability of models to act as agents to get systems capable of working as remote employees around 2027. Large numbers of those systems automate AI research and could compress years of algorithmic progress into a single year, producing superintelligence before the end of the decade. The economic value of automating cognitive work and the strategic stakes pull vast investment into data centres and power, and bring frontier development under a US government project. Once computation and robotics can substitute for human labour, automated production could move the economy into a much faster growth regime. (Introduction; §§I–II, IIIa, IV)

## Key details

- **Source:** *Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead*, Leopold Aschenbrenner, June 2024, official PDF updated 6 June 2024. `sources/situational-awareness-2024/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None.
- **Reading notes:** Full publisher PDF, with section and printed-page locators.
- **Scenario set:** Standalone.
- **Geography:** The US and China dominate the strategic discussion. Supply chains, electricity, wages and downstream consequences extend globally. (§§IIIa, IIId, IV)
- **Authors' framing:** A forecast of a plausible near-term pathway coupled with a national-security argument for action. (Introduction; §§I, IV)
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** “AGI by 2027 is strikingly plausible.” The author acknowledges that computation, algorithmic progress, long-horizon agency and data could fail to sustain the extrapolation. (§I, including “The uncertainty”)

### Timeline

- 2019–23 — model capabilities move from roughly preschool level to roughly a smart high-schooler's on the author's informal comparison. (§I)
- 2025–26 — the author expects models to exceed college graduates in important domains. The rise of AI revenue would justify large further investments. (Introduction; §IIIa)
- Around 2027 — trend extrapolation reaches plausible autonomous remote workers, increasing the potential returns to scaling. Annual aggregate AI investment could exceed $1 trillion. (§§I, IIIa)
- 2027–28 — the author expects some form of US government AGI project. (§IV)
- By the end of the decade — automation of AI research produces the possibility of superintelligence, while individual compute clusters may cost more than $1 trillion. (Introduction; §§II, IIIa)
- 2030s — radically accelerated research and automated industrial expansion could compound within years, subject to adoption and social constraints. (§II, pp. 68–70)

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Mixed. Private investment and scaling drive early capability progress, while federal coordination increasingly shapes development and deployment. (§§IIIa, IV)
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** A national-security project coordinates frontier work and pools resources as the geopolitics and danger become apparent. The US and allies compete with China over who first develops the most powerful systems. (§§IIId, IV)
- **Policies the source recommends:** Harden the security and oversight of leading labs, expand reliable US power and industrial capacity, take alignment seriously and place superintelligence under accountable government institutions in cooperation with allies. (§§IIIa–d, IV)

## Key dynamics explored

- Improvements in compute, efficiency and model interfaces add to effective capability. The author sees qualitative jumps when a model can plan over long horizons and do remote work without continual supervision. (§I)
- The first capable AI researchers speed up work on better algorithms. Many faster copies make a reinforcing loop in which better systems in turn accelerate the next generation. (§II)
- The market value of labour-saving products raises AI revenue and makes larger clusters attractive, while energy and manufacturing have much longer lead times than software. (§IIIa)
- Reproducible robotic labour removes the constraint of a fixed human workforce, so factories can produce other factories, compounding output. (§II, p. 69)
- Strategic competition and potential for decisive power raise the incentives for national coordination and make alignment and institutional control more consequential. (§§IIIb–d, IV)

## State of technology

The author projects annual growth of about half an order of magnitude in physical compute and another half in algorithmic compute efficiency, plus qualitative improvements that make systems usable as agents. A capable remote worker can operate tools, sustain multi-step work and perform tasks presently done by human employees over computers. AI research automation could yield five or more orders of magnitude in algorithmic progress in a year. Later, automated researchers and substantially faster robotic workers accelerate activity in both simulation and physical facilities. Chip supply, data, long-horizon reliability and electricity are constraints. (§§I–II, IIIa)

## State of adoption

The author argues that assistants need adaptation and training of their users, whereas drop-in remote workers could more directly replace existing roles and jump in economic value. Software engineering and research are leading cases. Adoption across other professions and physical production depends on robotics, regulation, consumer preference and institutional response. Some fields may retain humans despite machines' stronger technical performance, with law, medicine and childcare given as examples. (§I; §II, p. 69; §IIIa, pp. 80–82)

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Aschenbrenner sees a potential move to a new growth regime, comparable to the Industrial Revolution as a structural break. Replacing a fixed labour force with reproducible AI and robots could allow annual growth of 30% or more, with multiple doublings in some years. Society's reluctance to automate certain work, regulation and implementation lags can keep aggregate measured growth below what is attainable in domains that remove those barriers. (§II, pp. 68–70)

### Labour market dynamics

An economically useful “drop-in remote worker” is expected to automate essentially all work that can be done remotely. The author puts global white-collar annual pay in the tens of trillions, so even a partial substitution could justify very large capital investment. Robotics later broadens feasible automation to physical work. The essay gives no path for aggregate employment, wage distribution or worker transitions. (§I; §II, pp. 68–70; §IIIa, p. 82)

### Winners and losers

Large AI developers, chipmakers, cloud providers and those controlling access to compute and electricity benefit from demand in the build-out. The author focuses on the strategic advantage of the country that controls the frontier and does not lay out a household-level distribution of gains or losses. (§§IIIa, IIId, IV)

### Household incomes and living standards

Not addressed.

### Government finances

Public spending and coordination of national resources become possible once AGI is seen as strategically decisive. The author uses historical spending on the Manhattan and Apollo projects and mobilization during World War II to frame feasibility. He does not model tax bases, receipts, debt or social insurance. (§IIIa, pp. 82–84; §IV)

### International economic patterns

US and allied positions in frontier chips and leading developers provide an initial advantage, but the author argues that China can mobilize industrial resources and remain competitive. Capital, electricity availability and regulatory conditions could draw some projected infrastructure overseas, which he sees as a strategic risk. He favours US industrial capacity and cooperation with allies. (§§IIIa, IIId, IV)

### Market pricing and scarcity

The essay identifies GPUs, electrical generation, grid connection, transformers, and data centre construction as scarce inputs during a rapid scale-up. A trend table places end-of-decade clusters at roughly 100 million accelerator equivalents and approximately 100 GW, over 20% of then-current US electricity production if realized. The author expects robots and accelerated research to relax important production bottlenecks later. (§II; §IIIa, pp. 75–88)

### Financial markets and stability

Rising revenue could make AI the chief source of growth for the largest technology companies. The author suggests a first $10 trillion company and a corporate bond sale in the hundreds of billions as possibilities. He identifies the magnitude and financing needs of the build-out without predicting a credit crisis or modeling asset returns. (§IIIa, pp. 80–84)

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

The source describes the technical ability to replace remote cognitive labour and eventually much physical labour. It does not describe sustained career paths or compensation for displaced workers. (§§I–II)

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

Not addressed.

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Strategic control concentrates in a national program as capabilities rise. Aschenbrenner argues that the US government's constitutional checks and balances are preferable to entrusting extraordinary power to private lab chiefs. He also argues that democratic countries must retain strategic leverage over authoritarian powers. (§§IIId, IV)

### A fair share of progress

The author emphasizes the transformative economic and scientific potential of superintelligence and preserving its benefits for the free world. No broad allocation of income, ownership or access is specified. (§§II, IIId)

## Other dynamics

The essay treats alignment during accelerated research, secure control of frontier systems, geopolitical instability and the possibility of severe loss of control as urgent constraints. It expects an unprecedented acceleration of scientific and engineering research and compares years of future progress to compressing a century of prior advances. (§II, pp. 68–70; §§IIIb–d, IV)
