# Tier 3: Transformative disruption (unprecedented unemployment) — extract

## Summary
Unemployment exceeds historical peaks: “unemployment at levels never before sustained alongside an economy generating record output”. This is “past the edge of the maps that policymakers and economists have historically used to navigate” (p. 12). Job searches stretch past one year, then two, and for some they stop. Savings are drawn down, rent goes unpaid, and “the link… between contributing to the economy and sharing in its rewards—is strained or broken” (p. 12). The policy challenge shifts to “sustained income replacement for a large share of the workforce”. The authors propose extending existing supports and converging them toward a common floor; building new tax bases (capital gains, consumption, AI-use levies, digital dividends) and new redistribution mechanisms (UBI, AI sovereign wealth funds, worker equity-sharing, much larger capital accounts); and expanding public investment in human- and community-facing work (pp. 12–14). The authors stress they are “less certain” here and “not yet ready to advocate specific policies” (pp. 12–13).

## Key details
- **Source:** Anthropic’s Economic Policy Framework (PDF title page; running footer “A policy framework for AI’s impact on work”), Anthropic, Anthropic, June 2026. `sources/anthropic-economic-policy-framework-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** Anthropic’s web summary page, “Policy on the AI Exponential” / “Anthropic’s Economic Policy Framework” (`private/web-summary.txt`), restates the tiers and says Tier 3 views will be developed through The Anthropic Institute. This is a separate publication from The Anthropic Institute’s “Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI” (Korinek, Jones, Sacher, Cotter and McCrory, Working Paper No. 2026-02, September 2026), which is already on the map as `anthropic-modest`, `anthropic-substantial` and `anthropic-extreme`. The two use different scenario sets: the Institute paper uses modelled capability and adoption paths, while this framework uses policy tiers set by unemployment level. *[Inference: the 5% / 10% / unprecedented tiers do not map one-to-one onto Modest / Substantial / Extreme, and neither source says they do.]*
- **Reading notes:** Full text of the 14-page PDF (pp. 1–14) and the web summary page. Page locators use the PDF’s printed page numbers; p. 1 is the cover page. Page 7 says measures that could entrench the link between income and employment are flagged “below”, but no explicit flags appear in the extracted text. *[Inference: any flag may have been a visual marker lost in extraction, or it may be the convergence-to-a-common-floor discussion on p. 12.]*
- **Scenario set:** Three tiers, set by “the severity of AI-driven labor market disruption, using the unemployment rate as the primary trigger” (p. 6): Tier 1, Baseline economy (~5% unemployment with churn); Tier 2, Recession-level disruption (~10% unemployment); Tier 3, Transformative disruption (unemployment exceeding historical peaks) (pp. 7, 10, 12). A second dimension runs across the tiers without defining separate scenarios: whether disruption is “a temporary shock” or “an enduring restructuring, in which the demand for human labor is significantly and persistently lower” (p. 7). Tier 3 is where “more difficult, longer-term questions around what happens if the link between work and income breaks are addressed” (p. 4).
- **Geography:** United States. The authors say “the principles underpinning this framework are intended to be global” (p. 4).
- **Authors' framing:** Prescription and planning scenario, with an exploratory research agenda for this tier: “What follows is our current assessment of the directions most worth exploring” (p. 12). Tier 3 falls within “the harder questions where research and dialogue should inform decisions” (p. 3). The web summary calls it “novel economic territory”.
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned. “We cannot know today which scenario will unfold” (p. 6). The web summary says that if AI delivers “even a fraction of its potential… it’s likely that AI will have acted as a general substitute for labor”, with a reduction in demand for human work “difficult or impossible for many workers to absorb” (web summary).

### Timeline
No horizon year is given.
- Recent years — entry-level workers in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen weaker employment growth (p. 1).
- June 2026 — framework published. Recommendations are calibrated to capabilities “that exist today or will arrive within roughly the next year” (p. 3).
- Entry to this period — “the US may enter this period with elevated deficits and recent inflationary experience, limiting fiscal headroom” (pp. 12–13).
- Within the tier — job searches stretch “past a year, then past two, and for some, eventually stops”. Savings are drawn down, and rent “is paid late, then not at all” (p. 12).
- As conditions move toward Tier 3 — some Tier 1 and Tier 2 tools are phased out (p. 7). As UI extensions lengthen, benefits converge toward a common floor (p. 12).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** On. The tier is defined by the need for sustained, structural income replacement and new public revenue and redistribution mechanisms, though the specific instruments are left open.
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The tiers set out what governments should do, not what they do inside a story. The baseline context includes the existing UI and Extended Benefits system (p. 11), birth-seeded capital accounts (p. 8), and a tax system where “effective tax rates on labor substantially exceed those on capital” (p. 13).
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - *This tier:*
    - **Sustain income through existing systems** (p. 12). Automatically extend UI duration as conditions deteriorate. Enhance wage insurance “with higher caps and longer duration”. Expand basic needs relief “with broader eligibility, covering both underemployed workers and those who never qualified for traditional UI”. As extensions lengthen, “benefit levels should converge toward a common floor rather than continuing to track prior wages indefinitely”. A common floor “creates a natural on-ramp to the mechanisms below”.
    - **Fiscal approach** (pp. 12–13). Deficit-financed income support is “textbook countercyclical policy” for a rapid rise in unemployment. If displacement is structural alongside a growing economy, policies must be “likewise structural and fiscally sustainable”. If displacement coincides with continued inflation or fiscal crisis, the toolkit should prioritise “reforms that don’t require new spending, such as regulatory adjustments, private insurance mandates, or reallocation within existing budgets”.
    - **Establish new tax bases and redistribution mechanisms** (p. 13). “We are not yet ready to advocate specific policies for this scenario.” Candidates the authors name and say they will research:
      - *Revenue:* increasing the capital gains tax; broad-based consumption taxes; sector-specific levies on AI use “(measured by tokens, compute, or revenue)”; and scalable “digital dividends” funded by taxes on the digital sector.
      - *Redistribution:* universal basic income; AI sovereign wealth funds funded by investment stakes in AI-driven productivity; equity-sharing mechanisms giving workers partial ownership in AI enterprises; and “dramatically expanded pre-distributive capital accounts building on existing models”.
      - Trade-off: “broad-based approaches offer simplicity but may lack precision, while displacement-indexed mechanisms are more targeted but harder to administer” (p. 14).
    - **Substantially expand public investment in human- and community-facing work** (pp. 13–14). Direct part of any AI-driven surplus to roles that may resist AI substitution and face persistent shortages, such as teaching and health. This “closes staffing gaps” and “keeps paid work available for those who want it”. The authors note the second rationale rests on a value judgement, but say “the case here does not depend on it”.
    - **Anthropic’s commitment** (p. 14). “Regardless of the tax base or distribution mechanism: we are ready and willing to pay our fair share.” “If AI companies generate transformative returns, they have an obligation to ensure those returns are broadly shared.”
  - *Carried from earlier tiers:* UI extension (Tier 2), wage insurance (Tier 1) and basic needs relief (Tier 2) are broadened and deepened (p. 12). Capital accounts (Tier 1) are “dramatically expanded” as a candidate mechanism (p. 13). Some Tier 1 and Tier 2 tools are expected to be phased out because they may entrench “the link between income and employment that a Tier 3 world may need to loosen” (p. 7). The source does not say which. *[Inference: wage-linked benefits are the likely target, given the move away from benefits that “track prior wages” (p. 12).]* Pace-of-rollout management is introduced at Tier 2 levels (p. 4). The source does not say whether it continues in Tier 3.
  - *Applies across the set (foundational measures, “valuable no matter what happens”, web summary; pp. 5–6):*
    - **Measurement.** Expanded investment in statistical agencies; AI-specific survey instruments; and reporting requirements for AI labs and firms, “including Anthropic”, on deployment patterns and workforce effects (p. 5).
    - **A government unit focused on tracking AI’s effects**, modelled on the Council of Economic Advisers (p. 5).
    - **Delivery infrastructure.** Modernised UI; rails to expand coverage quickly; and investment in “revenue collection and the underlying digital public infrastructure—identity, payments, data exchange” (pp. 5–6).
    - **Broader signals.** Labour force participation, underemployment, wages and labour’s share of national income (p. 6).
    - **Firm conduct.** Firms should build training into deployment, avoid defaulting to headcount cuts, retrain and redeploy (p. 3). Voluntary action is no substitute for government standards (web summary).

## Key dynamics explored
*This tier*
- Transformative productivity gains can let the aggregate economy “grow substantially alongside sustained technological displacement”. Unemployment then becomes “more structural than cyclical” and needs structural, fiscally sustainable responses rather than only deficit-financed countercyclical support (p. 12).
- Rapid AI advance can produce “a structural decoupling of productive contribution from income” (p. 13).
- Effective tax rates on labour exceed those on capital. A shift of national income from labour to capital therefore means “the current tax system captures a shrinking share of a growing economy, even as the cost of sustained income support rises” (p. 13).
- Historically, rapid productivity growth expanded the tax base and improved fiscal positions. But elevated deficits and recent inflation could limit headroom, and displacement combined with inflation or fiscal crisis would require spending-neutral tools (pp. 12–13).
- Paying benefits in proportion to past wages is “harder to rationalize on fairness or fiscal grounds” when displacement lasts years. Converging to a common floor eases the transition toward universal mechanisms (p. 12).
- Human- and community-facing roles may resist substitution and face shortages, so funding them from AI surplus both fills staffing gaps and preserves paid work (pp. 13–14).
- If most income no longer comes from work, society’s responses “will necessarily rely on help we do not yet have, including from increasingly capable AI systems themselves” (p. 14).

*Applies across the set*
- “As disruption increases, the balance shifts from adaptability toward income support and redistribution” (p. 7).
- An enduring restructuring calls for more permanent responses than a temporary shock (p. 7).
- If AI becomes a general substitute, retraining, matching and mobility policy “may fail to deliver a stable, full-employment equilibrium”. Rollout may then need to be steered, uniformly across firms (p. 4).

## State of technology
*This tier:* “If AI capabilities advance rapidly and broadly” (p. 13), AI delivers “transformative productivity gains” (p. 12). AI use is measurable in tokens, compute or revenue for levy purposes (p. 13). “Increasingly capable AI systems” may help design society’s responses (p. 14). Market structure is not addressed, beyond “AI companies” that may “generate transformative returns” (p. 14) and “AI enterprises” in which workers could hold equity (p. 13).

*Applies across the set:* Recommendations are calibrated to capabilities “that exist today or will arrive within roughly the next year” (p. 3). AI “may become a general substitute for human labor” (p. 1). Physical robotics, open versus closed models, compute and energy constraints, and pricing are not addressed.

## State of adoption
*This tier:* Not addressed. *[Inference: unemployment exceeding historical peaks implies broad adoption, but the source does not describe it.]*

*Applies across the set:* “We’re still early in AI’s adoption” (p. 1). Claude is used more in the US than in any other country (p. 4). Pace and diffusion are uncertain (web summary).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
*This tier:* The economy is “generating record output” (p. 12). The aggregate economy “may grow substantially alongside sustained technological displacement” if AI delivers transformative productivity gains (p. 12). An “AI-driven surplus” exists that can be directed to public investment (p. 13).
*Applies across the set:* AI could generate growth “on a scale the modern world hasn’t seen” (p. 1). Economists’ scenarios include output doubling while wages collapse (p. 1).

### Labour market dynamics
*This tier:* Unemployment exceeds historical peaks, “at levels never before sustained” (p. 12). Job searches stretch past one year, then two, and for some they stop (p. 12). The policy target is income replacement “for a large share of the workforce” (p. 12). Underemployed workers and those who never qualified for UI need coverage (p. 12). Labour conditions are “without historical precedent—not merely high unemployment, but a structural decoupling of productive contribution from income” (p. 13). National income shifts from labour toward capital (p. 13). Human- and community-facing roles may prove more resistant to substitution and face persistent shortages, for example teachers and health workers (p. 13 and footnote 30). The web summary says “people work far less” may be a longer-horizon outcome.

### Winners and losers
*This tier:* Capital gains relative to labour as national income shifts toward capital (p. 13). AI companies may “generate transformative returns” (p. 14). Losers are the long-term unemployed, who exhaust savings and fall behind on rent (p. 12).
*Applies across the set:* Gains could concentrate narrowly or be broadly shared (web summary).

### Household incomes and living standards
*This tier:* Savings “built over a working life are drawn down; rent is paid late, then not at all” (p. 12). Policy shifts to sustained income replacement, with benefits converging to a common floor (p. 12). There is a possible “economy in which most income no longer comes from work” (p. 14).
*Applies across the set:* The goal is “a society where people can live well” (web summary).

### Government finances
*This tier:*
- Costs of sustained income support rise while the tax system captures a shrinking share of a growing economy, because labour is taxed more heavily than capital (p. 13).
- “We will need new revenue sources. Traditional safety net tools may not be sufficient” (p. 13).
- Elevated deficits and recent inflation may limit fiscal headroom on entry (pp. 12–13).
- If there is inflation or fiscal crisis, the response should use regulatory adjustments, private insurance mandates and reallocation within existing budgets (p. 13).
- Candidate revenue sources are listed under Policy stance (p. 13).
- Wage-proportional benefits over years are hard to justify on fiscal grounds (p. 12).

### International economic patterns
*This tier:* Not addressed.
*Applies across the set:* Only 52% of the world’s population is covered by any social protection scheme (p. 6). The principles are intended to be global (p. 4).

### Market pricing and scarcity
*This tier:* Continued inflation is possible alongside displacement (p. 13). Persistent workforce shortages remain in human- and community-facing work (p. 13). Asset prices are not addressed.
*Applies across the set:* Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability
*This tier:* Fiscal crisis is named as a possible accompanying condition (p. 13). Otherwise not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
*This tier:* “The link Americans have long taken for granted—between contributing to the economy and sharing in its rewards—is strained or broken” (p. 12). There is a “structural decoupling of productive contribution from income” (p. 13).
*Applies across the set:* “There is dignity in work” (p. 2).

### Affordable essentials
*This tier:* Rent “is paid late, then not at all” (p. 12). Expanded basic needs relief extends coverage (p. 12). Basic needs relief is defined in Tier 2 as covering food, housing and healthcare (p. 11).

### Reliable safety net
*This tier:* Existing systems are broadened (longer UI, enhanced wage insurance, broader basic needs relief) and converge toward a common floor (p. 12). “Traditional safety net tools may not be sufficient” (p. 13). Candidates include UBI and other universal mechanisms (p. 13).
*Applies across the set:* UI “is not sufficiently prepared for a large labor market shock” (pp. 5–6).

### Time for what matters
*This tier:* Public investment in human- and community-facing work “keeps paid work available for those who want it”. The authors acknowledge that the view “that work confers something income alone does not” is a value judgement “which not everyone shares and which future generations may weigh differently” (pp. 13–14).
*Applies across the set:* “Over a longer horizon, AI may move society toward a world in which people work far less, or in which work carries a different meaning than it does today. Changes of that scale would reach beyond labor policy into how society is organized” (p. 2).

### A say in your future
*This tier:* The authors will develop thinking “in partnership with The Anthropic Institute, policymakers, and external researchers” (p. 12). “We have significant agency to shape how the future unfolds” (p. 14).
*Applies across the set:* Workers, unions, small business owners and worker organisations are to be engaged directly (web summary).

### A fair share of progress
*This tier:* Mechanisms include AI sovereign wealth funds, worker equity in AI enterprises, UBI and dramatically expanded capital accounts (p. 13). “If AI companies generate transformative returns, they have an obligation to ensure those returns are broadly shared”, and Anthropic is “ready and willing to pay our fair share” (p. 14).
*Applies across the set:* The central challenge is “making sure the gains are widely shared” (p. 1).

## Other dynamics
*This tier:* Increasingly capable AI systems may help society design its responses (p. 14).
*Applies across the set:*
- The framework is published alongside Anthropic’s Advanced AI Framework (web summary).
- Anthropic announces $350 million in linked investments: a $200 million Economic Futures Research Fund and a $150 million national fellowship programme (web summary).
- Values: “We are not seeking job displacement. We are working to prevent or minimize it” and “Whatever happens, we are on the side of people” (p. 2).
- The framework is “necessarily incomplete” (web summary).
- Safety, geopolitics, culture, health, environment and the information ecosystem are not addressed in this document.
