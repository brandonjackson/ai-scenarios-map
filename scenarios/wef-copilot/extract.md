# Co-Pilot Economy — extract

## Summary
After a mid-2020s AI valuation bust, businesses adopt AI pragmatically to augment workers. AI skills become widespread and many standardized tasks shrink, while human–AI teams reshape value chains. Productivity rises and labour mobility improves, though job quality and access vary and jurisdictional rules diverge (pp. 11–12).

## Key details
- **Source:** *Four Futures for Jobs in the New Economy: AI and Talent in 2030*, World Economic Forum, 7 January 2026. `sources/wef-four-futures-jobs-2030/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** Sam Grayling and Kateryna Karunska, two report contributors, summarize the scenario in *Here are four ways AI and talent trends could reshape jobs by 2030* (World Economic Forum, 7 January 2026).
- **Reading notes:** Full 20-page white paper, including the graphical direction-of-change indicators in Table 1. The publisher’s PDF was captured from a public mirror after its PDF host timed out.
- **Scenario set:** Supercharged Progress, The Age of Displacement, Co-Pilot Economy and Stalled Progress. The axes are incremental versus exponential AI advancement and limited versus widespread workforce readiness. This branch combines incremental advancement and widespread workforce readiness (Figure 2, p. 7).
- **Geography:** Global, with variation by country, industry and workforce (Table 1, pp. 11–12).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory scenarios built from research, workshops and discussions with chief strategy officers and subject specialists to stress-test business decisions (pp. 3, 7, 14).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probabilities are assigned. Directions in Table 1 are illustrative rather than numerical forecasts (p. 8).

### Timeline
- Mid-2020s — valuations and initial capital commitments give way to a market bust, slowing frontier commercialization (p. 11).
- By 2030 — more than 40% of skills have changed, and worker mobility is higher (p. 12).
- 2030 — scenario horizon (p. 7).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Mixed. Early training, mobility, digital infrastructure and governance help absorb AI, and regulatory responses diverge (pp. 11–12).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Standards and data privacy rules multiply. Some jurisdictions pursue interoperability, while others nationalize or restrict infrastructure and tools. Talent mobility policies range from borderless digital pipelines to protectionism (p. 12).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* The recommendations are chiefly for businesses: run controlled pilots, align talent and technology strategy, embed training in work, invest in human–AI collaboration and data governance, build mobility and talent pipelines with education providers and governments, create multigenerational teams, build trust and safeguards, and use partnerships to learn across workflows and sectors (p. 16).

## Key dynamics explored
- An AI valuation reversal prompts pragmatic task-level integration. Early concern about displacement accelerates the redesign of education and industry training (p. 11).
- Broad but shallow deployment complements workers, increases experimentation and makes production more modular. Standardized tasks are easier to automate (pp. 11–12).
- Transparency lowers risk premiums and attracts investment, while energy and infrastructure gaps reinforce divergence (p. 12).

## State of technology
Progress is gradual, and firms generally tailor AI to specific tasks. Automation density rises for highly standardized processes, with humans still in core operations and decisions (pp. 11–12).

## State of adoption
AI literacy becomes as common as basic digital literacy was in the early 2020s. More industries use AI, but wide use is shallow and task-specific, with many sectors retaining legacy workflows and redesigning incrementally (pp. 11–12).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
Labour productivity growth rises above the 1.5% early-2020s rate, and gradual gains break prolonged weak GDP growth. Margins and lower cost pressure partly stabilize inflation, even as intensified competition and tight labour markets leave volatility risks (p. 12).

### Labour market dynamics
Job churn and displacement rise, and routine administrative and basic analytical tasks are hollowed out. AI cuts the completion time of some tasks by up to 80%. Demand grows for managerial, social, problem-solving and hybrid AI/domain skills. Mobility, gig work and entrepreneurship rise. Job quality improves for people who lead AI and worsens where it contracts creativity and agency (pp. 11–12).

### Winners and losers
Workers able to adapt and firms with transparent deployment attract opportunities and capital. Limited education, infrastructure or employer support excludes some people, although raised skills floors narrow wage gaps slightly among middle- and high-skilled workers (p. 12).

### Household incomes and living standards
Remote, flexible work and entrepreneurship widen opportunities in marginalized and peripheral communities. Broader application divides workers by access and ability to adapt (p. 12).

### Government finances
Not addressed.

### International economic patterns
Infrastructure maturity and energy costs split AI-ready economies from others. Rules and talent mobility become fragmented. Heightened rivalry over AI, talent and control of critical value chains is a risk (pp. 12, 15).

### Market pricing and scarcity
Lower cost pressures stabilize inflation somewhat, though competition and tight labour sustain volatility risk. Transparent adoption frameworks attract finance at lower risk premiums (p. 12).

### Financial markets and stability
The mid-2020s burst of AI valuations cuts frontier venture funding and resets commercialization timelines. As practical augmentation spreads, confidence strengthens where deployment is transparent. Weak investor confidence and tighter finance after the bust remain risks (pp. 11–12, 15).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Mobility, reskilling, cross-domain work and entrepreneurial opportunities expand. Job quality depends on the role retained for human creativity and agency (p. 12).

### Affordable essentials
Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net
Not addressed.

### Time for what matters
Not addressed.

### A say in your future
The scenario describes keeping people in key decision-making roles in firms while disinformation raises polarization and pushes trust toward verified sources and human intermediaries (p. 12).

### A fair share of progress
Broad access lifts skills floors and widens opportunity across places, alongside widening gaps for groups constrained by weaker infrastructure, education or employer support (p. 12).

## Other dynamics
Human judgment may weaken with over-reliance, sharpening risks from model weaknesses, bias and governance gaps. The Forum recommends institutionalizing human–AI collaboration, identifying uniquely human processes and scaling worker development (p. 15).
