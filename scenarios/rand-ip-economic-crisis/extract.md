# Economic Crisis (Infinite Potential) — extract

## Summary
Between late 2026 and mid-2027, a leading U.S. AI lab achieves AGI, which speeds up an employment crisis that has already begun. The result is record GDP growth alongside mass displacement of knowledge workers, a looming debt spiral and congressional gridlock (p. 4, Table 1). China captures global manufacturing dominance through AI-powered “lights-out factories”, and Chinese export controls on critical robotics inputs block U.S. hardware automation. Social unrest and political radicalization in the United States escalate (p. 4). Players must reconcile AGI acceleration with worker protection “using economic tools that are ill-suited for the new AI economy” (p. 4). The scenario keeps players on the economic and political-economic side of the disruption (p. 3). RAND reports what players identified and recommended across this scenario and its sibling, without judging the merits or feasibility of those recommendations (p. 7).

## Key details
- **Source:** Infinite Potential—Insights on Economic and Societal Impacts Across Two Scenarios: After-Action Report from a Series of Day After Artificial General Intelligence Games (RR-A4767-4), Anton Shenk, Richard S. Girven, Gregory Smith, Tobias Sytsma, RAND Corporation, 2026. `sources/rand-infinite-potential-economic-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None. The appendix lists a separate report on the sibling Business and Civil Society scenario (RR-A4767-1), but no separate report on Economic Crisis (p. 17).
- **Reading notes:** Full text read (pp. i–20). Figure 1 (p. 6) is an image and is not in the text. This is an after-action report that combines player findings from both scenarios. It describes the Economic Crisis world only in Table 1 (p. 4), in the shared opening turn (p. 3) and in passing remarks within player discussion. It does not give separate Turn 1 and Turn 2 content for this scenario. No finding is attributed specifically to Economic Crisis games. The few findings attributed specifically to Business and Civil Society games are left out here; they appear in the sibling extract. All other player findings are reported across the set.
- **Scenario set:** Two scenarios that share an opening turn. In that turn players face an accelerating AI-driven employment shock, a frontier lab about to deploy an AGI-level model, and the People’s Republic of China pursuing AI-enabled manufacturing dominance (p. 3). From there, Economic Crisis stays on economic and political-economic dimensions. Business and Civil Society adds “a large-scale cyber and infrastructure crisis of ambiguous origin” and shifts attention to crisis response and public-private coordination (p. 3). The scenarios are not built on axes. Each is a two-turn “Day After” tabletop game: in Turn 2 the scenario advances, “usually through escalation or a major change in circumstances” (p. 3).
- **Geography:** United States, with China as strategic competitor and the Global South as a field of competition (pp. 4, 10–11). By Table 2’s count, all seven Economic Crisis games took place in the United States: Washington, D.C., Santa Monica, San Francisco and Boston (p. 5).
- **Authors’ framing:** Planning scenario for contingency planning. Each game “presents a plausible future scenario in which advances in AGI outpace governance” (p. iii). The games are meant to show “the preparations needed before such an event, the decisions likely to matter most once that event begins, and the playbooks required to navigate it successfully” (p. iii). The method is RAND’s “Day After” approach, “designed to support strategic decisionmaking under uncertainty” (p. 2).
- **Authors’ view of likelihood:** Not stated beyond calling the scenario “plausible” (pp. iii, 2). The authors note that AGI “remains hypothetical” and that “there is no consensus on what it is” (p. 1).

### Timeline
Horizon is mid-2027. Infinite Potential scenarios are generally set 12–18 months after the game date (p. 3).
- April 2025 to May 2026 — Games played. By Table 2’s count, seven of the 26 were Economic Crisis games: 10 April, 5 June, 29 July, 12 August and 29 October 2025; 16 March and 9 April 2026 (pp. iii, 5).
- Opening turn (shared with the sibling) — An AI-driven employment shock accelerates, a frontier lab is about to deploy an AGI-level model, and the PRC pursues AI-enabled manufacturing dominance (p. 3).
- Late 2026 to mid-2027 — A leading U.S. AI lab achieves AGI, which accelerates the existing employment crisis. GDP growth hits record levels while knowledge workers are displaced en masse. A debt spiral looms and Congress is gridlocked. China captures global manufacturing dominance with “lights-out factories”. Chinese export controls on critical robotics inputs block U.S. hardware automation. Domestic social unrest and political radicalization escalate (p. 4).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** On. The scenario is built for players to design government responses to AGI-driven economic disruption, and the report is organised around the capabilities, playbooks and policies they recommended (pp. iii, 2, 7).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:**
  - Players assume existing institutions, laws and international relationships stay largely intact unless told otherwise (p. 3).
  - Congress is gridlocked (p. 4).
  - The available economic tools are “ill-suited for the new AI economy” (p. 4).
  - China imposes export controls on critical robotics inputs (p. 4).
  - *Applies across the set:* Players found that, at the time of gameplay, government lacks the legal authorities and institutional capacity for forceful intervention in frontier firms (p. 13). Existing retraining programs are being outpaced (p. 7). The tax system is still built around payroll and income taxes (p. 9).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* These are player recommendations that the authors synthesise without evaluating (p. 7).
  - **Labour (pp. v, 7–8):**
    - real-time monitoring of displacement, including a federal AI labour-impact dashboard
    - identifying “AGI-safe” jobs and skills
    - large-scale emergency financial support, modelled on the COVID-19 unemployment systems but designed for a possibly permanent shift
    - Civilian Conservation Corps- and Works Progress Administration-style jobs programs in infrastructure, caregiving and national service
    - vocational investment and incentives for care, health and education roles
    - playbooks with trigger thresholds for expanded unemployment insurance, job programs and income support
    - coordination playbooks for government, industry and schools
    - a public communications playbook
  - **Fiscal (pp. v, 9–10):**
    - real-time fiscal monitoring
    - capacity to rapidly model and implement new tax regimes
    - pre-authorised emergency fiscal tools such as temporary levies
    - graduated tax-restructuring playbooks that shift from labour-based to capital-based taxation
    - international coordination of AI taxation
    - candidate taxes: capital gains reform, higher corporate taxes, and taxes on compute, AI agent outputs, digital transactions, robots and windfall profits
    - framing restructuring as a New Deal-style initiative
    - a sovereign wealth fund modelled on Norway’s fund and the Alaska Permanent Fund
  - **China (pp. v–vi, 11):**
    - intelligence on China’s AI strategy
    - trade measures coordinated with allies: tariffs on goods from Chinese AI manufacturing, semiconductor export controls, alignment incentives
    - playbooks on the trade-off between acceleration and protection
    - playbooks for economic engagement and escalation ladders
    - a mutual-restraint framework modelled on nuclear arms control
    - alliance-building playbooks
  - **Frontier firms (pp. vi, 12–13):**
    - independent government capacity to evaluate frontier AI
    - monitoring of market structure and wealth
    - stronger authorities: compel disclosure, subpoena employees, nationalise labs in extremis, designate frontier AI as critical infrastructure
    - profit-sharing, minimum human employment ratios, public-private return sharing
    - graduated relationship playbooks
    - leverage over firms through energy, land, contracts, chips and antitrust
    - contingency plans for corporate resistance
  - **Work and welfare (pp. vi, 14–15):**
    - pilots of reduced work weeks, UBI, national service and care-economy expansion
    - welfare metrics beyond employment and GDP
    - education reform and lifelong learning
    - transition playbooks for stabilisation, then adaptation, then transformation
    - an honest but hopeful communications playbook
    - open, participatory deliberation begun before a crisis
  - **Authors’ next steps for the platform (pp. 15–16):** Probe sequencing and trade-off questions more deeply. Design games that force hard prioritisation.

## Key dynamics explored
- AGI achieved at a U.S. lab speeds up an existing employment crisis. Record GDP growth arrives alongside mass displacement of knowledge workers, because productivity gains “come at the expense of widespread job loss” (p. 4).
- China’s AI-powered lights-out factories win global manufacturing dominance. Chinese export controls on robotics inputs block U.S. hardware automation (p. 4).
- Displacement, a looming debt spiral and gridlock lead to social unrest and political radicalization in the United States (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set (player findings):*
  - **Speed of displacement.** Displacement moves faster than past automation. Whole categories of cognitive work, such as entry-level knowledge work, legal research, software engineering and financial analysis, could be displaced rapidly after a frontier model is deployed (p. 7).
  - **Lost entry rungs.** AI removes the bottom rungs of career ladders, leaving a generation without clear pathways into professional life. Players saw this as “a recipe for political radicalization and social disintegration” (p. 7).
  - **Robotics limits the trades fix.** In the scenario, displacement moves from white-collar to blue-collar jobs via robotics. That makes redirecting workers into trades “at best a temporary solution” (p. 7).
  - **Retraining under uncertainty.** Not knowing which jobs and skills will stay viable makes retraining hard to target (p. 7).
  - **Fiscal squeeze.** A vicious fiscal dynamic forms: unemployment cuts income-tax revenue and raises demand for spending, while gains flow to capital owners and a few technology firms. A tax system built on payroll and income is misaligned with productivity growth that is decoupled from employment (p. 9).
  - **Tax policy is political.** The firms gaining most from AGI also have the greatest political influence and the most sophisticated tax avoidance. Without proactive restructuring, government may be unable to fund even basic services (p. 10).
  - **Capital mobility.** Unilateral taxes on AI firms risk capital flight or relocation (p. 9).
  - **China dilemma.** Competition with China pushes the United States to “go faster”, while domestic unrest calls for it to “slow down”. Players called this an “unresolvable tension”. It persists even when the United States adds friction to China’s progress (pp. 10–11).
  - **K-shaped economy.** Concentrated frontier-firm power produces a K-shaped economy: GDP grows while employment and consumer spending fall. Players warned that this inequality is unsustainable and threatens social, economic and political stability (p. 12).
  - **Self-reporting as power.** Government depends on lab self-reporting to know what frontier systems can do. If firms control both the technology and government’s understanding of it, they effectively set the terms of oversight (p. 12).
  - **Not a normal recession.** Players first treated the scenario as a conventional recession. Later they concluded that displacement may be permanent rather than cyclical (p. 14).
  - **Labour-based ideologies.** Existing political ideologies, market-liberal and social-democratic alike, assume labour distributes income and purpose. If that assumption fails, they may fail too (p. 15).
  - **Closing window.** Once mass displacement produces acute instability, the room for inclusive institutional design shrinks, and responses become “reactive, fragmented, and potentially authoritarian” (p. 15).

## State of technology
- A leading U.S. AI lab achieves AGI between late 2026 and mid-2027 (p. 4). In the shared opening turn, a frontier lab is about to deploy an AGI-level model (p. 3).
- The report notes contested definitions of AGI: either “able to perform every intellectual task that a human can perform at the human level or better” or the ability to “rapidly reach superhuman performance” (p. 1).
- **Physical:** China runs AI-powered “lights-out factories”. U.S. hardware automation is held back by Chinese export controls on critical robotics inputs (p. 4). *Applies across the set:* the scenario’s displacement moves to blue-collar jobs via robotics (p. 7).
- **Market structure (applies across the set):** Frontier AI research is concentrated in a handful of private firms. Players described scenarios in which one firm or a few firms control “the most economically consequential technology in human history” (p. 12). The firms are “hyper-profitable” (p. 13). Their power rests on energy access, land use, government contracts, semiconductor supply chains and antitrust exposure (p. 13).
- Open versus closed models, compute and energy constraints, and pricing: Not addressed.

## State of adoption
- “Large-scale integration of advanced AI into the economy” (p. 3).
- AGI drives mass displacement of U.S. knowledge workers (p. 4).
- China moves from developing frontier models to deploying AI-enabled manufacturing at scale (pp. 4, 10). China is also deploying AI systems in Global South economies (p. 11).
- U.S. hardware automation is blocked by export controls on robotics inputs (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set:* Rapid AI deployment is outpacing worker retraining programs (p. 7). Players described rapid adoption at home as fuelling unemployment and unrest, which makes it politically hard to go faster (p. 10).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
Record GDP growth alongside mass job loss. Productivity advances “come at the expense of widespread job loss” (p. 4). *Applies across the set:* players said productivity growth is “decoupled from employment growth” (p. 9). In several games, players observed that GDP growth continued while employment and consumer spending fell (p. 12).

### Labour market dynamics
- Mass knowledge-worker displacement, which accelerates an existing employment crisis (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - Entry-level knowledge work, legal research, software engineering and financial analysis are at risk of rapid displacement (p. 7).
  - Junior and early-career workers face the greatest risk (p. 7).
  - Displacement moves from white-collar to blue-collar work via robotics (p. 7).
  - Roles that need empathy, emotional intelligence and human interaction were proposed as targets for employment: caregiving, health care and education (pp. 7–8).
  - Players questioned whether displacement is cyclical or permanent (p. 14).
  - Wages and labour share: Not addressed.

### Winners and losers
- China captures manufacturing dominance (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - Gains flow disproportionately to capital owners and a few technology firms (p. 9).
  - K-shaped economy: “prosperity for a few but leaves millions unemployed and struggling” (p. 12).
  - Junior workers lose most (p. 7).
  - Some players wondered whether the United States bears AGI’s social costs while China captures its economic benefits through manufacturing (p. 11).

### Household incomes and living standards
*Applies across the set:* Consumer spending declines (p. 12). Millions are left “unemployed and struggling” (p. 12). Otherwise Not addressed.

### Government finances
A debt spiral looms (p. 4). *Applies across the set:*
- Income-tax revenue falls while demand for spending rises (p. 9).
- In at least four Business and Civil Society games, players asked when government would “run out of money” (p. 9).
- Government might become unable to fund basic services, let alone an expanded safety net (p. 10).
- Tax reform takes years while the economic change takes months (p. 9).

### International economic patterns
- China gains global manufacturing dominance through lights-out factories (p. 4).
- China’s export controls on critical robotics inputs block U.S. hardware automation (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - China’s AI-enabled manufacturing threatens dominance over industrial production, supply chains and economic influence in the Global South (p. 10).
  - Risk of capital flight from unilateral AI taxation (p. 9).
  - Players discussed allies shifting toward China’s technology ecosystems (p. 11).

### Market pricing and scarcity
*Applies across the set:* UBI skeptics argued that it fuels inflation (p. 14). Players asked “What are the signposts of approaching a post-scarcity world?” (p. 14). Otherwise Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability
*Applies across the set:* Players warned that concentrated private economic power could destabilise markets (p. 12). Otherwise Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
*Applies across the set:* AI could remove “the bottom rungs of career ladders, resulting in a generation without clear pathways into professional life” (p. 7). Players noted that existing ideologies assume labour is the main mechanism for distributing income and providing purpose (p. 15).

### Affordable essentials
*Applies across the set:* Players proposed tracking “access to basic needs” as a welfare metric (p. 14). Otherwise Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net
*Applies across the set:* Players warned that government might be unable to fund basic services, let alone the expanded safety net that mass displacement requires (p. 10). They proposed large-scale emergency financial support (p. 8). They debated UBI: proponents called it “the only mechanism capable of maintaining social stability at scale”; skeptics said it creates dependency, fuels inflation, is fiscally unsustainable and fails to address the psychological and social functions of work (p. 14).

### Time for what matters
*Applies across the set:* Players explored cutting the work week from 40 hours to 20 or fewer. They proposed national service, civic engagement and care-economy expansion to provide purpose, and asked “How can people find meaning in a post-labor society?” (p. 14).

### A say in your future
- Congressional gridlock; political radicalization and social unrest escalate (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - Concentrated firm power risks “capturing or undermining democratic governance” (p. 12).
  - Firms could use economic power to capture political processes (p. 13).
  - Once instability is acute, responses may become “potentially authoritarian” (p. 15).
  - Players urged open, participatory deliberation before a crisis (p. 15).

### A fair share of progress
*Applies across the set:* Gains accrue “mainly to firms and their owners and not the broader society” (p. 12). Players proposed sovereign wealth funds, profit-sharing and other ways to distribute AGI gains broadly (pp. 10, 13).

## Other dynamics
- **Geopolitics:** U.S.–China competition shapes the scenario (pp. 4, 10). *Applies across the set:* players debated confrontation versus cooperation with China, and some argued both countries face similar workforce disruption (p. 11). Players were frustrated by poor visibility into China’s intentions (p. 11). They proposed arms-control-style mutual restraint with verification (p. 11).
- **Social cohesion and mental health (applies across the set):** Players proposed new metrics for sense of purpose, social cohesion, mental health and civic engagement (p. 14).
- **Corporate power (applies across the set):** Players framed concentrated firm power as a new form of power concentration that antitrust was not designed for (p. 12). Some saw nationalisation as a way to halt dangerous capability growth and to stop firm leaders from subverting state authority (p. 13). Firms might move offshore or deploy AI in ways that undermine governance (p. 13).
- **Education (applies across the set):** Players proposed reorienting schooling toward critical thinking, reasoning, collaboration and project management, plus AI literacy and lifelong learning (p. 15).
- Safety and loss of control, environment, information ecosystem: Not addressed for this scenario.
