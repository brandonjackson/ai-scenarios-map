# AI Disrupts the Workforce — extract

## Summary

By 2030, relatively narrow AI systems provide effective automation across cognitive and physical work (§5.5, opening and “Capability”). Data-centre shortages and price spikes encourage narrow lower-compute tools, while persistent inflation makes deploying them cheaper relative to labour (§5.5, “Key turning points in 2025”). Productivity rises and goods become cheaper, but unemployment, falling wages and poverty increase (§5.5, “wider context in 2030”). Protest and debate follow as economic harm mounts even though users trust systems to operate as intended (§5.5, opening and “Safety”).

## Key details

- **Source:** AI 2030 Scenarios: Helping policy makers plan for the future of AI (Annex C), Government Office for Science, Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (GOV.UK), 2025-04-28. `sources/go-science-ai-2030-scenarios-annex-c-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None.
- **Reading notes:** Full GOV.UK HTML annex. The scenarios were developed in 2023 for the November AI Safety Summit, the report is dated January 2024 on its cover, and the annex was published on GOV.UK on 28 April 2025 (§1.2; GOV.UK parent publication change history).
- **Scenario set:** Unpredictable Advanced AI, AI Disrupts the Workforce, AI “Wild West”, Advanced AI on a Knife Edge, and AI Disappoints. The five uncertainties are capability, ownership/access/constraints, safety, level and distribution of use, and geopolitical context (§§1.3–1.4).
- **Geography:** Global developments, with implications for the UK (§1.2).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory policy-planning scenario. “Scenarios are a tool to help policy makers explore uncertainty and identify a resilient course of action that can withstand a range of future conditions” (§1.2).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated (§§1.2 and 3.1).

### Timeline

- Early 2020s — foundation models improve and GPU supply falls short of demand (§5.5, “Capability” and “wider context in 2030”).
- 2025 — scarce data centre capacity, price spikes and high labour costs shift vendor incentives to narrow models and company incentives to substitution (§5.5, “Key turning points in 2025”).
- By 2030 — semiconductor supply has largely caught up and initial onshoring progresses. AI is widely deployed, including in robots and autonomous vehicles, with labour disruption concentrated in exposed sectors (§5.5, “Capability”, “Geopolitical Context” and “wider context in 2030”).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. New government interventions are excluded for stress testing (§1.2).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Existing regulation limits use in sectors such as construction, though businesses ask for changes when other countries change their rules. Western countries work to onshore advanced semiconductor production (§5.5, “Level and distribution of use” and “Geopolitical Context”).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Include systemic economic and societal effects and narrow models in policy planning. Consider worker rights, inequality, taxation, retraining, skills and the balance between human and machine labour under permanent or transitional job losses. Weigh the productivity costs of slowing adoption against the harm this might prevent. Establish international cooperation before economic rivalry makes it harder (§5.5, “Key policy considerations”).

## Key dynamics explored

- Data-centre constraints and higher returns on easier-to-deploy domain-specific products steer vendors towards narrow models (§5.5, “Ownership, constraints, and access” and “Key turning points in 2025”).
- Persistent inflation keeps labour expensive relative to AI. Firms replace labour to capture productivity benefits and cut costs (§5.5, “Level and distribution of use” and “Key turning points in 2025”).
- Trust in technical reliability encourages rapid integration, but inadequate bias controls harm people subject to automated decisions (§5.5, “Safety”).
- Physical AI deployment supplies new real-world training data, improving systems further (§5.5, “Capability”).

*Applies across the set.* The report identifies AI skills, international cooperation and sustained attention to AI harms as considerations across the set. Five narratives vary those conditions to let policymakers compare their responses against differing plausible futures (§§1.2–1.3, 5.1 and 7.1).

## State of technology

Tech giants fine-tune foundation models for narrow tasks, scale compute and data, and acquire or synthesise specialised datasets. Robots and autonomous vehicles improve physical capabilities. Delays to data-centre construction encourage lower-compute specialisation. Smaller developers cannot afford comparable compute or data. Tools are intuitive but accessible through controlled interfaces (§5.5, “Capability” and “Ownership, constraints, and access”).

## State of adoption

High in large firms, start-ups and among higher-skilled people; medium among other SMEs; low in the public sector and among lower-skilled people. Both UK and rest-of-world usage are medium-high, as is both cognitive and physical automation. IT, accounting, taxis and freight lead. Agriculture accelerates existing automation, construction is constrained by regulation, and public-sector unions limit substitution while enabling a shift towards tasks human input improves. Consultancies switch towards AI implementation, while businesses without legacy systems leapfrog incumbents (§5.5, “Level and distribution of use” and “Frontier AI Adoption in 2030”).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Productivity starts rising significantly as sectors automate, with improved efficiency for many firms. Protests and industrial action cause disruptions. The authors identify productivity-driven growth as an opportunity (§5.5, “wider context in 2030” and “Key policy considerations”).

### Labour market dynamics

There are net employment losses, significant wage declines and greater wage inequality. Repetitive cognitive work such as accounting is heavily exposed, as are physical jobs in agriculture and transport. Some skilled AI users perform the work of several people. Hospitality and entertainment become attractive, especially to young people; specialist AI managers gain. Less human oversight means fewer posts in affected sectors, while industrial action is less effective where work is heavily automated (§5.5, “Level and distribution of use”, “Frontier AI Adoption in 2030” and “wider context in 2030”).

### Winners and losers

AI developers, deploying businesses, those who own and train systems, and workers with model oversight skills gain. Those in roles replaced by automation lose jobs or wages. Some newcomers overtake legacy businesses, while smaller AI developers struggle to compete (§5.5, “Ownership, constraints, and access”, “Level and distribution of use”, “wider context in 2030” and “Key policy considerations”).

### Household incomes and living standards

Despite lower prices from higher productivity, unemployment, declining wages and poverty increase and social cohesion erodes (§5.5, “wider context in 2030”).

### Government finances

Growth-driven higher tax revenues and improved public finances are identified as an opportunity. The authors ask how structurally unemployed workers would be helped, but give no specified welfare outlay or realised budget effect (§5.5, “Level and distribution of use” and “Key policy considerations”).

### International economic patterns

Fierce economic and technology competition limits cooperation on AI and climate. Western semiconductor onshoring reduces interdependence between blocs and raises conflict risk (§5.5, “Geopolitical Context”).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Productivity makes consumer goods and services cheaper; manufacturing and construction automation also reduces green technology and infrastructure costs. Data-centre prices spike in 2025, and rising compute costs encourage efficiency measures by firms (§5.5, “Key turning points in 2025” and “wider context in 2030”).

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Net employment and wage losses coexist with gains for people who run, oversee or fine-tune AI systems. The report explicitly raises the prospect of a permanent net reduction in jobs (§5.5, “wider context in 2030” and “Key policy considerations”).

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

The report asks what policy should help structurally unemployed workers and suggests considering retraining, taxation and workers’ rights. It does not specify a change in support inside the scenario (§5.5, “Level and distribution of use” and “Key policy considerations”).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Public protests and strikes follow rising unemployment and poverty, but highly automated sectors limit the effect of industrial action. Concerns grow over public surveillance and biased recruitment decisions (§5.5, “Level and distribution of use” and “wider context in 2030”).

### A fair share of progress

Cheaper goods benefit consumers, but income gains to owners, developers and deployers coexist with lower wages and greater inequality (§5.5, “wider context in 2030” and “Key policy considerations”).

## Other dynamics

Technical reliability reduces the salience of existential risk, while biased decisions and economic impacts remain. Some commentators worry that outputs become less creative and unique as human training data runs short. AI’s environmental footprint rises, partially offset by cheaper green infrastructure and eventual work on computational efficiency (§5.5, “Safety”, “Level and distribution of use” and “wider context in 2030”).
