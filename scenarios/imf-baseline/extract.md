# Baseline Diffusion — extract

## Summary
AI and robot capabilities become very powerful — assumed to reach human expert level within five years and, by the early 2030s, to be technically able to perform most cognitive and physical tasks — but "society-wide adoption and diffusion across sectors and countries remain uneven," held back by societal pushback, restrictive AI regulation, strong job protection, occupational licensing, costly human-supervision requirements, infrastructure bottlenecks and skills gaps (pp. 13, 25–26). Data-centre expansion continues through 2026, but in 2027 financial-market expectations shift as AI returns "underwhelmed," and AI-intensive firms that financed data centres with debt and equity "faced a reckoning" (p. 26). Under unchanged policies, growth gains are weaker than in the runaway scenario and diverge sharply across industries; older workers are shielded by licensing, unions and political protection while younger, less-experienced workers face higher displacement risk and labour force participation declines (p. 14). The authors judge that slow, uneven adoption "gradually raises productivity while remaining broadly compatible with current fiscal, monetary, and financial frameworks," while still producing "gradual but persistent pressures on fiscal space and policy transmission" (pp. 15, 19).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Mixed — governments actively restrain AI inside the story (restrictive regulation, strong job protection, licensing), but fiscal and monetary policy stay within existing frameworks and the first discussion round assumed "no policy changes or government interventions" (pp. 13, 25).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:**
  - "Many countries adopt restrictive AI-related regulations that increase legal risks and compliance costs, and they enforce strong job protection programs" (p. 25).
  - "Strict occupational and licensing policies, as well as costly human supervision requirements" in services (p. 25).
  - Robot diffusion in mining, construction and manufacturing "hindered by strict regulations such as safety standards and tight job protection requirements" (pp. 25–26).
  - "Integration of AI into fiscal and monetary policy operations is guarded. The adoption of AI technologies in the financial sector is constrained by regulatory requirements" (p. 26).
  - "Fiscal and monetary policies were assumed to operate under existing frameworks" (p. 25). The "Status Quo" session assumed no policy changes; the "Governments Respond" session let participants assume policy changes "that they perceive as likely or important given the details and assumptions of the scenario" (p. 25). The note does not report Governments Respond results separately for this scenario.
  - Country-specific labour market institutions, licensing requirements, labour unions and political protection slow adjustment (p. 14); energy regulations and cultural attitudes shape cross-border allocation (p. 15).
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - *This scenario:*
    - In both scenarios, governments "might need to facilitate AI adoption by financially constrained small and medium enterprises and state-owned enterprises through access to shared infrastructure or public good AI models" (p. 17).
    - In both scenarios, governments "will need to balance between public interest and private power by developing 'public-good' capabilities and regulatory bodies to oversee AI deployment" (p. 18).
  - *Applies across the set:*
    - Four headline areas: "(1) strengthening social spending and tax systems; (2) upgrading monetary diagnostics and scenario tools under noisier price signals and uncertainty about the neutral rate; (3) expanding supervisory technology and macroprudential monitoring to new transition risks; and (4) reinforcing competition, diffusion support, and international coordination on standards and taxation" (p. 2; restated p. 16).
    - Figure 8 (p. 16). Fiscal: erosion of labour tax bases; rising spending in social welfare and cyber security; de-linking safety nets from employment; shift toward capital and rent taxation; targeted public investment in AI infrastructure. Monetary: noisier inflation signals; uncertainty about stance and transmission; greater reliance on scenario analysis; new tools for a digitalised economy and finance. Financial stability: AI-linked investment booms and leverage cycles; concentration, interconnectedness and solvency risks; expanded regulatory and supervisory perimeter; forward-looking, scenario-based risk management; enhanced monitoring of AI exposures; expanded scope for lender of last resort. Structural and international: competition and governance of AI services; skills upgrading and labour reallocation; public-good AI capabilities; international cooperation on standards and taxation.
    - Tax: rebalance "toward better capturing economic rents while minimizing distortions on investment decisions, supported by a stronger AI-enabled tax administration"; "Avoiding blunt 'robot taxes,' strengthening capital income taxation at the individual level with safeguards against base erosion, and leveraging AI to improve compliance and enforcement are important across scenarios" (p. 16).
    - Social protection: transfer systems "that can scale quickly, target vulnerable groups, and operate with weaker ties to formal employment"; "De-linking social protection and safety nets from employment status, facilitating labor market adjustment, and supporting skill adaptation are central to building public support for AI adoption"; in more extreme cases, pressure to consider UBI-type programmes (p. 17).
    - Public investment in energy, data centres and connectivity, with "coordinated diffusion support" (p. 17).
    - Labour market (Day 1): monitor task composition, expertise intensity and sectoral employment rather than aggregate "AI exposure"; facilitate reallocation "through retraining, improved job matching, and wage insurance—rather than attempting to impede technological adoption"; public investment in complementary sectors; transitional adjustment assistance and expanded wage insurance; broader income support if automation spreads across many sectors at once (p. 8). "Humancentric" upgrades to unemployment insurance; temporary targeted support for reskilling and job-to-job transitions; unions and sectoral bargaining (p. 11).
    - Monetary: more scenario-based analysis and explicit consideration of alternative options (p. 17); adapt to uncertainty around equilibrium rates and noisier inflation signals (p. 20); rethink central bank mandates and governance if digital money spreads (p. 17).
    - Financial: better visibility of AI-related systemic risk, efficient macroprudential buffers, contain leverage at banks and systemically important nonbanks, better data on AI exposures (pp. 17–18); lender-of-last-resort frameworks may need to consider systemically important nonbanks, with safeguards against moral hazard; forward-looking, scenario-based supervision; robust capital and liquidity buffers; stress tests against valuation and concentration shocks (p. 18); "Macroprudential oversight should extend to third party and vendors" (p. 20).
    - Structural: preserve "contestability in models, data, and compute"; upgrade education and training; reform labour contracts "including mechanisms for broader sharing of gains" (p. 18); industrial policy for clean power and smart grids; avoid regulation that reinforces concentration; interoperable safety and reporting standards (p. 20).
    - International: cooperation "on taxation principles and interoperable assurance frameworks" (p. 18); multilateral surveillance should treat AI "as a potentially asymmetric global shock" (p. 21).
    - Implementation and trust: clear public communication and expectations management; account for "low take-up, administrative complexity, and social stigma" (p. 20).
    - Data and diagnostics: track AI diffusion, sectoral concentration and labour market exposure "in near real time"; close gaps on "AI‑related investment, pricing, occupational exposure, adoption, and usage" to "distinguish between gradual and more rapid diffusion paths" (pp. 20–21); fiscal analysis should include persistent declines in labour income share; "regular scenario refreshes" (p. 21).
    - Sequencing: "Countries should carefully consider sequencing and prioritization considering their capacity and policy space" (p. 19).
    - PIIE adds: begin "developing playbooks"; consider "tax systems or other capital-sharing mechanisms"; adapt competition and data-governance frameworks; track four indicator groups (AI research breakthroughs, product releases and benchmarks, diffusion and deployment, and financial market trends) to signal "when to shift from incremental adaptation to more transformative policy responses."

## Key dynamics explored
*This scenario:*
- Restrictive regulation, compliance costs and legal risk lead to slow, uneven adoption, because they raise the cost of deploying capable AI (p. 25).
- Occupational licensing and costly human-supervision requirements lead to AI diffusion in services that is "significant but not transformative" (p. 25).
- Safety standards and job protection requirements lead to slower robot diffusion in mining, construction and manufacturing (pp. 25–26).
- Sector-specific regulation and societal resistance lead to sharply divergent growth across industries: tighter-regulated sectors see slower productivity gains while less-regulated ones adopt faster, so overall growth is weaker than under runaway diffusion (p. 14).
- Underwhelming near-term returns lead to a shift in market expectations in 2027, and so to a reckoning for AI-intensive firms that financed data centres with debt and equity (p. 26).
- Licensing, unions and political protection lead to older workers keeping greater job security, so displacement risk shifts toward younger, less-experienced workers (p. 14).
- Discouraged workers exit, so labour force participation declines (p. 14). *[The sentence sits in the baseline-scenario passage; see Reading notes on attribution.]*
- Persistent frictions that differ across countries lead displaced workers to seek work in countries where similar tasks are not yet automated (p. 15).
- Country-specific labour market structures, energy regulation and cultural attitudes shape cross-border allocation of resources (p. 15).
- Uneven uptake leads to "gradual but persistent pressures on fiscal space and policy transmission" (p. 15).

*Applies across the set:*
- AI raises output by automating more tasks, raising productivity within tasks and accelerating R&D; low elasticities of substitution, bottlenecks and diminishing returns limit aggregate gains, so "even very high productivity achieved through automated tasks may translate into modest aggregate growth" (p. 6).
- "The share of tasks automated is likely to be more important for growth than productivity gains within individual tasks" (p. 6).
- Automating AI research may create strong feedback, but "intelligence explosions are not inevitable once diminishing returns and validation constraints to research are considered" (p. 7).
- Market expectations of transformative AI raise real interest rates ahead of realised gains, which worsens debt dynamics for highly indebted sovereigns (p. 7).
- Automating high-expertise tasks lowers barriers to entry, raising employment but stagnating or lowering wages; automating low-expertise tasks raises the expertise of remaining work, raising wages but lowering employment in that occupation (pp. 7–8).
- AI substitutes for labour in "intelligence" tasks, moving workers toward "physical" tasks; wages may rise at first and then fall at higher adoption if displaced labour crowds into sectors with fixed capital (p. 8).
- High data and compute requirements create barriers to entry and concentration among core AI providers, which could dampen innovation and spillovers (p. 14); scale economies amplify "winner-take-most" dynamics (pp. 2, 9–10).
- Automation expands supply and raises investment demand while job displacement weakens household consumption, so the net effect on prices is uncertain (p. 14).
- Better infrastructure, human capital and finance let advanced economies capture more of the gains, but more AI-exposed workers mean they could face more political resistance (p. 14).
- Expectations of AI-driven growth that adjust faster than realised productivity lead to "asset repricing, leverage stress, and feedback loops between the real economy and financial markets" (p. 19).
- Weak state capacity can "lock countries into slow diffusion equilibria characterized by weaker growth, eroded tax bases, and heightened exposure to volatility" (p. 19).
- High perceived risks (job loss, compliance liability, reputational harm, risk aversion, doubts about model reliability) make adoption "hesitant and uneven," delaying productivity gains while prolonging structural instability and financial stress (p. 20).

## State of technology
*This scenario:*
- "The underlying capabilities of AI technologies and robots become very powerful" (p. 25).
- Not addressed: market structure or provider concentration specific to this scenario (Appendix 2 states provider dominance only for the runaway scenario).

*Applies across the set (shared assumption):*
- Capability is held fixed across the two scenarios so discussion can "abstract from uncertainty about technical feasibility" (p. 13).
- AI attains "human expert-level capabilities over the next five years, driven by technological advancements in major economies, particularly in the US and China"; by the early 2030s it can technically perform "most cognitive and physical tasks" and replace workers across sectors via integration with robotics (p. 25). The body text says "a wide range of cognitive and manual tasks by the early 2030s" (p. 13).
- These timelines follow "the accelerated timelines projected by the heads of leading AI companies," well ahead of median economic forecasts (p. 25).

*Applies across the set (Day 1 state of play):*
- AGI timelines have shortened; "estimates of 8–10 years now viewed as conservative by segments of the tech community—the so-called 'San Francisco Consensus'" (p. 4). Drivers: falling compute costs, large-scale data-centre investment and hardware–software feedback loops, despite "jagged" capabilities, meaning some tasks are easy for AI while others of similar difficulty are beyond it (pp. 4–5, footnote 2). Forecasts point to earlier AGI, a threshold at which AI becomes "a broadly deployable, autonomous 'dropin- worker'" (p. 5; Figure 1, a Metaculus forecast).
- Inference prices for some frontier models have fallen "by over 99 percent" (p. 5; Figure 2).
- Movement toward multi-agent systems, where "the 'rules of the game'... gain more significance than marginal improvements in model intelligence" (p. 5).
- Generative AI needs heavy compute investment ("specialized hardware and proprietary datasets"), concentrating market power among "a small number of dominant firms and 'hyper-scalers'" (p. 10; Figure 6, annualised revenue of AI companies).
- Energy demand, grid capacity and data-centre infrastructure are bottlenecks (pp. 2, 6, 17); rising compute power demand requires clean power and smart grids (p. 20).
- Figure 3 shows median benchmark trends approaching saturation by 2030 across software engineering, biology and mathematics. Its shaded 90% intervals cover curve-fit error and are narrower than total uncertainty. Figure 4 shows selected benchmark performance approaching or exceeding the chart’s human baseline at different times by 2024; this is a comparison of benchmarks, not an economy-wide skill measure (pp. 7, 9).
- Not addressed: open vs closed models and pricing to end users beyond inference-cost declines.

## State of adoption
*This scenario:*
- Adoption across sectors and countries stays uneven, "constrained by societal pushback, government interventions, and infrastructure bottlenecks" (p. 25); body text: "gradual and uneven, constrained by regulation, societal resistance, infrastructure bottlenecks, and skills gaps" (p. 13); executive summary: "constrained by regulatory, infrastructure, and organizational frictions" (p. 2).
- "Many workers and firms initially struggle to use new AI technologies" (p. 25).
- Services: diffusion "significant but not transformative" (p. 25).
- Mining, construction, manufacturing: robot diffusion hindered by regulation (pp. 25–26).
- Sectors with tighter regulation and stronger societal resistance see slower gains; less-regulated sectors see faster uptake (p. 14).
- "Public skepticism persists and anti-AI sentiment emerges among vulnerable workers" (p. 26).
- Countries: "The gap between advanced economies and most emerging and developing economies in AI-related preparedness persists" (p. 25).
- Government and finance: integration of AI into fiscal and monetary operations is "guarded"; financial-sector adoption is constrained by regulation (p. 26).
- Firm size: Not addressed in the note for this scenario. PIIE: "Large firms and tech hubs integrate AI, but smaller businesses, governments, and developing economies lag behind. Regulatory friction, public skepticism, and skill shortages slow diffusion."

*Applies across the set:*
- Adoption is limited "not just by infrastructure but largely by regulatory uncertainty, compliance burdens, organizational inertia, and trust issues"; behavioural barriers such as reliability concerns and managerial risk aversion delay uptake; complex regulation can favour dominant firms; gains are "delayed or concentrated among organizations with higher readiness" (p. 5).
- "Adoption is a sequence of human decisions—mediated by managerial judgment, worker expectations, and public narratives" (p. 4).
- Early firm-level evidence shows gains "as experimentation gives way to integration," though adoption is uneven across firms, sectors and countries (p. 5).
- Early movers and digitally mature firms and countries consolidate advantages; later adopters see slower absorption and weaker spillovers (p. 19).
- Many emerging and developing countries have lower AI exposure and weaker readiness (p. 9).
- Financially constrained SMEs and state-owned enterprises may need government help to adopt in both scenarios (p. 17).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
*This scenario:*
- AI boosts productivity and output over five years, but "overall, growth effects are weaker in the baseline scenario, reflecting stronger institutional frictions and social pushbacks" (p. 14).
- "Growth outcomes diverge sharply across industries" (p. 14).
- "When adoption is slow and uneven, AI gradually raises productivity" (p. 19).

*Applies across the set:*
- "Across both trajectories, productivity gains could be substantial but unevenly distributed within and across countries" (p. 2); "Across scenarios, productivity gains are substantial but structurally uneven" (p. 19).
- Global growth rises "through higher productivity and associated capital accumulation" (p. 14).
- Channels, bottlenecks and the priority of task share over within-task gains (p. 6); capital accumulation feedback, which automation may partly offset by relaxing diminishing returns (p. 6); "historical experience suggests that major technological advances do not automatically generate proportional productivity growth when such constraints bind" (p. 6).
- In semi-endogenous growth frameworks, AI-driven research "may yield bounded or explosive outcomes depending on the strength of feedback and returns to scale" (p. 7).
- Even under rapid scaling, wage and output growth may plateau if physical constraints bind: "neither sharp wage collapses nor explosive growth is likely without a major structural change" (p. 8).
- Figure 5 (from Cerutti and others 2025) shows cross-country real-GDP deviations after 10 years under high- and low-TFP-growth cases (p. 10; US and the two other advanced-economy groups shown are above the corresponding world averages; the rest of the world, low-income groups, emerging groups and China are below them. These are Cerutti and colleagues’ cases, distinct from the note’s Day 2 scenarios).
- "Aggregate productivity growth alone becomes an insufficient guide for policy" when diffusion is rapid (p. 19).

### Labour market dynamics
*This scenario:*
- "Country-specific labor market institutions and social acceptance slow adjustment" (p. 14).
- "Older workers tend to retain greater job security because of licensing requirements, labor unions, and political protection, whereas younger, less-experienced workers face higher displacement risk" (p. 14).
- "Although younger workers may adapt more easily to AI-enabled roles, labor force participation declines as discouraged workers exit the labor market" (p. 14).
- Strong job protection programmes are in force (p. 25).
- Displaced workers may seek jobs abroad where tasks are not yet automated (p. 15).
- PIIE: "labor market disruption occurs but is manageable."

*Applies across the set:*
- "Labor markets face widespread job displacement pressures, with outcomes shaped by institutional frictions" (p. 14).
- Effects depend on how automation changes "the task composition and expertise content of work—not only on headline job displacement" (p. 2). Expertise explains "roughly one‑third of observed wage variation" across occupations, after controlling for education (p. 8).
- Intelligence-to-physical reallocation and the possible fall in wages at high adoption (p. 8).
- Reskilling "may be a less reliable solution when the target occupations themselves are on a shrinking horizon, reflecting the accelerating depreciation of human capital" (p. 8, footnote 3).
- "Under more extreme scenarios, the traditional link between employment and income may weaken" (p. 11).
- Traditional indicators of slack become less informative "as human employment plays a minor role in defining capacity constraints" (p. 17).
- AI adoption may reduce labour's bargaining power (p. 17).
- Displacement costs fall on incumbent workers and borrowers while productivity gains go to new entrants and AI-intensive firms (p. 15).
- Fiscal analysis should consider "persistent declines in labor income share" (p. 21).

### Winners and losers
*This scenario:*
- Winners: less-regulated sectors (faster uptake) and older workers protected by licensing, unions and political protection (p. 14).
- Losers: sectors with tighter regulation and stronger societal resistance (slower productivity gains); younger, less-experienced workers (higher displacement risk); discouraged workers who exit the labour force (p. 14); "vulnerable workers" among whom anti-AI sentiment emerges (p. 26); AI-intensive firms that debt- and equity-financed data centres (2027 reckoning) (p. 26); most emerging and developing economies (persistent preparedness gap) (p. 25).

*Applies across the set:*
- Capital owners capture "a large share of the gains," raising wealth inequality (p. 14). *[The sentence follows "Rapid AI diffusion leads to broad job displacement" and precedes "In the baseline scenario"; whether it covers the baseline is ambiguous.]*
- Some high-skilled workers in occupations complementing AI "benefit disproportionately" (p. 14) *[same attribution ambiguity]*.
- Concentration among core AI service providers (p. 14).
- "Metropolitan areas with innovation hubs and advanced AI infrastructure attract capital and talent, whereas regions with weaker digital and energy infrastructure fall further behind" (p. 14).
- Advanced economies, especially the United States, gain more; emerging markets and low-income countries gain less (p. 14).
- Rents accrue "disproportionately to AI-intensive firms and countries" (p. 9).
- Countries that "primarily consume AI services, rather than produce them" face the sharpest tax-base problems (p. 16).

### Household incomes and living standards
*This scenario:* Not addressed beyond labour market effects.

*Applies across the set:*
- "Household consumption weakens as job displacement increases" (p. 14).
- Sharp falls in household and firm income during periods of rapid adoption weaken debt-servicing capacity (p. 15).
- Strengthened social protection "can mitigate short-term income losses without undermining incentives to reallocate" (p. 8).
- The transition "may involve prolonged periods of labor displacement, income concentration, and financial stress" (p. 19).

### Government finances
*This scenario:*
- "Gradual but persistent pressures on fiscal space and policy transmission" (p. 15).
- Slow, uneven adoption remains "broadly compatible with current fiscal, monetary, and financial frameworks" (p. 19). PIIE: "fiscal and monetary frameworks remain largely intact."
- Tax-base erosion is described as "more pronounced in the runaway scenario" (p. 16). *[Inference: milder here by comparison.]*

*Applies across the set:*
- Fiscal strain from "erosion of labor tax bases and rising social spending needs—particularly if employment-linked social insurance becomes less effective" (p. 2).
- Shifting tax from labour to capital via higher corporate income tax "may only partially offset these losses given capital mobility, market concentration, and international tax competition" (p. 16).
- Rising spending pressure on social welfare and cyber security (Figure 8, p. 16).
- Limited fiscal space may restrict options (p. 17); "Preexisting fiscal constraints are also likely to limit policy options, particularly for low-income countries and fragile states" (p. 19).
- Higher real rates priced ahead of gains worsen sovereign debt dynamics by "raising interest-growth differential" (p. 7); fiscal policy must manage "pressures on public debt dynamics during the transition" (p. 20).
- Countries with stronger administrative and digital public infrastructure are better placed "to capture rents" (p. 19).
- PIIE: "In the United States, for example, approximately three-quarters of federal tax revenue comes from labor income."

### International economic patterns
*This scenario:*
- The preparedness gap between advanced economies and most emerging and developing economies persists (p. 25).
- "Country-specific labor market structures, energy regulations, and cultural attitudes toward AI play a major role in shaping cross-border allocation of resources" (p. 15).
- Migration: "persistent frictions may encourage workers displaced by automation to seek opportunities in countries where similar tasks have not yet been automated" (p. 15).

*Applies across the set:*
- "Cross-country inequalities widen as advanced economies capture a disproportionate share of AI-related gains" (p. 14).
- "Traditional migration flows from lower- to higher-income countries are likely to decline as labor demand in advanced economies falls" (p. 15).
- Day 1: robotics compressing labour costs may reshore manufacturing and services to advanced economies, "undermining export-led growth models"; capital may move toward AI-ready jurisdictions, increasing outflows from less-prepared economies; some developing countries risk "a persistent cycle of industrial decline" (p. 11).
- "The digital frontier may serve as a new barrier to global convergence rather than a bridge" (p. 11).
- Emerging and developing economies risk being "locked into low diffusion equilibria" (p. 18).
- Cross-border spillovers via "capital flows, mobile rents, and fragmented standards" (p. 18).

### Market pricing and scarcity
*This scenario:* Not addressed.

*Applies across the set:*
- Net price effect "uncertain and depends on the balance between expanded supply and subdued demand" (p. 14).
- "Large relative price changes complicate inflation diagnostics" (p. 17).
- Rising demand for energy and critical raw materials for AI infrastructure (p. 14).
- Inference cost declines of more than 99% (p. 5).
- PIIE: relative price changes include "changes in the relative value of labor"; "efficiency gains may exert disinflationary pressure"; rapid productivity growth "could push up the neutral interest rate."

### Financial markets and stability
*This scenario:*
- "The expansion of data centers continued throughout 2026. In 2027, however, there was a change in expectations in financial markets as the immediate financial returns from AI adoption underwhelmed and investors started questioning the optimistic projections of leading labs. AI-intensive firms that used various debt and equity instruments to finance data centers faced a reckoning" (p. 26).
- Financial-sector AI adoption is "constrained by regulatory requirements" (p. 26).

*Applies across the set:*
- "Although vulnerabilities may differ across scenarios," priorities include visibility of AI-related systemic risks, macroprudential buffers, and containing leverage among banks and systemically important nonbanks (p. 17).
- AI-driven trading "could heighten herding risks, decouple market movements from fundamentals, amplify bubbles, and elevate volatility" (p. 15). *[This sits in a paragraph that opens "In the runaway scenario"; whether it also applies here is not stated.]*
- Leverage to finance AI investment plus rapid capital obsolescence complicate credit risk assessment; tech-sector concentration raises single-source exposures (p. 15).
- "Valuation cycles around AI-intensive firms, financing structures tied to data centers and energy infrastructure" can propagate shocks across balance sheets and borders, with many vulnerabilities arising "well before any new steady state is reached" (p. 19).
- "Current real interest rates provide limited evidence that markets are pricing in nearterm, extremely rapid AIdriven growth" (p. 7).
- Reduced diversification, lower return predictability and uncertain collateral values complicate buffer calibration (p. 18).
- PIIE: risks include "stranded assets, debt overhang, and the potential for sharp corrections if expectations disappoint."

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
*This scenario:* Older workers keep job security through licensing, unions and political protection; younger, less-experienced workers face higher displacement risk; participation falls as discouraged workers leave the labour force (p. 14).

*Applies across the set:* Expertise, distinct from formal education, is "a central determinant of wages" (p. 8). Reskilling may be unreliable when target occupations are shrinking (p. 8, footnote 3).

### Affordable essentials
Not addressed. (The note discusses aggregate price pressures only, p. 14.)

### Reliable safety net
*This scenario:* "Strong job protection programs" are enforced (p. 25). Pressures on fiscal space are "gradual but persistent" (p. 15).

*Applies across the set:* Employment-linked social insurance becomes less effective under rapid diffusion; safety nets should be de-linked from employment status; in extreme cases, UBI-type programmes may be considered (p. 17). ### A say in your future
*This scenario:* "Public skepticism persists and anti-AI sentiment emerges among vulnerable workers" (p. 26). Societal pushback and political protection shape adoption and job security (pp. 14, 25).

*Applies across the set:* "The ability of institutions to acquire and maintain public trust determines the stability of the transition" (p. 2). Governance should incorporate "the perspectives of workers, firms, civil society, and affected communities" (p. 12). Unions and sectoral bargaining are "a vital instrument for participatory governance" (p. 11). Governance frameworks "are currently struggling to keep pace"; this challenges "democratic accountability" (p. 11). Advanced economies could face more political resistance (p. 14). Adjustment mechanisms should "build and sustain social consensus" (p. 20).

### A fair share of progress
*This scenario:* Not addressed separately.

*Applies across the set:* "Without proactive and well-timed institutional responses, the gains from AI are likely to remain concentrated, exacerbating inequalities both within and across countries" (p. 12). Capital owners capture a large share (p. 14). Labour-contract reforms "including mechanisms for broader sharing of gains" can reduce resistance (p. 18). Governments should develop "public-good" AI capabilities (pp. 16, 18). Whether productivity gains "translate into durable and broadly shared improvements in welfare" is central to policy (p. 19).

## Other dynamics
*Applies across the set:*
- Safety and governance: "Limited standards, reporting, and enforcement mechanisms for frontier AI models challenge the balance between innovation, safety, and democratic accountability"; harmonised international safety and transparency standards are lacking; options include third-party audits, disclosure, and licensing or liability regimes for frontier models, shifting oversight "toward pre-deployment rather than ex-post correction" — but "poorly calibrated frameworks risk slowing diffusion and reinforcing concentration" (p. 11). There is uncertainty about "the pace, controllability, and concentration of AI development" (p. 12).
- Existential risk: market anticipation of "heightened existential risk" could also raise real interest rates (p. 7).
- Misuse and cyber: "Financial instability, misuse, and geopolitical competition" call for multilateral approaches (p. 11); cyber security spending pressure (Figure 8, p. 16).
- Geopolitics: capability is driven by the US and China (p. 25); "escalating geopolitical competition for technological supremacy" could permanently widen global inequality (pp. 10–11); demand for energy and critical raw materials "could further heighten geopolitical tensions" (p. 14).
- Environment and energy: industrial policy for "clean power and smart electric grids with rising compute power demands" (p. 20).
- Monetary system: digital money and CBDC-type instruments; currencies pegged to major currencies could weaken transmission "especially in smaller and more open economies" and affect central bank independence (p. 17).
- Information ecosystem: adoption is mediated by "public narratives" (p. 4); clear public communication and expectations management (p. 20).
