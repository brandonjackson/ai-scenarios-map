# Underemployment and a Lost Generation — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Policy off (or, more precisely, policy blind).** The scenario doesn't assume government refuses to act. It assumes government *doesn't see* the problem because its indicators don't show it. Policy is reactive and late, not absent.

## Big assumptions
1. **AI substitutes for junior work and complements senior work.** Experience, judgement and client relationships stay valuable, while codifiable entry-level tasks go to AI. This matches early evidence (e.g., payroll data showing falling employment for young workers in AI-exposed occupations, cited by Toloui) and anecdotes from law, software and consulting.
2. **Firms don't invest in training pipelines.** Each firm finds it rational to hire experienced staff and skip juniors, although the industry as a whole needs future seniors. A classic collective-action problem.
3. **Headline indicators mask the damage.** Underemployment, rising graduate–non-graduate mismatch and delayed household formation aren't in the statistics policymakers watch.
4. **The cost of essentials keeps rising on its own.** Housing and childcare shortages continue regardless of AI.
5. **Moderate, not transformative, AI.** It is capable enough to replace junior tasks but not most senior ones. This middle ground could last for years.

## What must be true for this to occur
- Entry-level cognitive tasks become cheaper to automate than to train a human on, and stay that way.
- Senior roles stay human long enough for the ladder to break below them rather than all at once.
- Employers don't create new apprenticeship-style routes in.
- Policy doesn't add youth-specific measures (underemployment, hiring of graduates and school-leavers, earnings by age group) to its dashboard.

## Tensions and weak points
- Self-limiting or self-reinforcing? If seniors aren't replaced as they retire, firms will eventually need to train juniors again, unless AI improves fast enough to fill senior roles too (moving toward High Substitution).
- Augmentation research (call centres, coding) often finds AI helps *less-experienced* workers most. That points the other way: AI could compress the experience premium and help juniors (compare Agrawal et al.'s Turing Transformation).
- The scenario's realism is its strength: it needs no AGI and no breakthrough, only today's capabilities and today's incentives.

## Scoring notes
Draft scores (2/2/2/3/2) fit. Because the goal judges for the worst-off group, this scenario scores low even if the average citizen is fine. That shows why the worst-off rule matters.
