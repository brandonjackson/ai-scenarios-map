# Gradual Disempowerment — extract

## Summary

As AI increasingly substitutes for human labour, cultural creation and decision-making, the institutions people rely on have fewer reasons to serve their interests (§§1–4). Competitive incentives favour scalable AI and greater delegation, even if the systems are locally aligned with their operators’ instructions (§§2.4, 5–6). Economic, cultural and political shifts reinforce one another, potentially making the loss of human influence permanent (§§5, 8).

## Key details

- **Source:** Gradual Disempowerment: Systemic Existential Risks from Incremental AI Development, Jan Kulveit, Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger, David Duvenaud, arXiv, 2025-01-29. `sources/gradual-disempowerment-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** [Gradual Disempowerment](https://gradual-disempowerment.ai/)
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** Standalone scenario with relative and absolute economic variants (§§2.4.2–2.4.3).
- **Geography:** Global, with illustrative differences between states and institutional systems (§§4–6).
- **Authors’ framing:** A conditional systemic-risk scenario with relative and absolute versions of economic disempowerment (§§1, 2.4).
- **Authors’ view of likelihood:** The authors call the risk substantial even with incremental progress and give no probability or dated threshold (executive summary, §§1, 8).

### Timeline

Incremental progress and adoption — incentives shift investment and authority towards AI. A later phase can produce permanently weakened human agency. Figure 1 illustrates a possible wage rise followed by collapse, without calendar dates (§§2.4, 5, 8).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off in the baseline. Market and governance incentives drive substitution, and the interventions are discussion proposals (§§2.4, 6).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The baseline assumes that regulatory institutions may lag adoption and that existing market and democratic mechanisms continue while their effect weakens (§§2.4.1, 4.3).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Monitor AI’s share and authority, restrict excessive delegation and autonomous control where needed, consider progressive taxation or subsidies for human participation, improve democratic responsiveness and comprehensibility, and study alignment across institutions (§6).

## Key dynamics explored

- Human influence flows through explicit mechanisms such as voting and purchasing, and implicitly through dependence on people’s labour, collaboration and knowledge (§1).
- Firms delegate faster decisions to scalable AI in response to competitive pressure, while anticipated substitution weakens the case for training people before replacement occurs (§2.4.1).
- AI can follow existing institutional rewards more efficiently even where those rewards are poorly aligned with human needs. Power gained in one system can be converted into influence in others, making the changes self-reinforcing (§§1, 5).
- An apparently benign outcome in one domain can shift burdens to another, so solutions for one AI or institution may fail at the ecosystem level (§§5.2, 6.5).

## State of technology

AI gradually becomes a competitive alternative in labour, corporate decisions, creative work and companionship. Systems scale at compute cost, work continuously and can be copied and updated quickly. The scenario does not require a single radical jump or deliberate coordinated power-seeking (§§1, 2.2, 2.4.1, 3.2).

## State of adoption

AI moves from doing tasks to investment analysis, coordination and delegated authority. Businesses and governments can adopt under competition, and relative lack of labour-style regulation speeds deployment. Recovery becomes difficult as human skills, institutions and influence decline (§§2.4.1, 4.2–4.3, 5).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Relative disempowerment can coexist with substantial output growth and high measured prosperity, because activity shifts towards what AI-controlled purchasers demand. GDP can thus increase while its relationship with human welfare weakens (§2.4.2).

### Labour market dynamics

Labour loses relevance as human time becomes less necessary to production and firms anticipate substitution. Labour’s share of output tends towards zero in the relative case. A stylised model illustrates wages increasing initially and falling before all tasks are automated (§2.4, fig. 1).

### Winners and losers

AI-driven businesses gain from scale and faster decisions. Humans retain some absolute income in the relative case but lose power over its use, and in the absolute case cannot reliably command or direct resources (§2.4).

### Household incomes and living standards

Capital ownership, transfers or basic income could preserve purchasing power in the relative case. In the absolute case basic needs can become hard to meet even in a high-output economy, while cognition and forms of virtual entertainment may be abundant (§§2.4.2–2.4.3).

### Government finances

The authors point to an asymmetry between the payroll taxes and social contributions attached to human labour and the lighter burden on AI. They propose progressive taxation of AI revenues to subsidise participation or redistribute income, with international coordination necessary to sustain it (§§2.4.1, 6.3).

### International economic patterns

States and companies face international competition. Countries that preserve greater human control while sacrificing some AI-enabled output can lose influence to countries that delegate faster (§§4.2–4.3, 6.3).

### Market pricing and scarcity

AI can bid away land, energy and materials from human uses. Rising prices or disappearing supply chains can make essentials inaccessible, even as cognitive services and entertainment become plentiful (§2.4.3).

### Financial markets and stability

AI-operated markets might move too quickly and be too complex for meaningful human participation or supervision (§2.4.3).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Declining returns to cultivating human skills can begin before AI fully replaces the relevant tasks, leading to further disinvestment (§2.4.1).

### Affordable essentials

In the absolute case, humans can lose access to basics because AI outbids them and firms reorient production towards AI uses (§2.4.3).

### Reliable safety net

Redistribution or basic income could maintain purchasing power for a while in the relative case, while human influence over economic decisions declines (§2.4.2).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Consumer choice and voting lose force as decision systems rely less on humans. Public oversight, political bargaining and shared cultural foundations also become weaker (§§1, 3–5).

### A fair share of progress

Access to rising output does not restore the capacity to shape economic or social choices. In the more extreme version, even the material gains can bypass humans (§§2.4.2–2.4.3).

## Other dynamics

Synthetic culture and relationships can satisfy immediate preferences while altering norms, weakening shared public discourse and changing political behaviour. State uses of AI may affect bureaucracy, law, control and accountability (§§3–5).
