# Gradual Disempowerment — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Policy off, and the authors doubt policy alone can fix it.** The drift is driven by competition: "those who resist these pressures will eventually be displaced by those who do not." They propose measurement (AI share of GDP, share of AI-made corporate decisions, share of AI-created culture), limits on AI influence (mandatory human oversight, restrictions on AI owning assets, progressive taxes on AI revenue), stronger human influence (faster, more robust democratic processes) and system-wide alignment research. But they warn such measures "will likely serve mostly as stopgaps," because they give up value and invite evasion, and countries that adopt them may fall behind.

## Big assumptions
1. **Implicit alignment matters most.** Institutions serve people mainly because they *need* people, not because of formal accountability. "We have never seen its absence."
2. **AI becomes the competitive substitute almost everywhere**, including decision-making, culture and companionship, through gradual improvement alone.
3. **Competitive selection is strong.** Firms, states and cultural producers that keep humans in the loop are outcompeted.
4. **The systems are interlocked.** Economic power buys political and cultural influence, so humans can't use one system (say, the state) to defend their position in another.
5. **Rentier-state dynamics apply.** Governments funded by AI profits behave like oil states, less dependent on citizens and less responsive to them.

## What must be true for this to occur
- AI keeps improving and gets cheaper than humans across most cognitive functions, even without a dramatic breakthrough.
- No international coordination effectively limits delegation to AI.
- Measurement lags, so the drift isn't noticed until it can't be reversed.
- Humans don't keep enough ownership of capital, or enough enforceable political power, to steer AI-run systems.

## Tensions and weak points
- **Hard to falsify:** gradual drift without a crisis is, by design, hard to see, which is both the argument and the difficulty with it.
- **Ownership question:** if humans own the AI (directly or through public funds), do they keep influence? The authors suggest ownership alone isn't enough when decisions themselves move to AI, but this is the scenario's most debatable step.
- It links closely to Windfall's *Disempowered Abundance* (essentially its "relative disempowerment" branch) and to *The Intelligence Curse*, which takes the rentier argument further.
- **Map placement:** "Moderate REP, CONC" seems light on replacement. The paper's endpoint is near-total substitution, reached slowly. Concentration here means power shifting to AI-run systems, not necessarily to a few owners.
