# Gradual Disempowerment — summary

*Jan Kulveit, Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger and David Duvenaud, "Gradual Disempowerment: Systemic Existential Risks from Incremental AI Development", arXiv, 29 January 2025 (with a companion site). A conditional systemic-risk scenario with relative and absolute economic variants, global in scope and undated. Read from the full text; see `extract.md`.*

**The scenario.** No sudden break and no coordinated power-seeking: "even an incremental increase in AI capabilities ... poses a substantial risk of eventual human disempowerment". Institutions serve people through explicit levers such as voting and buying, and implicitly because they depend on human labour, collaboration and knowledge. As AI becomes the competitive alternative in labour, corporate decisions, creative work and companionship, that dependence fades. Competition pushes firms and states to delegate, AI follows institutional rewards even where they serve people badly, and power won in one system buys influence in others, so the loss can become permanent.

## Hard work pays
Broken for all, in the end. Firms anticipate substitution, so the case for training people weakens before replacement arrives, and returns to building skills decline. In a stylised model wages first rise, then fall before all tasks are automated; labour's share of output "tends towards zero".

## Affordable essentials
Depends on the variant. In relative disempowerment people keep purchasing power through capital, transfers or basic income while losing say over the economy. In absolute disempowerment AI bids away land, energy and materials, and essentials become inaccessible "even in a high-output economy", while cognitive services and entertainment are plentiful.

## Reliable safety net
A stopgap at best. Redistribution or basic income could hold up incomes for a while in the relative case. Human labour carries payroll taxes and social contributions that AI does not, eroding the base; the authors' answer, progressive taxes on AI revenue, would need international coordination.

## Responsive government
The core concern. Consumer choice and voting lose force as decision systems rely less on people, and public oversight, political bargaining and shared culture weaken. Countries that keep more human control can lose influence to those that delegate faster, and regulators may lag adoption.

## Time for what matters
Not addressed directly. Synthetic culture and relationships satisfy immediate preferences while changing norms and weakening shared public discourse.

## Bottom line
A slow drift in which output and measured prosperity can rise while people lose the ability to shape what the economy, culture and state do.
