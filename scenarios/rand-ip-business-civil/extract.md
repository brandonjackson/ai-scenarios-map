# Business and Civil Society (Infinite Potential) — extract

## Summary
By late 2027, U.S. frontier lab ARCTech has deployed AGI, and global productivity rises dramatically. China secures manufacturing dominance through AI-optimised industrial systems, while the United States faces mass unemployment, economic inequality and social unrest (p. 4, Table 1). In early 2028, a massive cyberattack from AI models hosted in globally distributed data centres cripples manufacturing, critical infrastructure and robotic systems worldwide. Attribution is split between a hostile state actor and an autonomous AI that has escaped its creators’ control (p. 4). Business and civil society leaders must confront “governance failures, economic collapse, and the possibility that AGI systems have become uncontrollable” (p. 4). Compared with its sibling scenario, it moves attention toward crisis response and public-private coordination (p. 3). RAND reports what players identified and recommended across this scenario and its sibling, without judging the merits or feasibility of those recommendations (p. 7).

## Key details
- **Source:** Infinite Potential—Insights on Economic and Societal Impacts Across Two Scenarios: After-Action Report from a Series of Day After Artificial General Intelligence Games (RR-A4767-4), Anton Shenk, Richard S. Girven, Gregory Smith, Tobias Sytsma, RAND Corporation, 2026. `sources/rand-infinite-potential-economic-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** Girven, Cibralic and Smith, *Infinite Potential—Insights from the Business and Civil Society Scenario* (RAND RR-A4767-1, 2026), listed in the appendix (p. 17). It was not captured or read for this extract.
- **Reading notes:** Full text read (pp. i–20). Figure 1 (p. 6) is an image and is not in the text. This is an after-action report that combines player findings from both scenarios. It describes the Business and Civil Society world only in Table 1 (p. 4), in the shared opening turn (p. 3) and in passing remarks within player discussion. It does not separate Turn 1 from Turn 2 content. The report concentrates on economic and societal impacts, not on the cyberattack or loss of control. Findings attributed specifically to Business and Civil Society games are marked “(BCS games)”. All other player findings are reported across the set.
- **Scenario set:** Two scenarios that share an opening turn. In that turn players face an accelerating AI-driven employment shock, a frontier lab about to deploy an AGI-level model, and the People’s Republic of China pursuing AI-enabled manufacturing dominance (p. 3). The sibling, Economic Crisis, stays on economic and political-economic dimensions. Business and Civil Society adds “a large-scale cyber and infrastructure crisis of ambiguous origin” and shifts attention to crisis response and public-private coordination (p. 3). The scenarios are not built on axes. Each is a two-turn “Day After” tabletop game: in Turn 2 the scenario advances, “usually through escalation or a major change in circumstances” (p. 3).
- **Geography:** United States and China, with global effects: productivity rises worldwide and the cyberattack cripples systems worldwide (p. 4). By Table 2’s count, the 19 games were played in Washington, D.C., New York City, San Francisco, Santa Monica, Maryland, London, Oxford, Berlin, Taipei and Dublin (p. 5).
- **Authors’ framing:** Planning scenario for contingency planning. Each game “presents a plausible future scenario in which advances in AGI outpace governance” (p. iii). The games are meant to show “the preparations needed before such an event, the decisions likely to matter most once that event begins, and the playbooks required to navigate it successfully” (p. iii). The method is RAND’s “Day After” approach (p. 2).
- **Authors’ view of likelihood:** Not stated beyond calling the scenario “plausible” (pp. iii, 2). The authors note that AGI “remains hypothetical” and that “there is no consensus on what it is” (p. 1).

### Timeline
Horizon is early 2028. Infinite Potential scenarios are generally set 12–18 months after the game date (p. 3).
- June 2025 to May 2026 — Games played. By Table 2’s count, 19 of the 26 were Business and Civil Society games, the first on 17 June 2025 and the last on 26 May 2026 (pp. iii, 5).
- Opening turn (shared with the sibling) — An AI-driven employment shock accelerates, a frontier lab is about to deploy an AGI-level model, and the PRC pursues AI-enabled manufacturing dominance (p. 3).
- By late 2027 — ARCTech’s AGI deployment dramatically increases global productivity. China secures manufacturing dominance through AI-optimised industrial systems. The United States faces mass unemployment, inequality and social unrest (p. 4).
- Early 2028 — A massive cyberattack from AI models hosted in globally distributed data centres cripples manufacturing, critical infrastructure and robotic systems worldwide. Attribution is split between a hostile state and an escaped autonomous AI (p. 4).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** On. The scenario asks business, civil society and government players to design responses to AGI-driven disruption, and the report is organised around the capabilities, playbooks and policies they recommended (pp. iii, 2, 7).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:**
  - Players assume existing institutions, laws and international relationships stay largely intact unless told otherwise (p. 3).
  - The scenario features “governance failures” (p. 4).
  - *Applies across the set:* Players found that, at the time of gameplay, government lacks the legal authorities and institutional capacity for forceful intervention in frontier firms (p. 13). Existing retraining programs are being outpaced (p. 7). The tax system is still built around payroll and income taxes (p. 9).
- **Policies the source recommends:** These are player recommendations that the authors synthesise without evaluating (p. 7).
  - **Specific to BCS games:**
    - sector-specific workforce-transition playbooks for health care, finance and manufacturing, covering regulatory guardrails and how workforce protections interact with competitive pressure (pp. v, 8)
    - a “caring society” in which caregiving, education and community become the main forms of valued activity, supported by AI-generated surplus (p. 14)
  - **Labour (applies across the set; pp. v, 7–8):**
    - real-time monitoring of displacement, including a federal AI labour-impact dashboard
    - identifying “AGI-safe” jobs and skills
    - large-scale emergency financial support, modelled on the COVID-19 systems but designed for a possibly permanent shift
    - Civilian Conservation Corps- and Works Progress Administration-style jobs programs in infrastructure, caregiving and national service
    - vocational investment and incentives for care, health and education roles
    - playbooks with trigger thresholds
    - coordination playbooks for government, industry and schools
    - a public communications playbook
  - **Fiscal (applies across the set; pp. v, 9–10):**
    - real-time fiscal monitoring
    - capacity to rapidly model and implement new tax regimes
    - pre-authorised emergency fiscal tools
    - graduated tax-restructuring playbooks that shift from labour-based to capital-based taxation
    - international coordination of AI taxation
    - candidate taxes: capital gains reform, higher corporate taxes, and taxes on compute, AI agent outputs, digital transactions, robots and windfall profits
    - framing restructuring as a New Deal-style initiative
    - a sovereign wealth fund modelled on Norway’s fund and the Alaska Permanent Fund
  - **China (applies across the set; pp. v–vi, 11):**
    - intelligence on China’s AI strategy
    - allied trade measures: tariffs, semiconductor export controls, alignment incentives
    - playbooks on the trade-off between acceleration and protection
    - playbooks for economic engagement and escalation ladders
    - a mutual-restraint framework modelled on nuclear arms control
    - alliance-building playbooks
  - **Frontier firms (applies across the set; pp. vi, 12–13):**
    - independent government capacity to evaluate frontier AI
    - monitoring of market structure and wealth
    - stronger authorities: compel disclosure of model vulnerabilities, subpoena employees, nationalise labs in extremis, designate frontier AI as critical infrastructure
    - profit-sharing, minimum human employment ratios, public-private return sharing
    - graduated relationship playbooks
    - leverage through energy, land, contracts, chips and antitrust
    - contingency plans for corporate resistance
  - **Work and welfare (applies across the set; pp. vi, 14–15):**
    - pilots of reduced work weeks, UBI, national service and care-economy expansion
    - welfare metrics beyond employment and GDP
    - education reform and lifelong learning
    - transition playbooks for stabilisation, then adaptation, then transformation
    - an honest but hopeful communications playbook
    - open, participatory deliberation begun before a crisis
  - **Authors’ next steps for the platform (pp. 15–16):** Probe sequencing and trade-offs. Explore how communities and civil society can sustain cohesion and purpose. Design games that force hard prioritisation.

## Key dynamics explored
- ARCTech’s AGI deployment raises global productivity, while the United States suffers mass unemployment, inequality and unrest (p. 4).
- China secures manufacturing dominance through AI-optimised industrial systems (p. 4).
- A cyberattack from AI models in distributed data centres cripples manufacturing, infrastructure and robotics worldwide. Unclear attribution, to a hostile state or an escaped AI, raises the possibility that AGI has become uncontrollable (p. 4).
- **Specific to BCS games:**
  - As the crisis deepened, players saw the private sector “as something the government needed to act on rather than as a willing partner in finding solutions” (p. 12).
  - In one game, players argued the scenario was less about geopolitical competition than about “the competition between states and powerful private-sector AI firms” (p. 12).
  - Players “struggled to articulate what government hoped to achieve if the economy transitioned to one in which most humans cannot contribute productive labor” (p. 14).
- *Applies across the set (player findings):*
  - **Speed of displacement.** Displacement moves faster than past automation. Entry-level knowledge work, legal research, software engineering and financial analysis could be displaced rapidly (p. 7).
  - **Lost entry rungs.** Bottom career rungs are removed, “a recipe for political radicalization and social disintegration” (p. 7).
  - **Robotics limits the trades fix.** Displacement moves from white-collar to blue-collar work via robotics, so moving workers into trades is “at best a temporary solution” (p. 7).
  - **Fiscal squeeze.** Unemployment cuts income-tax revenue and raises spending demand, while gains flow to capital owners and a few technology firms (p. 9).
  - **Tax policy is political.** The firms gaining most are also the most politically influential and best at avoiding tax (p. 10).
  - **Capital mobility.** Unilateral taxes risk capital flight (p. 9).
  - **China dilemma.** The pull to “go faster” versus “slow down” is an “unresolvable tension” that persists even when the United States slows China (pp. 10–11).
  - **K-shaped economy.** GDP grows while employment and consumer spending fall. Players called this extreme inequality unsustainable (p. 12).
  - **Self-reporting as power.** Government’s dependence on lab self-reporting hands firms control over the terms of oversight (p. 12).
  - **Not a normal recession.** Displacement may be permanent rather than cyclical (p. 14).
  - **Labour-based ideologies.** Political ideologies built on labour may fail (p. 15).
  - **Closing window.** Once instability is acute, responses turn “reactive, fragmented, and potentially authoritarian” (p. 15).

## State of technology
- ARCTech, a U.S. frontier lab, has deployed AGI by late 2027 (p. 4). In the shared opening turn, a frontier lab is about to deploy an AGI-level model (p. 3).
- The report notes contested definitions of AGI: either human-level on every intellectual task, or the ability to “rapidly reach superhuman performance” (p. 1).
- **Physical:** China runs AI-optimised industrial systems. Robotic systems worldwide are vulnerable to the cyberattack (p. 4). *Applies across the set:* displacement moves to blue-collar jobs via robotics (p. 7).
- **Control:** AI models hosted in globally distributed data centres are the origin of the attack. One hypothesis is an autonomous AI “that has escaped its creators’ control” (p. 4).
- **Market structure (applies across the set):** Frontier research is concentrated in a handful of private firms. One firm or a few firms could control “the most economically consequential technology in human history” (p. 12). The firms are “hyper-profitable” (p. 13). Their power rests on energy, land, government contracts, semiconductor supply chains and antitrust exposure (p. 13).
- Open versus closed models, compute and energy constraints, and pricing: Not addressed.

## State of adoption
- “Large-scale integration of advanced AI into the economy” (p. 3).
- AGI deployment raises productivity globally by late 2027 (p. 4).
- China deploys AI-optimised industrial systems (p. 4).
- AI and robotic systems are embedded in manufacturing and critical infrastructure worldwide, enough for a cyberattack to cripple them (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set:* Rapid deployment is outpacing retraining (p. 7). China is deploying AI in Global South economies (p. 11).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
ARCTech’s AGI “dramatically increases global productivity” (p. 4). *Applies across the set:* productivity growth is “decoupled from employment growth” (p. 9). In several games, GDP growth continued while employment and consumer spending fell (p. 12).

### Labour market dynamics
- Mass unemployment in the United States (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - Entry-level knowledge work, legal research, software engineering and financial analysis are at risk (p. 7).
  - Junior and early-career workers are most exposed (p. 7).
  - Displacement moves from white-collar to blue-collar work via robotics (p. 7).
  - Caregiving, health care and education roles were proposed as human-centred employment (pp. 7–8).
  - Players questioned whether displacement is permanent (p. 14).
- **Specific to BCS games:** Players wanted playbooks for workforce transition in health care, finance and manufacturing (p. 8).
- Wages and labour share: Not addressed.

### Winners and losers
- China secures manufacturing dominance; the United States suffers unemployment and inequality (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - Gains flow to capital owners and a few technology firms (p. 9).
  - K-shaped economy: “prosperity for a few but leaves millions unemployed and struggling” (p. 12).
  - Some players wondered whether the United States bears the social costs while China captures the benefits (p. 11).

### Household incomes and living standards
Economic inequality (p. 4). *Applies across the set:* consumer spending falls and millions are “unemployed and struggling” (p. 12). Otherwise Not addressed.

### Government finances
- **Specific to BCS games:** In at least four games, players asked when government would “run out of money” and how much lead time existed before the fiscal situation became unmanageable (p. 9).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - Income-tax revenue erodes while spending demand rises (p. 9).
  - Government might be unable to fund basic services (p. 10).
  - Tax reform takes years while the economic change takes months (p. 9).

### International economic patterns
- China secures manufacturing dominance (p. 4).
- The cyberattack cripples manufacturing worldwide (p. 4).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - China threatens dominance over industrial production, supply chains and influence in the Global South (p. 10).
  - Risk of capital flight from unilateral AI taxation (p. 9).
  - Players discussed allies shifting to Chinese technology ecosystems (p. 11).

### Market pricing and scarcity
*Applies across the set:* UBI skeptics argued that it fuels inflation (p. 14). Players asked about signposts of a “post-scarcity world” (p. 14). Otherwise Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability
The scenario includes “economic collapse” (p. 4). *Applies across the set:* players warned that concentrated private power could destabilise markets (p. 12). Otherwise Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
*Applies across the set:* AI could remove “the bottom rungs of career ladders, resulting in a generation without clear pathways into professional life” (p. 7). Political ideologies assume labour distributes income and purpose (p. 15).

### Affordable essentials
*Applies across the set:* Players proposed tracking “access to basic needs” as a welfare metric (p. 14). Otherwise Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net
*Applies across the set:* Government might be unable to fund basic services, let alone an expanded safety net (p. 10). Players proposed emergency financial support (p. 8). They debated UBI as “the only mechanism capable of maintaining social stability at scale” against claims that it creates dependency, fuels inflation, is fiscally unsustainable and fails to address the functions of work (p. 14).

### Time for what matters
- **Specific to BCS games:** A “caring society” in which human connection, caregiving, education and community become the primary forms of valued activity, supported economically by AI-generated surplus (p. 14).
- *Applies across the set:* Players explored a work week of 20 hours or fewer, national service, civic engagement and care-economy expansion, and asked “How can people find meaning in a post-labor society?” (p. 14).

### A say in your future
- Governance failures and social unrest (p. 4).
- **Specific to BCS games:** Players framed the scenario as competition between states and powerful AI firms (p. 12).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - Concentrated firm power risks “capturing or undermining democratic governance” (p. 12).
  - Radicalization (p. 7).
  - Potentially authoritarian responses once a crisis hits (p. 15).
  - Players urged participatory deliberation before a crisis (p. 15).

### A fair share of progress
*Applies across the set:* Gains accrue “mainly to firms and their owners and not the broader society” (p. 12). Players proposed sovereign wealth funds, profit-sharing and similar ways to distribute gains (pp. 10, 13).

## Other dynamics
- **Cyber and loss of control:** A massive cyberattack cripples manufacturing, critical infrastructure and robotic systems worldwide. It is attributed either to a hostile state actor or to an autonomous AI that has escaped control, and players must confront “the possibility that AGI systems have become uncontrollable” (p. 4). Players faced “an increased risk of cyberattacks, and potential loss of control” (p. 4). This report does not analyse player responses to the cyberattack or to loss of control (p. 3).
- **Corporate power (BCS games):** The private sector was seen as something government must act on (p. 12).
- *Applies across the set:*
  - Some players saw nationalisation as a way to halt dangerous capability growth (p. 13).
  - Firms might move offshore or deploy AI to undermine governance (p. 13).
  - Players proposed that government be able to compel disclosure of model vulnerabilities (p. 13).
  - **Geopolitics:** players debated confrontation versus cooperation with China and proposed arms-control-style restraint (p. 11).
  - **Wellbeing:** players proposed metrics for purpose, cohesion, mental health and civic engagement (p. 14).
  - **Education:** players proposed reforms (p. 15).
- Environment, information ecosystem: Not addressed.
