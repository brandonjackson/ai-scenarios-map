# Jobs Are Lost and New Jobs Emerge — summary

*Scenario #3 of four in "How Might Fiscal Policy Respond to the Rise of Artificial Intelligence?" by Karen Dynan, Douglas Elmendorf and Louise Sheiner (Brookings Institution, August 2026), written for the Brookings–University of Utah conference on America's fiscal future. The four scenarios are layered thought experiments for US federal fiscal policy over CBO's 30-year horizon, not predictions; the authors assign no probabilities. Read from the paper's full text; see `extract.md`.*

**The scenario.** Scenario #2 plus job losses. Productivity growth rises by half a point a year, GDP ends about 16% higher by 2056 and all extra income goes to the top fifth. In addition, AI displaces workers across occupations in rolling waves, "first, perhaps, call-center workers, and then some types of lawyers, and so on". New jobs emerge, but displaced workers struggle to find ones that match their skills. Over the next decade unemployment ends a point higher and participation a point lower: about 3 million people at any time, and "many millions over the next few decades". Other labour input makes up the lost work, so GDP and the debt path are roughly as in Scenario #2: debt ends about 49 points of GDP below the baseline. The losses are spread nationwide rather than concentrated in particular regions.

## Hard work pays
Broken for the displaced. They "often did everything society asked of them in the old system, only to discover that their hard-won expertise has become yesterday's technology" (Strauss, quoted). Technology-driven job loss brings lasting earnings losses as workers move to lower-paying occupations or leave the labour force.

## Affordable essentials
Not addressed.

## Reliable safety net
Too short and too thin for this shock. Unemployment insurance pays about half of former wages, capped, for 26 weeks, misses gig and informal workers and is "too short for occupational change". Trade Adjustment Assistance, with its wage insurance and training, has lapsed for job losses after June 2022. US spending on training and employment services is under a fifth of the advanced-economy average. The authors weigh longer UI, wage insurance and better training, but none is in place.

## Responsive government
Stable, with a lag. Because the losses are spread across occupations and places, the risk of "self-reinforcing downward regional spirals" in local public finances is lower than in the China shock, and the federal budget still improves. The warning is about timing: with the China shock "much of the policy discussion occurred only after many of the shock's economic and social consequences had become deeply entrenched".

## Time for what matters
Not addressed. The health harms of job loss bear on the displaced, not on time for family and community.

## Bottom line
A moderate, China-shock-style churn without the regional concentration. The economy and the budget barely notice; a few million displaced workers at any time carry the cost, through earnings and health losses that current safety-net programmes were not built for.
