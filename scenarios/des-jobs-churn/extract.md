# Jobs are lost and new jobs emerge — extract

## Summary
AI raises average annual total factor productivity (TFP) growth in the US nonfarm business (NFB) sector from 1.1% to 1.6% over 30 years. As in Scenario #2, all extra income goes to the top quintile. In addition, AI displaces large numbers of workers across occupations over time, "first, perhaps, call-center workers, and then some types of lawyers, and so on". New jobs emerge, but displaced workers struggle to find jobs that match their skills. Over the next decade the unemployment rate ends up 1 percentage point higher and labour force participation 1 point lower, about 3 million people at any time (pp. 2, 12–13; Table 4). GDP follows the Scenario #2 path because other labour input, such as more hours or AI-driven human-capital gains, offsets the lost work. Debt is therefore roughly the same: 49 percentage points of GDP below the baseline after 30 years (pp. 13–14; Table 4). Displaced workers face large, lasting earnings and health losses. The authors weigh expanding unemployment insurance, establishing wage insurance and expanding training and employment services (pp. 14–18).

## Key details
- **Source:** How Might Fiscal Policy Respond to the Rise of Artificial Intelligence?, Karen Dynan, Douglas Elmendorf and Louise Sheiner, Brookings Institution, August 2026. The paper was written for the Brookings Institution–University of Utah conference on "The Forces Shaping America's Fiscal Future" in June 2026 (cover). `sources/dynan-elmendorf-sheiner-fiscal-ai-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text (pp. 1–39, including references). Figure 1, the chart of debt paths, is image-only in the text extraction. This scenario's debt path is not estimated separately (pp. 7, 13).
- **Scenario set:** Four illustrative scenarios: #1 A rising tide lifts all boats proportionally; #2 Income inequality increases; #3 Jobs are lost and new jobs emerge (this one); #4 Capital wins amid major permanent job displacement. They are built from three drivers: the increase in annual TFP growth (0.5 pp in #1–#3, 1 pp in #4), the reduction in the number of workers (none, none, 2%, 6%) and the distribution of the extra income (proportional to income without AI; proportional to capital and labour but all to the top quintile; the same; all to owners of capital in the top quintile). This scenario is #2 plus job losses (pp. 1–3; Table 1).
- **Geography:** United States, federal budget only (p. 1). Job losses are spread across the country rather than geographically concentrated (pp. 13–15).
- **Authors' framing:** Thought experiment and planning scenario for fiscal policy. "The scenarios are not intended to be specific predictions, but rather illustrations of the types of effects that have garnered scholarly and popular attention in recent months" (p. 1). The scenarios are additional to the AI effects already in CBO's projections (p. 1). This scenario is "somewhat analogous to the China shock of the 2000s" (p. 13).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned: "We do not attempt to assign probabilities to the different scenarios, which seems to us exceedingly difficult" (p. 1). *Applies across the set:* the scenarios "include only a subset of the possible effects of AI" (p. 3). "Immense uncertainty remains" (p. 32).

### Timeline
- Horizon: 30 years, matching CBO's long-term projections to 2056 (pp. 1, 6).
- 2026 — federal debt held by the public is 101% of GDP in CBO's extended baseline (p. 6).
- Next decade — the unemployment rate rises 1 pp and labour force participation falls 1 pp against the no-AI path. GDP is about 5% higher than without AI (pp. 10, 12).
- Over time — displacement rolls across occupations: call-center workers first, then some types of lawyers, and so on (p. 12). Job losses occur "at a moderate pace as additional TFP emerges" (p. 3).
- 2056 (after 30 years) — GDP is 16% higher, and debt is roughly 49 pp of GDP below the baseline's 175% (pp. 6, 10, 13; Table 4).
- Historical references: the China shock cost about 1.5–2 million manufacturing jobs, 2000–2007 (p. 13). TAA was established in 1962. Workers who lost jobs after 30 June 2022 are not eligible for its benefits (pp. 16–17).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Off. The scenario's budget results assume no significant changes in fiscal policy, under current law and CBO's projection methodology. Worker-support measures are discussed as options (pp. 1, 4, 13; Table 4).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Current law and CBO's projection methodology, with the authors' extended-baseline treatment of discretionary spending (pp. 5–6). Existing unemployment insurance pays about half the former wage, up to a cap, for 26 weeks (p. 15). The TAA programme has lapsed (p. 17).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *This scenario:* options, with arguments for and against (Table 4, p. 13; pp. 15–18):
  - From Scenarios #1–#2: keep budget categories closer to historical relationships with GDP, and increase progressivity through means-tested benefits or universal low-cost public services (p. 15).
  - Whether to respond at all. For: Strauss (2025) on those who "did everything society asked of them". Against: retraining may not work, targeted support may be unfair to others without suitable jobs, and support may weaken incentives to reskill and search (p. 15).
  - **Expand unemployment insurance.** It already exists and does not require determining why a job was lost (Gimbel 2025). Its limits are gaps in coverage (gig and informal workers), the need to modernise administration, 26 weeks being too short for occupational change, and capped benefits leaving large income drops. Longer or higher benefits reduce search urgency (Marinescu and Skandalis 2021), though the optimal replacement rate is higher when jobs are scarce (Landais, Michaillat and Saez 2018). Extended benefits could be conditioned on transition activities. Funding could come from existing UI taxes, all taxpayers, or higher-income taxpayers only (pp. 15–16).
  - **Establish wage insurance**, modelled on TAA's: for workers aged 50 and over, up to two years of half the gap between old and new wages. Its strengths are support beyond the short term, encouragement to get back to work, and time and amount limits. Its weaknesses are weaker incentives to find better jobs (though evidence suggests this problem is small: Hyman, Kovak and Leive 2024; Hyman et al. 2021), fairness concerns, and the difficulty of identifying AI-caused job loss. Options are eligibility for all laid-off workers or an intermediate test for structural causes (mass layoffs, declining occupational employment, large earnings losses). Funding could come from taxes on employed workers in a social-insurance model, all taxpayers, or higher earners (pp. 16–17).
  - **Expand worker training and employment services.** US public spending on these is under one-fifth of the advanced-economy average relative to GDP. The record is mixed (Katz et al. 2022; Holzer 2026), but sector-focused programmes show "substantial and persistent earning gains". Design is crucial: programmes should be tied to employers' needs, short, repeatable and responsive to worker preferences. Policymakers must decide on eligibility (AI-only or broader) and funding (pp. 17–18).

  *Applies across the set:* "policies that are robust to different scenarios would be especially valuable" (p. 1). The China shock shows the cost of waiting: much policy discussion came only after consequences "had become deeply entrenched" (p. 32). One option is a modernised form of TAA on a limited scale now, or one triggered automatically "if unemployment persisted above a preset threshold". Another is modest public equity purchases through a sovereign wealth fund (p. 32). Stronger transition systems "might be viewed by policymakers as valuable even apart from AI" (p. 32). Table 6 catalogues all responses (p. 31). Marinescu (2025) proposes TAA-like assistance plus a UBI funded by a digital-economy tax (p. 31).

## Key dynamics explored
*This scenario*

- AI displaces workers across occupations in rolling waves. New jobs emerge, but skill mismatch leaves some displaced workers in prolonged unemployment and others out of the labour force (p. 12).
- Lost labour input is assumed to be offset by other labour input, perhaps more hours or AI-driven human-capital gains. GDP and the labour share are therefore the same as in Scenarios #1–#2 (pp. 2, 13).
- Unlike the China shock, the drop in labour demand is not geographically concentrated, because affected occupations change over time and are spread nationwide. This lessens the risk of "self-reinforcing downward regional spirals" in local public finances and business confidence. It does not necessarily ease skill matching (pp. 13–15).
- Job loss from structural drops in labour demand causes persistent earnings losses (Jacobson, LaLonde and Sullivan 1993; Couch and Placzek 2010). Nearly half of the earnings decline after job loss stems from technological change, as workers move to lower-paying occupations (Braxton and Taska 2023). Technology-driven job loss brings larger long-term earnings declines than other job loss (Mei and Rindels 2026) (p. 14).
- Historical analogues: telephone operators were in lower-paying jobs or out of work a decade after mechanical switching (Feigenbaum and Gross 2024). Employment in China-shock areas rebounded mainly through new entrants, not re-employment of displaced workers (Autor et al. 2025) (p. 14).
- Job loss harms health: higher mortality for high-seniority men, visible for at least two decades (Sullivan and von Wachter 2009); more antidepressants and mental-health hospitalisations (Kuhn, Lalive and Zweimüller 2009); fatal drug overdoses (Pierce and Schott 2020); sustained mortality rises after NAFTA and the China shock, especially among working-age men (Finkelstein, Notowidigdo and Shi 2026) (p. 14).
- Adaptive capacity and AI exposure are positively correlated, but millions of highly exposed workers, many in clerical or administrative roles, would struggle to transition (Manning and Aguirre 2026) (p. 15).
- Training effects fade partly because technological change keeps shifting labour demand (Hyman 2022). Effective reskilling needs targeting to occupations that fit workers' identity, and clear information on programme quality (Delfino et al. 2026) (p. 18).

*Shared with Scenarios #1–#2 (repeated here)*

- Faster NFB TFP growth (NFB is about 75% of the economy) adds 0.38 pp directly to annual GDP growth (p. 4). It slightly raises revenue relative to GDP and markedly lowers non-interest spending relative to GDP under current law and CBO's method. It shrinks existing debt relative to GDP and raises interest rates (pp. 5–6).
- The upward income shift raises personal income tax, lowers payroll tax and keeps more people eligible for benefits. The extra GDP is taxed at the top quintile's 25.9% average rate (p. 11).
- The improvement "may prove at least partly illusory" if policymakers keep historical budget-to-GDP relationships (p. 8).

*Applies across the set*

- The pace of disruption matters. In Scenarios #3 and #4 job losses come at a moderate pace. "Faster changes would be more disruptive and would increase public pressure for policy responses" (p. 3).
- Ramsey-style analysis gives no clear deficit guidance in any scenario (pp. 8–9).

## State of technology
AI adds 0.5 pp to annual NFB TFP growth beyond the 0.1 pp in CBO's projections for the coming decade (pp. 1, 12). AI can perform work across many occupations over time, from call-center work to some legal work (p. 12). AI might improve human capital, offsetting lost labour input (pp. 2, 13). Providers, market structure and pricing are not addressed. *Applies across the set:* an AI boom might spur investment in computing capacity and energy (p. 8).

## State of adoption
Displacement spreads across occupations and places over time rather than hitting one region or industry (pp. 12–13). Job losses come at a moderate pace as TFP rises (p. 3). *Applies across the set:* a low-impact case driven by public resistance is not explored. Pew (2025) found 50% of Americans more concerned than excited, 10% the opposite (p. 3).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
NFB TFP growth averages 1.6% a year instead of 1.1%. GDP follows the Scenario #2 path: about 5% higher after a decade and 16% after three (pp. 2, 10, 12). In an alternative version, lost labour input is not offset and GDP is lower than in Scenario #2 (p. 14).

### Labour market dynamics
Over the next decade the unemployment rate ends up 1 pp higher and the participation rate 1 pp lower than otherwise (p. 12). The number of workers falls by 2%, about 3 million people at any given time out of a labour force of roughly 170 million, and many millions over the next few decades. The China shock cost about 1.5–2 million manufacturing jobs in 2000–2007 (p. 13; Table 1). Displaced workers struggle to find jobs matching their skills (p. 12). Those who remain employed supply more labour input (p. 2). The labour share is unchanged (p. 13). Highly exposed workers who would struggle to move include many in clerical or administrative roles (p. 15).

### Winners and losers
The top quintile receives all the extra income (p. 13). Displaced workers "face large obstacles both professionally and personally": lasting earnings losses, moves to lower-paying occupations or leaving work, and health harms (Table 4; pp. 14–15). Affected occupations include call-center workers and some types of lawyers (p. 12). Older workers aged 50 and over were the target group for TAA wage insurance (p. 16).

### Household incomes and living standards
Incomes outside the top quintile are not raised by AI (p. 13). Displaced workers suffer persistent earnings declines, and UI recipients still see significant income drops, especially higher earners hitting benefit caps (pp. 14–15).

### Government finances
- Because GDP and the labour share match Scenario #2, the debt path is "roughly the same" and is not estimated separately. Table 4 shows debt reduced by 49% of GDP after 30 years against the extended baseline, which rises from 101% of GDP in 2026 to 175% (pp. 6, 13; Table 4).
- A more precise estimate would include higher means-tested spending from the forgone earnings of the unemployed and non-participants (pp. 13–14).
- Alternative version: if lost labour input lowers GDP, debt would not decline as much relative to GDP (p. 14).
- Responses have budget costs: expanding UI, wage insurance or training must be funded through UI taxes, social-insurance taxes on employed workers, taxes on all taxpayers or higher-income taxpayers only (pp. 16–18). Broad wage-insurance eligibility "would increase budgetary cost" (p. 17).
- Shared with Scenarios #1–#2: the Scenario #1 decomposition (30 pp spending, 2 pp revenue, 7 pp GDP and interest), with a further 12% of GDP debt reduction if rates were unchanged (pp. 7–8). The top-quintile tax rate of 25.9% versus 20.6% overall, and a one-third smaller Medicaid/CHIP reduction (p. 11). Discretionary spending falls from 6.1% of GDP (2025) to 4.5% (2056) (p. 6).
- *Applies across the set:* even at face value, "federal debt would continue to rise significantly relative to GDP in the scenarios we analyzed" (p. 30).

### International economic patterns
US public spending on training and employment services is less than one-fifth of the advanced-economy average relative to GDP (OECD 2026) (pp. 17–18). The scenario is compared with the China trade shock (p. 13). Otherwise not addressed. The authors exclude international effects (p. 1).

### Market pricing and scarcity
Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability
Not addressed for this scenario. *Applies across the set:* interest rates rise by roughly the TFP increment in CBO's analysis, with uncertainty (p. 8).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Strauss (2025): "the people most likely to be left behind [by automation] often did everything society asked of them in the old system, only to discover that their hard-won expertise has become yesterday's technology" (p. 15). Displaced workers often move to lower-paying occupations (p. 14).

### Affordable essentials
Not addressed for this scenario.

### Reliable safety net
Unemployment insurance gives about half of former wages, capped, for 26 weeks. It excludes some workers, such as gig and informal workers, and is too short for occupational transitions (pp. 15–16). TAA's wage insurance, training and extended benefits lapsed for job losses after 30 June 2022 (pp. 16–17). Options to strengthen support are set out above (pp. 15–18). Some argue AI-specific support would be unfair to other jobless people (p. 15).

### Time for what matters
Not addressed for this scenario. *Applies across the set:* the leisure-choice alternative is not analysed (p. 4).

### A say in your future
Faster disruption would increase public pressure for policy responses (p. 3). With the China shock, policy discussion came only after consequences were entrenched (p. 32). Many Americans' concerns about AI "might be assuaged to some degree if certain policy responses were initiated now" (p. 32).

### A fair share of progress
Gains go to the top quintile, while displaced workers bear concentrated losses (pp. 13–14). *Applies across the set:* the authors expect "considerable pressure to ensure that gains from AI would be shared broadly across society, in terms of both incomes and opportunities for meaningful work" (pp. 30–31).

## Other dynamics
Health: job loss raises mortality, mental-health problems and drug overdoses, with effects lasting up to two decades (p. 14). Regional: dispersed shocks reduce the risk of local downward spirals in public finances and business confidence (pp. 14–15). The paper excludes the environment, social interactions, national security, AI regulation and other countries (p. 1). *Applies across the set:* alternatives not explored are small AI effects, AI reducing inequality, and the leisure case (pp. 3–4).
