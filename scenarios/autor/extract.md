# Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs — extract

## Summary

AI could make some decision-making tasks accessible to workers who already have relevant training and experience, expanding the set of valuable functions that middle-skill workers perform (pp. 2, 8–13). Workers use AI guidance to complement their judgement in medicine, skilled trades, software and other occupations, while continuing to handle unusual cases and exercise responsibility (pp. 8–13). Some occupations and skills still lose value and others emerge. Which effects dominate depends on deployment choices by institutions, workers and firms (pp. 14–16).

## Key details

- **Source:** Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs, David Autor, National Bureau of Economic Research, 2024-02-12. `sources/autor-rebuild-middle-class-jobs-2024/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** [AI Could Actually Help Rebuild The Middle Class](https://www.noemamag.com/how-ai-could-help-rebuild-the-middle-class/)
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** Standalone
- **Geography:** United States as the main case, with implications for industrialised countries (pp. 1–2, 14–15).
- **Authors' framing:** Conditional scenario and prescription (p. 15, “A Scenario, Not a Forecast”).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** A possible opportunity. Autor does not estimate its probability (pp. 15–16).

### Timeline

No dates for the scenario. The historical framing traces artisanal work through industrial mass expertise and the subsequent computer-era rise in the value of elite judgement (pp. 3–7).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Mixed. The opportunity depends on choices by public and private institutions (pp. 15–16).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Workers have relevant expertise, employers organise AI around complementarity and permission to perform some work can extend to less-credentialled practitioners (pp. 8–13, 15–16).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Guide deployment toward wider access to skilled decision-making and restore the market value of human expertise. Choices by governments, industry, universities, worker organisations and individuals can all shape use (pp. 15–16).

## Key dynamics explored

- Industrialisation broke artisanal work into standardised components and supported mass middle-skill expertise. Conventional computing automated explicit rules and raised the relative value of expert judgement while hollowing out many procedural jobs (pp. 3–7).
- Statistical AI learns rules and patterns from examples. Real-time assistance can blend prior experience, information and suggested decisions, so a worker with sound foundational expertise can tackle a broader range of cases (pp. 7–9, 12–13).
- Expertise remains valuable when unforeseen cases require interpretation, accountability and skills acquired through practice. Blind reliance can amplify bad advice and erase performance gains outside a tool’s capabilities (pp. 10–13).
- Innovation may create new goods, work and expertise while automating other tasks. The workers displaced need not be the workers who benefit (pp. 11, 14–16).

## State of technology

AI provides real-time pattern recognition, advice and synthesis. It can fail outside the range where it has been tested and does not remove the importance of tacit and physical expertise. Capable, inexpensive robotics in unpredictable real-world environments remains distant in the author’s assessment (pp. 7–13).

## State of adoption

Applications include nurse practitioners handling some primary care with clinical protocols and judgement as a pre-AI analogy, customer-support chat assistance, coding assistance, document work and expert help with unfamiliar procedures. Adoption and value depend on reliability, the relevance of user expertise, institutional rules and workflow choices (pp. 8–13, 15).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

The author discusses productive gains from decision support and contrasts possible new products and services with simple replication of existing tasks. No GDP forecast is offered (pp. 9–11, 14–16).

### Labour market dynamics

AI can broaden access to high-value judgement for workers with a suitable skills base, especially in middle-skill roles. It also automates tasks and eliminates or changes roles. A more immediate challenge in ageing industrial economies is shortage of workers, while the threat from poorly directed AI is the devaluation of expertise and labour’s bargaining position (pp. 1–2, 8–16).

### Winners and losers

Less-experienced call-centre workers and workers with initially lower task performance gained most in cited studies. Nurses and technicians could expand their remit, while generic workers and patent owners would have unequal stakes under a future in which human expertise is devalued (pp. 8–11, 15).

### Household incomes and living standards

Middle-class employment can expand if market rewards for skilled labour are spread to more workers. If expertise becomes economically irrelevant, wealth could accrue largely to AI patent owners (pp. 2, 15–16).

### International economic patterns

Rich countries face labour shortages amid ageing. Institutional choices can differ across countries, and the article notes the export of surveillance systems between autocratic governments (pp. 1, 15).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

The opportunity is for people with relevant foundations and practical expertise to gain access to better-paid decision-making responsibilities, expanding the middle class (pp. 2, 8–13, 16).

### Affordable essentials

Decision support could extend the reach of medical expertise. The nurse-practitioner example shows how access to routine primary care can expand when suitably trained practitioners take on some services (pp. 8–9, 13).

### Reliable safety net

Not addressed.

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Institutional choices alter political power and civil rights. The paper describes surveillance and suppression of dissent as possible uses, and concentrated AI ownership as a danger if human expertise loses economic value (p. 15).

### A fair share of progress

Spreading access to expertise can distribute economic opportunity more widely, with policy and institutional choices shaping the result (pp. 15–16).

## Other dynamics

AI may support customised tutoring, translation and medical discovery, or enable state surveillance and repression (p. 15).
