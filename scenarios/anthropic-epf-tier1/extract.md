# Tier 1: Baseline economy (~5% unemployment) — extract

## Summary
At about 5% unemployment “the headline numbers look normal”, but underneath the labour market “may be churning faster than workers can adapt” as AI automates tasks across occupations at once and “steadily compress[es] the value of skills that workers spent years acquiring” (p. 7). The tier’s purpose is to “Prepare for disruption and ensure families share in AI’s upside” (p. 7). Its central measure is universal pre-distributive capital accounts, seeded before disruption is visible so they have compounded by the time they are needed (p. 8). Alongside them come wage insurance, occupational licensing reform, retention tax incentives, workforce training grants and job-matching infrastructure, which make it easier for workers to move into new roles and buy time (pp. 9–10). The source describes the state of the world in this tier only briefly; most of the section is policy.

## Key details
- **Source:** Anthropic’s Economic Policy Framework (PDF title page; running footer “A policy framework for AI’s impact on work”), Anthropic, Anthropic, June 2026. `sources/anthropic-economic-policy-framework-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** Anthropic’s web summary page, “Policy on the AI Exponential” / “Anthropic’s Economic Policy Framework” (`private/web-summary.txt`), restates the tiers and announces linked funding commitments. This is a separate publication from The Anthropic Institute’s “Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI” (Korinek, Jones, Sacher, Cotter and McCrory, Working Paper No. 2026-02, September 2026), which is already on the map as `anthropic-modest`, `anthropic-substantial` and `anthropic-extreme`. The two use different scenario sets: the Institute paper uses modelled capability and adoption paths, while this framework uses policy tiers set by unemployment level. *[Inference: the 5% / 10% / unprecedented tiers do not map one-to-one onto Modest / Substantial / Extreme, and neither source says they do.]*
- **Reading notes:** Full text of the 14-page PDF (pp. 1–14) and the web summary page. Page locators use the PDF’s printed page numbers; p. 1 is the cover page. The text extraction contains tables (pp. 9–11), which were read in full. Page 7 says measures that could entrench the link between income and employment are flagged “below”, but no explicit flags appear in the extracted text. *[Inference: any flag may have been a visual marker lost in extraction, or it may be the convergence-to-a-common-floor discussion on p. 12.]*
- **Scenario set:** Three tiers, set by “the severity of AI-driven labor market disruption, using the unemployment rate as the primary trigger” (p. 6): Tier 1, Baseline economy (~5% unemployment with churn); Tier 2, Recession-level disruption (~10% unemployment); Tier 3, Transformative disruption (unemployment exceeding historical peaks) (pp. 7, 10, 12). A second dimension runs across the tiers without defining separate scenarios: whether disruption is “a temporary shock, after which the labor market re-stabilizes” or “an enduring restructuring, in which the demand for human labor is significantly and persistently lower” (p. 7). Each tier “buys time for workers and the economy to adapt, and eventually judge whether the interventions in the next tier are needed” (p. 4).
- **Geography:** United States. The authors say “the principles underpinning this framework are intended to be global”, and the right policy mix in any country “will turn on its labor law, social contract, and where models are developed” (p. 4).
- **Authors' framing:** Prescription and planning scenario. Tiers are contingencies for calibrating policy, not forecasts: “We cannot know today which scenario will unfold, but we believe this uncertainty warrants preparation… our policy recommendations anchor on three possible scenarios” (p. 6). The framework separates “the steps that make sense now in almost any scenario from the responses worth having ready if conditions worsen, and from the harder questions where research and dialogue should inform decisions” (p. 3).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned to any tier. “We can’t forecast with confidence where AI capabilities will be even a few years from now” (p. 3). The web summary says that if AI delivers “even a fraction of its potential… it’s likely that AI will have acted as a general substitute for labor” (web summary). *[Inference: the tier is called the “Baseline economy”, and the cover page says the US is “still early in AI’s adoption” (p. 1). This suggests Tier 1 is close to current conditions, but the source does not say so outright.]*

### Timeline
No horizon year is given.
- 1970 — Extended Benefits UI programme established (p. 11). This is context for later tiers.
- Recent years — entry-level workers in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen weaker employment growth (p. 1).
- June 2026 — framework published. Recommendations are calibrated to “AI capabilities that exist today or will arrive within roughly the next year” (p. 3).
- Already in place — the federal government has “new capital accounts seeded at birth” (p. 8). The web summary says these accounts “can hold only index funds—not a stake in AI companies” (web summary).
- Sequencing — capital accounts are proposed in Tier 1 because “it must start before disruption is visible. Accounts compound; the earlier they are seeded, the more they are worth when they are needed” (p. 8).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** On. The tier is defined as a trigger for a set of government responses, and the whole document prescribes policy.
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The tiers set out what governments should do, not what they do inside a story. Existing policies the text treats as current: federal capital accounts seeded at birth (p. 8; per the web summary, limited to index funds); state-run Unemployment Insurance on legacy systems (pp. 5–6); and the Extended Benefits programme (p. 11).
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - *This tier:*
    - **Universal pre-distributive capital accounts** (p. 8). Make the existing birth-seeded accounts permanent and expand them towards universal coverage, giving initial priority to the cohorts most exposed to near-term AI disruption. This includes:
      - *Phased eligibility expansion* to young adults entering the workforce and to incumbent workers in the most AI-exposed occupations, with “a defined path to coverage for all working-age adults”.
      - *Flexible withdrawal for workforce transitions*: retraining, relocation, credentialing and other transition expenses.
      - *Equity funding*: expand how accounts are funded, “including with equity in AI companies, so that beneficiaries share directly in nearer-term gains from AI-driven growth”. The web summary describes this as expanding “eligibility and permitted holdings”.
      - Caveat: “this measure does not cushion near-term displacement”. The accounts are “slow-compounding and cannot alone compensate for permanent job loss that happens soon—they must be paired with the income support and transition mechanisms in Tiers 2 and 3” (p. 8).
    - **Wage insurance** (p. 9). Time-limited, capped, government-funded supplemental income for workers who take lower-paying jobs after displacement. US evidence suggests it is self-funding through avoided UI payments and higher income tax. Evidence from Canada and Germany is “less encouraging”.
    - **Occupational licensing reform** (p. 9). Federal funding for states that make licensed occupations easier to enter, such as by recognising licences from other states or shortening the path to qualification, “without weakening the rules on what licensed workers may do or who supervises them”.
    - **Retention tax incentives** (p. 9). Multi-year tax credits for companies that retain workers and redeploy them into substantially different roles when adopting AI. These can reduce distributional harm and preserve employment, but “can also prop up low-productivity firms”. Design must deal with noncompliance and fraud.
    - **Workforce training grants** (p. 10). Grants for employer-connected sectoral training programmes that place workers into roles in strong current demand.
    - **Job matching infrastructure** (p. 10). Investment in “AI-powered skills-based labor market platforms, Learning and Employment Records, and skills wallets”. Evaluations find “modest effects on reemployment”.
  - *Applies across the set (foundational measures, “valuable no matter what happens”, web summary; pp. 5–6):*
    - **Measurement.** Expanded investment in statistical agencies; sustained funding for AI-specific survey instruments; and reporting requirements for AI labs and firms, “including Anthropic”, on deployment patterns and workforce effects. Metrics should be directly observable, such as AI usage rates, intensity of use, and worker productivity by level of AI adoption (p. 5).
    - **A government unit focused on tracking AI’s effects.** A small dedicated unit to track AI sector by sector, stress-test institutions and flag early signals that should trigger a response, modelled on the Council of Economic Advisers created for the post-war transition (p. 5).
    - **Delivery infrastructure.** Modernise UI systems to process claims faster, flex quickly when benefits change, and run real-time identity and cross-state fraud matching (pp. 5–6). Countries should invest now in “the rails required to expand and adjust coverage quickly”, including revenue collection and digital public infrastructure: identity, payments and data exchange (p. 6).
    - **Broader signals.** Watch labour force participation, underemployment, wages and labour’s share of national income alongside unemployment (p. 6).
    - **Pace of rollout.** If AI becomes a general substitute for labour and disruption reaches “the levels described in Tier 2”, the authors would support firm-level regulations and incentives that manage the pace of displacement, applied uniformly (p. 4).
    - **Phase-out.** Some Tier 1 and Tier 2 tools would be phased out if conditions move toward Tier 3, because some near-term measures “could make matters worse in an enduring restructuring by entrenching the link between income and employment” (p. 7).
    - **Firm conduct.** Firms should build training into deployment, decide in advance how freed-up capacity will be used “rather than defaulting to headcount reductions”, retrain and redeploy people, and redesign early-career roles around AI (p. 3). “Voluntary action isn’t a substitute for a government response that holds companies to the same standard” (web summary).

## Key dynamics explored
*This tier*
- Normal-looking headline unemployment can hide fast churn, because AI automates tasks across many occupations at the same time and compresses the value of skills that took years to acquire (p. 7).
- Capital accounts must be seeded early because they compound, so their value builds before disruption is visible. By the same logic, they cannot offset job loss that comes soon (p. 8).
- Wage insurance can pay for itself in the US because it reduces nonemployment and raises employment, lowering UI payments and raising income-tax receipts (p. 9).
- Licensing requirements reduce employment and raise prices without net welfare gains, so easing entry helps displaced workers move into new occupations (p. 9).
- Retention credits can preserve employment and reduce distributional harm, but they risk propping up low-productivity firms and attracting fraud (p. 9).
- Short sectoral training linked to employers produces significant income gains, while traditional government retraining shows mixed evidence (p. 10).

*Applies across the set*
- Interventions serve stability (income support and transfers that cushion the shock and sustain demand), adaptability (moving people toward durable work, easing transitions, slowing displacement), or both. “As disruption increases, the balance shifts from adaptability toward income support and redistribution” (p. 7).
- A temporary shock calls for temporary supports withdrawn as the labour market recovers. An enduring restructuring calls for more permanent responses (p. 7).
- If AI becomes a general substitute for labour, “realistic combinations of retraining, job matching, and mobility policy may fail to deliver a stable, full-employment equilibrium” (p. 4). Economic theory then suggests rollout “should be steered, or sequenced”. This must happen across the whole field, because “any firm that slows down alone simply cedes the market to those that do not” (p. 4).
- Firms can use AI to produce the same output with fewer people, or to do more with the same people by building new products, serving new markets and raising the capability of less-experienced workers (p. 3).
- “The economy has real capacity to adapt, but adaptation is not automatic” (p. 3).

## State of technology
*This tier:* “AI is automating tasks across occupations simultaneously” (p. 7).

*Applies across the set:* Recommendations are calibrated to the broad diffusion of capabilities “that exist today or will arrive within roughly the next year” (p. 3). If capabilities keep advancing, “AI may become a general substitute for human labor” (p. 1). AI could generate growth “on a scale the modern world hasn’t seen” (p. 1), and the web summary speaks of “decades of scientific progress compressed into years” (web summary). AI-powered job-matching platforms are proposed as a policy tool (p. 10). Market structure, number of providers, open versus closed models, compute and energy constraints, and pricing are not addressed. “AI companies” and “AI labs” are mentioned as a class (pp. 5, 8, 14).

## State of adoption
*This tier:* Not addressed beyond automation across occupations (p. 7).

*Applies across the set:* “We’re still early in AI’s adoption” (p. 1). Claude is used more in the US than in any other country (p. 4). Pace and diffusion are uncertain (web summary). Firms choose whether to deploy for headcount reduction or for expanded output, and Anthropic says it provides training and enablement to business customers and tools to measure workforce fluency (pp. 3–4). Measurement of AI usage rates and intensity of use is proposed (p. 5).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
*This tier:* Capital accounts are proposed “at the moment when AI is fueling growth” (p. 8).
*Applies across the set:* AI could generate economic growth “on a scale the modern world hasn’t seen”. “The central challenge isn’t how to stimulate growth; it is making sure the gains are widely shared” (p. 1). Economists’ models range “from modest productivity-driven growth to scenarios where economic output doubles but wages collapse” (p. 1).

### Labour market dynamics
*This tier:* Unemployment is about 5% “with churn” (p. 7). Headline numbers look normal, but churn may outpace workers’ ability to adapt, and the value of acquired skills is steadily compressed (p. 7). Displaced workers may take lower-paying jobs, which is the case wage insurance addresses (p. 9). About 24% of US workers hold a certification or licence (p. 9). Incumbent workers in the most AI-exposed occupations and young adults entering the workforce are named as priority cohorts (p. 8).
*Applies across the set:* Entry-level workers in the most AI-exposed occupations have already seen weaker employment growth (p. 1). People may keep their jobs while “the pay, security, and quality of that work erode” (p. 6).

### Winners and losers
*This tier:* Workers whose skills took years to acquire see their value compressed (p. 7). Cohorts most exposed to near-term disruption are incumbent workers in exposed occupations and new labour-market entrants (p. 8). Account holders with AI-company equity “share directly in nearer-term gains” (p. 8).
*Applies across the set:* The gains could concentrate narrowly or be broadly shared, depending on choices made “in the next few years” (web summary).

### Household incomes and living standards
*This tier:* The framing is “ensure families share in AI’s upside” (p. 7). Wage insurance tops up the incomes of re-employed displaced workers (p. 9). Sectoral training shows “significant income gains for participants” (p. 10).
*Applies across the set:* “Our goal should be more than economic stability. It should be a society where people can live well” (web summary).

### Government finances
*This tier:* Wage insurance is government-funded but, on US evidence, offset by avoided UI payments and higher income tax (p. 9). Licensing reform uses federal funding to states (p. 9). Retention incentives are multi-year tax credits, with noncompliance and fraud risks (p. 9).
*Applies across the set:* Investment in statistical agencies and UI delivery systems (pp. 5–6). Legacy UI systems paid out “billions of dollars worth of improper payments” during the pandemic (p. 6).

### International economic patterns
*This tier:* Wage-insurance evidence from Canada and Germany is less encouraging than US evidence (p. 9). Job-recommendation evaluations in “a number of countries” find modest effects (p. 10).
*Applies across the set:* Only 52% of the world’s population is covered by any social protection scheme. In many countries, delivery systems “cannot absorb a fast-moving labor market shock” (p. 6). The authors hope to discuss the framework at the G7 and the AI Summit in Geneva (web summary).

### Market pricing and scarcity
*This tier:* Licensing requirements “raise prices without corresponding net welfare gains” (p. 9).
*Applies across the set:* Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability
Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
*This tier:* AI is “steadily compressing the value of skills that workers spent years acquiring” (p. 7).
*Applies across the set:* “There is dignity in work. We should help people find work wherever we can, and society should keep searching for ways to remain at full employment” (p. 2).

### Affordable essentials
Not addressed for this tier.

### Reliable safety net
*This tier:* Wage insurance supports displaced workers who take lower-paid jobs (p. 9). Capital accounts allow withdrawals for transition costs but “do not cushion near-term displacement” (p. 8).
*Applies across the set:* UI “is not sufficiently prepared for a large labor market shock”. Legacy state systems “buckled under pandemic claim volumes” and took months to implement emergency extensions (pp. 5–6).

### Time for what matters
*This tier:* Not addressed.
*Applies across the set:* “People are more than their jobs. Over a longer horizon, AI may move society toward a world in which people work far less, or in which work carries a different meaning than it does today” (p. 2).

### A say in your future
*This tier:* Not addressed.
*Applies across the set:* “These proposals will be stronger if workers help shape them.” Anthropic says it will engage workers, unions, small business owners and worker organisations directly (web summary).

### A fair share of progress
*This tier:* Capital accounts are meant to “give every American a direct financial stake in the economy at the moment when AI is fueling growth”, including through equity in AI companies (p. 8).
*Applies across the set:* The central challenge is “making sure the gains are widely shared” (p. 1).

## Other dynamics
*Applies across the set:*
- The framework is published alongside Anthropic’s Advanced AI Framework on governing increasingly capable systems (web summary).
- Anthropic announces $350 million in linked investments: a $200 million Economic Futures Research Fund for policy trials and evaluation, and a $150 million national fellowship programme for early-career people extending AI’s benefits to communities (web summary).
- Anthropic contributes data through the Anthropic Economic Index but notes that “data from a single company cannot tell the whole story” (p. 5).
- Values: “We are not seeking job displacement. We are working to prevent or minimize it” (p. 2).
- The framework is “necessarily incomplete, and the pace of AI development may render some of these proposals inadequate before they can be implemented” (web summary).
- Safety, loss of control, geopolitics, culture, health, environment and the information ecosystem are not addressed in this document.
