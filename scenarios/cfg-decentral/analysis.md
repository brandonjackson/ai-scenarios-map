# Decentralised Mayhem — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Policy off until a crisis forces reactive policy.** No coordination steers the spread of capability. Governments act only after harm, with blunt instruments (bans on high-scale open models). That is policy as damage control, not design.

## Big assumptions
1. **Open weights stay near the frontier.** Fast capability growth happens across many actors rather than in one lab, which assumes compute isn't the binding constraint (or is cheap and widely available) and that leading labs keep releasing weights.
2. **Defence lags offence.** Guardrails on released models can be stripped, and automated attack capability outpaces defensive tools.
3. **Diffusion means displacement.** Cheap, widespread capability replaces work faster than new uses create it (compare Toloui's Disruption Swarm).
4. **Decentralisation is unstable.** CFG's framework suggests fast progress either needs centralised control or creates containment problems that push toward it.

## What must be true for this to occur
- Efficiency improvements let open models track the frontier despite export controls and the cost of compute.
- No major jurisdiction restricts frontier open-weight releases until after a serious incident.
- Capability crosses dangerous thresholds (autonomous cyber offence, persuasive disinformation at scale) while still widely accessible.
- Governments lack verification or monitoring tools short of outright bans.

## Tensions and weak points
- **Naming (fixed) and source gap:** CFG's report has no scenario called "Decentralized Takeoff", the map's old name. The CSV now uses "Decentralised Mayhem", the closest published narrative. CFG places that ending under *Plateau*, where the misuse comes from security failure rather than runaway capability, so the map's coordinates for this point (strong replacement) are an inference, not CFG's.
- The essentials gain (cheap open AI) and the government loss (misuse) come from the same openness. The scenario is a trade-off, not a destination.
- Compare Buterin's d/acc, which argues that decentralised *defensive* technology can tip the offence/defence balance. This scenario assumes it doesn't.
