# Decentralised Mayhem — extract

## Summary

After a period of weak frontier returns and uneven adoption, a large 2027 run produces a more reliable agent. A capable open-weight rival follows and initially attracts developers, startups and hobbyists (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027). Misuse then spreads, disrupting smaller firms and governments and eroding trust in digital news and services. The US, EU and China restrict large open-weight releases, while businesses must buy defensive products from closed providers and governments invest in collective resilience (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2028–29).

## Key details

- **Source:** Advanced AI: Possible futures, Bengüsu Özcan, Daan Juijn, Jakob Graabak, Sam Bogerd, Centre for Future Generations, 2025-07-10. `sources/cfg-advanced-ai-possible-futures-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** The publisher’s scenario PDF and companion methodology, technical context and governance analysis are listed in the source manifest.
- **Reading notes:** Full publisher scenario text, narrative PDF, and companion methodology and governance analysis.
- **Scenario set:** The study crosses speed of capability progress with concentration of frontier development. The five core narratives are Plateau, Big AI, Diplomacy, Arms Race, and Take-off, each with two alternative endings (“How to navigate the scenarios?”; narrative PDF §1). This is Plateau’s second ending. The sibling ending, A Bright Winter, appears in the catalogue’s Plateau row.
- **Geography:** Global, with particular attention to the US, China and Europe (scenario narratives).
- **Authors' framing:** Plausible foresight scenarios for stress-testing policy under uncertainty, constructed from expert consultation and structured identification of key parameters. The numbers for training compute and R&D automation are directional (“Key Takeaways”; “AI futures in the numbers”; Methodology).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned. The authors selected plausible, deliberately diverse cases for decision-making (“Key Takeaways”; “How to navigate the scenarios?”).

### Timeline

- Mid-2025 to mid-2027 — specialized uses grow as data, reliability and capital constraints slow general progress (Plateau, main path).
- Mid-to-late 2027 — FrontierAI’s long-horizon agent succeeds. Four months later OmniAI releases a competitive open-weight model with limited access to agent tooling and added safeguards (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027).
- Late 2027 to early 2028 — safeguards fail, misuse spreads, and organizations and platforms experience major disruptions. Protests broaden to the industry (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027–28).
- Early to mid-2028 — spurred by costly incidents and weak coordination, NATO and EU institutions receive wider cyber-response roles. The US, EU and China adopt harmonized release restrictions (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2028).
- Mid-2028 to mid-2029 — closed-model cyber defence becomes a commercial market. Public authorities invest in critical supplies and industrial and digital resilience (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2028–29).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Mixed. A permissive opening gives way to emergency public intervention and restrictions (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** NATO expands incident coordination, the EU gives ENISA intervention powers, and the US, EU and China prohibit open publication beyond a compute threshold. Authorities also make resilience investments and educate citizens about AI-enabled scams and misleading information (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2028–29).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* Stress-test policy across different trajectories. Invest in adaptive labour-market and education institutions, information resilience and cyber defence. Coordinate among institutions and countries and develop credible verification for international agreements. Build scalable safeguards alongside innovation rather than relying solely on access restrictions (“Key Takeaways”; governance analysis §§2–5 and conclusion).

## Key dynamics explored

- The new model’s longer-horizon reliability revives investor excitement and accelerates diffusion through talent movements and open competitors (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027).
- EU authorities hesitate to restrict open weights because domestic providers are weak and local models support public-service integration, privacy and healthcare under demographic pressure (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, late 2027).
- Once widely circulated, model weights are hard to withdraw. Misuse and information pollution provoke backlash, harden regulation and create demand for closed defensive systems (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027–29).

## State of technology

Specialized reasoning and agents advance in verifiable domains such as maths and coding. General agents remain costly and unreliable over long workflows. The story attributes plateauing performance to constrained data, costly synthetic data, weak verifier generalization and investment drying up. Distillation and local inference bring useful models to phones and laptops, and grassroots groups adapt open weights to local languages and niche applications (Plateau, “Baseline assumptions”; “Mid 2025 – early 2026”; “Early 2026 – mid 2027”). In the alternative ending the large run improves reliability over longer tasks. A competitive open-weight release quickly follows, but its protection against misuse fails. The methodology assumes above-trend 2029 compute investment for defensive closed models (“Decentralised mayhem”; Methodology, Appendix 2).

## State of adoption

Integration beyond simple tasks needs customization, security reviews and staff training, often taking years. Early uses include e-commerce testing, university AI tutors, financial reconciliation, game development, repetitive form checking and government use of local models. Coding agents become routine by mid-2026. Global inference demand rises in spite of weaker investment in training (Plateau, “Baseline assumptions”; “Mid 2025 – early 2026”; “Early 2026 – mid 2027”). Advanced open weights spread among developers, SMEs and hobbyists. Public services use local open models to protect sensitive data. Later, persistent security risks prompt SMEs to pay for defensive tools from closed providers (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027–29).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Not addressed.

### Labour market dynamics

The common Plateau path describes professionals using specialized agents in accounting, games and software. The alternative ending focuses on misuse, business interruption and a defensive market, without projecting employment or wages (Plateau, main path and “Decentralised mayhem”).

### Winners and losers

Providers of closed defensive AI gain a lucrative market. Smaller firms, local governments and operators lacking strong defences incur disruption and costs. Technically adept younger users adapt faster than older adults to the deteriorating information environment (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027–29).

### Household incomes and living standards

Not addressed.

### Government finances

Public authorities increase investment in digital infrastructure, cybersecurity and critical supply chains. The story describes billions of dollars of collateral damage from containing a U.S. incident, without a public/private cost breakdown (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2028–29).

### International economic patterns

U.S.–China trade tensions increase US data-centre costs. Europe uses the slowdown to invest in catch-up, including multibillion-euro projects backed in some cases by Middle Eastern private capital. One EU member’s leading company nearly matches US and Chinese leaders by end-2025. The AI arms race framing recedes during 2026–27 as governments lose confidence that AI delivers a decisive strategic edge (Plateau, “Mid 2025 – early 2026”; “Early 2026 – mid 2027”). Major powers often tolerate incidents against rivals, Western participation dominates a subsequent summit, and coordinated restrictions coexist with weak broader diplomatic cooperation (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2028).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Cybersecurity becomes a continuing business expense. Closed providers price tools in a market created by damage from unsafe releases, and smaller buyers face persistent threats (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2028–29).

### Financial markets and stability

The top AI chipmaker’s shares rise 20% on the improved 2027 frontier agent, reigniting investment enthusiasm (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, mid-to-late 2027).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Not addressed.

### Affordable essentials

Governments see open-weight systems as useful for public-service integration, sensitive data protection and rising healthcare needs. Retailers, logistics and municipal services suffer disruptions during the misuse wave (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027–28).

### Reliable safety net

Not addressed.

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Misleading content and cybercrime erode trust, spur protests and create a generation gap in ability to navigate digital services. Governments respond to public anger with enforcement, education and institutional changes (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027–29).

### A fair share of progress

Open weights initially enable broad access and local experimentation. Later restrictions and reliance on closed defence tools redirect gains to a narrower group of providers (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”, 2027–29).

## Other dynamics

*Applies across the set.* The authors argue that even existing AI capabilities can disrupt labour, education, media and political institutions if widely deployed. Resistance can impede beneficial uses. The pace of R&D automation, control of supply chains and the ability to verify safety commitments are critical uncertainties (“Key Takeaways”; governance analysis §§1–5). Public perceptions blur the distinction between open and closed models and the eventual equilibrium relies on vigilance (Plateau, “Decentralised mayhem”).
