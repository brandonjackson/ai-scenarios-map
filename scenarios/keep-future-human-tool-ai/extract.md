# Keep the Future Human: Tool AI — extract

## Summary

Governments coordinate limits on the most autonomous, general and capable AI systems. Developers redirect investment to powerful but controllable tools, including narrow expert systems, passive systems and combinations of tools with oversight and verifiable boundaries (pp. 37–49). These tools can expand science, medicine and public capabilities, while major labour displacement and concentration still require policy responses (pp. 49–56).

## Key details

- **Source:** Keep the Future Human: Why and How We Should Close the Gates to AGI and Superintelligence, and What We Should Build Instead, Anthony Aguirre, Keep The Future Human, 2025-03-05. `sources/keep-the-future-human-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** Two paths in the essay are the unchecked AGI race and coordinated limits paired with controllable Tool AI. The dividing line is the combination of high autonomy, broad generality and superhuman capability (pp. 3–8, 14–17, 47–48).
- **Geography:** Global coordination, with US national policy and public research examples (pp. 37–46, 52–53).
- **Authors’ framing:** A prescription for developing beneficial AI while retaining human oversight (pp. 6–8, 37–56).
- **Authors’ view of likelihood:** The author calls governance feasible if political and social will can be mobilised. Tool AI is presented as probably manageable with coordination and safeguards; success is conditional (pp. 7–8, 49–52).

### Timeline

Near term — introduce reporting, oversight and liability. Then add caps, enforceable tiered rules and international agreements while redirecting investment. Reconsider more sovereign systems only if sufficient scientific understanding and governance capacity develop (pp. 6–8, 37–56).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** On. The intervention depends on public rules that make technical limits credible and on policies to spread the economic gains (pp. 37–56).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Track and certify training and operational compute, impose hard limits, apply strict liability to the highest-risk systems and safe harbours where justified, and require safety cases with oversight proportional to risk. Coordinate internationally and use the concentrated specialised hardware supply chain to help implement the limits (pp. 37–46).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Invest in trustworthy tools through public, nonprofit and private development. Develop controllability guarantees, support basic income or assets and compensation for data contributors, and preserve democratic authority over future expansion (pp. 47–56).

## Key dynamics explored

- Limits keep AI development away from systems that are simultaneously highly autonomous, superhuman and general, while allowing other combinations with appropriate oversight (pp. 37–49).
- Modular tool systems can provide substantial capability while experts make key choices and verify how components behave (pp. 47–49).
- Caps redirect money from simply scaling models to improving, specialising and diversifying smaller models. Hardware demand can remain substantial because those tools still need compute (p. 49).
- Task automation can make output cheaper and more plentiful while depressing earnings for workers whose tasks become less valuable. Redistribution and stronger demand for human-centred work can moderate the loss (pp. 50–51).

## State of technology

Tools can be narrowly superhuman, broadly capable with little autonomy, or limited in the scope of what they can do independently. Human decision-makers remain involved and more autonomous components require credible, preferably quantified guarantees. The essay discusses modular architectures and formal verification (pp. 47–49).

## State of adoption

Developers and startups continue to make smaller or specialised systems. Possible users include researchers, clinicians, educators, public agencies and people seeking control over personal assistants. Governments can supply infrastructure and standards (pp. 47–56).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

The author expects a large AI hardware and software industry and continuing gains from better tools for production and science. He gives no GDP path and recognises that some scientific gains might arrive more slowly than under a hypothetical safely controlled AGI (pp. 49–50).

### Labour market dynamics

Restricting AGI reduces the number of jobs entirely replaced by agents, but substantial task displacement remains probable over years. Some complementary workers may earn more, while displaced workers face lower market pay. The author proposes supporting and valuing human-centred work (pp. 50–51).

### Winners and losers

Smaller and specialised developers can have more room than in a winner-take-all AGI race. Where automation still reduces earnings, the effects depend on access to tools, redistribution and owners’ incentives (pp. 48–55).

### Household incomes and living standards

Aguirre proposes universal basic assets or income and royalties for data contributors to prevent widespread hardship, with benefits from scientific tools and cheaper production distributed more widely (pp. 49–51, 53–55).

### Government finances

The author proposes publicly backed compute oversight, infrastructure, research and investment and broad-based economic benefit mechanisms. The essay gives no cost or funding model (pp. 52–55).

### International economic patterns

International agreements and verifiable commitments reduce pressures to race. Countries can invest in secure, controllable infrastructure and public scientific collaboration rather than assuming strategic advantage requires the most autonomous systems (pp. 37–46, 52–53).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Automation can reduce the cost of some goods and increase their abundance, while other services can rise in relative cost through cost disease. The balance for affected households depends on their income as well as prices (pp. 50–51).

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

The author favours valuing and rewarding work that depends on people and suggests compensating the people whose data creates value for AI (pp. 50–51).

### Affordable essentials

Lower prices for automatable goods may coexist with higher relative costs elsewhere. The source treats household welfare as contingent on both effects and on income support (pp. 50–51).

### Reliable safety net

Universal basic assets or income could protect people from economic suffering during task displacement (pp. 50–51).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Human supervision, accountable institutions, improved public deliberation and participation in deciding whether to build more autonomous systems are central to the proposal (pp. 53–56).

### A fair share of progress

Public and nonprofit development and broad distribution mechanisms can keep tools and economic benefits from being captured by a few companies or states (pp. 53–55).

## Other dynamics

Tool AI brings continuing risks of impersonation, manipulation, loss of privacy and unsuitable automation in sensitive decisions. Aguirre proposes provenance, privacy and data rights, liability and accountable oversight. He also points to benefits for medicine and science (pp. 49–53).
