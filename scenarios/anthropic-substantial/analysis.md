# Substantial — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Policy off.** The model omits policy responses. The authors list options for larger shocks, from "worker retraining to new forms of insurance and income support", but do not model them.

## Big assumptions
1. **Capability outruns adoption.** AI could do half of knowledge work, but only about a fifth of cognitive tasks are affected by 2030.
2. **Mostly automation.** Three quarters of affected work is automated, with one new task for every four automated.
3. **Displaced workers move into non-cognitive work** and immediately earn its wage.
4. **No demand feedback.** The model cannot produce the spiral in which displacement cuts spending and causes more displacement.

## What must be true for this to occur
- Agentic AI becomes reliable enough to take over a fifth of cognitive work within four years.
- Firms automate rather than augment most of the work AI touches.
- Non-cognitive occupations (trades, care) absorb displaced workers fast enough to keep unemployment near 5%.

## Tensions and weak points
- Instant re-employment at the going wage is generous to displaced workers. Evidence on job loss shows lasting pay cuts, so real outcomes for the worst-off are probably worse than modelled.
- The scenario is close to what the public expects, which makes it a useful anchor but not evidence of likelihood.
- Flat cognitive wages alongside rising trade wages is a reversal of the last 40 years of skill premia, with political effects the model does not cover.

## Scoring notes
Draft scores are 3 on every promise. Work could arguably be 2 for knowledge workers forced into new occupations, but unemployment stays historically normal and other workers gain, so 3 fits "manageable disruption".
