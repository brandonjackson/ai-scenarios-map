# Substantial — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Off.** The model isolates technology and labour-market adjustment and leaves policy responses out of the simulated paths. The authors' suggestions (retraining, income support, universal basic capital, universal basic income) and the linked policy framework's phased responses are recommendations, not part of the scenario.

## Big assumptions
1. **Capability: reach roughly doubles.** From 14% to 30% of task mass by 2030, with productivity on affected tasks up about 57%. Effects are confined to cognitive tasks.
2. **Adoption: use quadruples.** From 10% to 40% of eligible instances, so 12% of task instances are affected.
3. **Markets: mostly automation, with capital responding.** 75% of affected work is automated, one new task appears per four automated, and the capital stock grows 13.8% above the no-AI path. Displaced cognitive workers face search frictions but find work in growing non-cognitive occupations.
4. **Politics: not modelled.**

## What must be true for this to occur
- Agentic AI becomes reliable enough to take on a large slice of cognitive work by 2030, and firms adopt it four times as widely as in mid-2026.
- Firms mostly automate rather than augment the work AI touches.
- Trades, care and other non-cognitive occupations absorb displaced workers quickly enough to keep total unemployment near 4.6%.

## Tensions and weak points
- The catalogue says AI "could do half of all knowledge work by 2030 but is used for only about a fifth of it". The extract gives reach as 30% of all task mass and use as 40% of eligible instances; it does not state a share of knowledge work, so the description should be checked.
- Results are sensitive to assumptions. With less elastic capital the average wage change could be −1.6%; with more rigid wages, cognitive unemployment rises to 5.4%.
- The scenario is close to what the public expects, which makes it a useful anchor but not evidence of likelihood.
- Capital takes about nine tenths of the added income (capital income up 18.9% on a 40% share, labour up 1.4% on 60%), so the map now puts it further towards concentrated gains (x −0.3, from −0.2).
- The model leaves out demand feedback, financial disruption and robotics; the authors also say worker-level displacement costs are coarse, so outcomes for the worst-off are probably worse than modelled.
- Government finances are not addressed; the fiscal y position (0.2) is inferred: GDP grows fast, labour income rises 1.4% and capital income 18.9%, while total unemployment rises less than a point, so the tax base widens faster than benefit demand.
- Close neighbours: `gradual-disemp`, `turing-trap`, `tcb-uneven` and `wef-supercharged`.

## Scoring notes
Draft scores are 3/–/3*/–/–, so only two promises are scored, below the three a headline rating needs. Work could be 2 for knowledge workers pushed into new occupations, but cognitive unemployment of 4.5% is historically normal and other workers' wages rise, so it is manageable disruption. The safety net is 3*, inferred from the authors' view that reallocation at this scale is costly for those affected but within what the US labour market has absorbed. Essentials, government and time are not addressed: the model has no prices for essentials, no political channel and nothing on time; the earlier 3 for government leaned on the shift to capital, which says nothing about whether votes matter.
