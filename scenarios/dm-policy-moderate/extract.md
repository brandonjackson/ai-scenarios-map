# Scenario 2: Moderate displacement and wage compression — extract

## Summary
AGI capabilities advance and cause “moderate-to-broad displacement”: “more substantial labor market churn, prolonged unemployment spells, and potentially permanent under/unemployment” (“Scenario 2”). New demand emerges in sectors where human involvement is part of the value, but “labor reinstatement effects may take longer to unfold relative to the pace of automation” (“Scenario 2”). If data show this emerging, safety nets should shift “from temporary transition support to durable income support” (“Scenario 2”). The matched response is transforming an expanded EITC into a Negative Income Tax, triggered when data reveal “prolonged unemployment spells and falling median wages that outpace job reinstatement”; the authors prefer it to UBI and wage insurance (Introduction; “Scenario 2”).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** On. The scenario is defined by its matched, trigger-activated policy: conversion of an expanded EITC into an NIT (Introduction; “Scenario 2”).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Expanded EITC and UI already in place from Scenario 1; the EITC “transitions to a negative income tax (NIT) if Scenario 2 thresholds are triggered” (“Scenario 2”). Trigger: “prolonged unemployment spells and falling median wages that outpace job reinstatement” (Introduction). Escalation trigger to Scenario 3: “a sustained decline in labor’s share of GDP alongside increasing capital returns” (Introduction).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *This scenario.*
  - **NIT via EITC.** “If policymakers establish a tax code where individuals below a particular income threshold automatically receive cash transfers, which phase out as their earned wages rise, the state could guarantee a basic standard of living while preserving incentives for work”; “a more economically efficient mechanism for redistributing technological gains” (“Scenario 2”).
  - **NIT over wage insurance.** An NIT supports people “based strictly on income level, rather than the cause of income loss”, avoiding the task of “untangling whether a worker was displaced specifically by AGI, or by standard market churn” (“Scenario 2”).
  - **NIT over UBI.** NITs take effect only below an income level, making them “much cheaper and more feasible” than UBI (“Scenario 2”). UBI “risks kindling inflationary pressure”, is “an expensive and blunt instrument that may fail to concentrate sufficient relief where it is needed most”, “would not alter the structural forces that shape economic participation”, and “may also leave people with no direct stake in the automated parts of the economy” (“Scenario 2”). UBI programs “dominate public discourse” about AGI economic policy (“Scenario 2”).
  - In the Durability table's Broad Displacement column, UBS (74.0), NIT (69.8), UBC (66.0) and UI (65.9) score highest; EITC falls to 52.3 and ALMPs to 20.6 (Durability table). *[Inference: the Durability table's “Broad Displacement” column corresponds to this scenario; the essay labels the scenarios and the table columns differently and does not state the mapping explicitly.]*

  *Applies across the set.* Three sequenced “least-regret” interventions: Scenario 1, expanded UI, EITC and employer-led retraining; Scenario 2, transform the EITC into an NIT; Scenario 3, a UBC backstop designed now and deployed on trigger (Introduction). Implement automatically adjusting policies (expanded UI, NIT) sooner and basic capital later (Closing). Invest in data collection and monitoring against predetermined thresholds, so already-designed policies can be introduced at the right time (Introduction; Closing). More experiments, empirical evaluations and research on AGI's global developmental impacts are needed to validate options (Closing).

  *Applies across the set — method and rubric.* Policies are scored on four dimensions (Introduction):
  - **Welfare and Resilience:** material living standards, meaning and purpose, economic stability (sub-criteria: Standards of Living; Meaning & Human Value; Macro Stabilisation).
  - **Agency and Voice:** individual economic choice, direct ownership of AI-driven gains, democratic participation (sub-criteria: Economic Participation; Ownership of Gains; Democratic Voice).
  - **Feasibility and Efficiency:** political support, popular approval, economic cost, administrative simplicity, speed of rollout, institutional readiness, and whether a policy creates conditions for AGI-enabled growth (sub-criteria: Political Support; Economic Feasibility; Popular Support; Admin. Capacity; Speed; Ready†, the last “author-coded policy operational maturity”).
  - **Durability across potential AGI economic futures:** suitability across mild disruption, broad displacement and complete economic transformation (columns: Mild Disruption; Broad Displacement; AGI Transformation).

  Ratings combine manual literature reviews, surveys and “51 AI agent raters trained on real economists”: each agent's personality was built from survey data on 51 real economists with diverse economic and political attitudes, a method “pioneered by John Horton” that “removes some degrees of researcher freedom” (Introduction). Tables cite the “Authors' Multi-Agent Expert Economist Panel Benchmark (N = 51 EDSL personas)”, scores 0–100 (rating tables). Headline finding: “no single initiative is the answer to everything”; some policies are popular but infeasible, others resilient but could undermine agency, so strategy “should be sequenced around observable thresholds” with investment in data infrastructure (Introduction).

  The 11 policies (“11 policies we evaluated”): Panel A, targeted labour-market and wage interventions: ALMPs & retraining; wage insurance; EITC; Federal Jobs Guarantee; unemployment insurance. Panel B, universal floors, services and structural assets: NIT; UBI; Sovereign AI Dividend; Universal Basic Capital; Universal Basic Services; industrial policy.

## Key dynamics explored
*Applies across the set*

- Past technological transformations raised long-run living standards but in the short term often “produced lower wages for many, hollowed out regional economies, and deepened inequality”, so many who lived through them “were hurt” (Introduction).
- Past policy often came after the fact because of poor institutions and data, or fear of acting too early; today stronger institutions, better social science on technology shocks and better knowledge of how to scale policies give “reasons for hope” (Introduction).
- Because the scenario and timescale cannot be known in advance, policy should be flexible and “tied to clear empirical triggers”, avoiding acting too late or “overreacting prematurely with interventions that produce a cure worse than the disease” (Introduction; Closing).
- Three challenges: lack of granular, timely labour-market data (platform metrics cannot show net employment effects, field experiments rarely generalise, administrative data lag); deciding which policies distribute AGI benefits widely while preserving agency; and no common rubric for comparing interventions, including data-based deployment triggers (Introduction).
- Work carries non-monetary psychological and social value across the income distribution and supports social cohesion, so preserving people's ability to contribute to the economy should be a policy criterion (“Welfare and agency”).
- Policies that automatically adjust to labour-market conditions (expanded UI, NIT) may be worth implementing sooner; basic-capital programmes later as evidence clarifies. Deploying too early “could impose unnecessary costs and market distortions”; too late could leave society unable to address initial shocks (Closing).
- Policies are designed to escalate: expanded EITC and UI (Scenario 1) “could—both politically and economically— pave the way for a Negative Income Tax if displacement deepens (Scenario 2)”; in Scenario 3 the NIT “could soon after function as a universal income floor as earned wages declined”, paired with a pre-designed UBC (“Building positive AGI economic futures”).
- Growth “that concentrates among a small segment of society is socially and politically fraught” (Closing).

*This scenario*

- Automation outpaces labour reinstatement, producing churn, long unemployment spells and possibly permanent under/unemployment (“Scenario 2”).
- Efficiency gains still drive new demand where human involvement is part of the value, or where human expertise or oversight is demanded (“Scenario 2”).
- Because displacement becomes durable, temporary transition support no longer suffices and income support must become durable (“Scenario 2”).
- An income-tested floor phasing out with earnings guarantees a basic standard while preserving work incentives, and is cheaper than universal transfers because it pays only those below the threshold (“Scenario 2”).
- Cause-based targeting (wage insurance) breaks down because AI-caused displacement cannot be distinguished from ordinary churn (“Scenario 2”).
- Universal cash risks inflation and leaves structural drivers of participation unchanged (“Scenario 2”).

## State of technology
*This scenario.* “AGI capabilities advance” enough to cause moderate-to-broad displacement (“Scenario 2”). The durability rubric describes broad displacement as “widespread displacement outrunning new job creation” (Durability table).

*Applies across the set.* AGI is defined as “advanced AI systems capable of performing cognitive tasks at least at the level of the average person” (Introduction). AI capabilities “have increased tremendously over the last few years, and they show no signs of slowing down” (Introduction). By “automating intelligence itself, AGI could allow for far more comprehensive automation of cognitive work” than prior technologies, risking “outpacing society’s ability to adapt” (Introduction). Because AGI automates intelligence, its economic impacts are “very uncertain”; at one extreme gains and disruption “could fail to materialize as expected” (Introduction). An AGI transition could reduce “the costs of goods and services throughout the economy” (Closing). Market structure, providers, open vs closed models, compute, energy and pricing: Not addressed. Physical/robotics capability: Not addressed, beyond the reference to “the broad and dexterous nature of AGI capabilities” (Introduction).

## State of adoption
*This scenario.* Not addressed beyond automation proceeding faster than reinstatement (“Scenario 2”).

*Applies across the set.* Anxiety about AI and jobs is already “a top concern for political candidates and for younger generations – all before the effect of AI shows up in most economic data” (Introduction). Economists “are currently not seeing definitive evidence of systemic employment or wage impacts” (Introduction). Better data are needed, including “new measures—such as on consumer demand for AI and bottlenecks to adoption” (Introduction). Adoption speed, sectors, firm sizes and countries: Not addressed.

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
*This scenario.* “Efficiency gains drive new demand” (“Scenario 2”). No figures.

*Applies across the set.* AGI could bring “huge benefits” including better health, leisure and higher living standards (Introduction). “If AGI emerges and does indeed increase economic growth, as we expect”, the central question is wide access to its benefits (Closing). If AGI boosts productivity, gains “should appear throughout the economy, not just in the AI sector” (“Scenario 3”). No growth figures given.

### Labour market dynamics
*This scenario.* “More substantial labor market churn, prolonged unemployment spells, and potentially permanent under/unemployment”; jobs change as new demand appears “particularly in sectors where human involvement is part of the value, and areas demanding human expertise or oversight”; “labor reinstatement effects may take longer to unfold relative to the pace of automation” (“Scenario 2”). Trigger indicators: prolonged unemployment spells and falling median wages (Introduction). Wage compression is in the scenario title (Introduction).

*Applies across the set.* Since the Industrial Revolution labour “has remained a relatively constant force in the economy–contrary to consistent predictions of mass labor displacement” (Introduction). At the other extreme AGI could reduce “demand for human labor in most cognitive occupations” (Introduction).

### Winners and losers
*This scenario.* Workers in roles where human involvement is valued, or requiring expertise or oversight, gain from new demand; the long-term un- and under-employed and those with falling wages lose (“Scenario 2”; Introduction). *Applies across the set:* past transitions “deepened inequality” (Introduction).

### Household incomes and living standards
*This scenario.* Falling median wages (Introduction). The NIT guarantees “a basic standard of living” to those below an income threshold (“Scenario 2”). NIT scores 74.3 on Standards of Living, the highest of the 11 (Welfare table).

### Government finances
*This scenario.* NIT is “much cheaper and more feasible” than UBI because it pays only below a threshold; UBI is “an expensive and blunt instrument” (“Scenario 2”). Economic Feasibility scores: NIT 78.9, UBI 40.2 (Feasibility table).

*Applies across the set.* Feasibility scores include an “Economic Feasibility” sub-criterion (“fiscal/macro cost viability”) (Feasibility table). Funding mechanisms (14) are evaluated only in the unread SSRN paper (Introduction).

### International economic patterns

### Market pricing and scarcity

*Applies across the set.* An AGI transition could reduce “the costs of goods and services throughout the economy” (Closing).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
*This scenario.* NIT transfers phase out “as their earned wages rise”, “preserving incentives for work” (“Scenario 2”). UBI “may fail to support economic agency” and offers “no direct mechanism for economic participation” (“Scenario 2”).

*Applies across the set.* Work has non-monetary value “across the income distribution” and serves social cohesion, “establishing relationships and deterring antisocial behavior”; maintaining people's ability to contribute “should be a salient dimension” of AGI policy (“Welfare and agency”). The rubric scores “Meaning & Human Value” and “Economic Participation” (rating tables).

### Affordable essentials
Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net
*This scenario.* Safety nets “should shift from temporary transition support to durable income support”; the NIT is a guaranteed income floor paid automatically below a threshold, regardless of the cause of income loss (“Scenario 2”; “11 policies we evaluated”).

### Time for what matters
Not addressed.

*Applies across the set.* AGI could bring benefits including “improvements in health, leisure time, and higher standards of living” (Introduction).

### A say in your future
*This scenario.* Not addressed beyond panel scores: Democratic Voice NIT 57.0, UBI 46.2 (Agency table). NIT Political Support is 22.9 (Feasibility table).

*Applies across the set.* Agency and Voice is a rubric dimension; “Democratic Voice” means “individual influence over economic governance and workplace decisions” (Agency table). Policy should “maintain individual agency and the balance of power that is needed for economies and democracy to thrive” (“Welfare and agency”). “Society has the capacity and tools to shape our economic trajectory in the AGI era” (Closing).

### A fair share of progress
*This scenario.* NIT is described as “a more economically efficient mechanism for redistributing technological gains”; UBI “may also leave people with no direct stake in the automated parts of the economy” (“Scenario 2”). Ownership of Gains: NIT 39.8, UBI 51.3 (Agency table).

*Applies across the set.* “A central question of our time may be one about ensuring wide access to the benefits of that growth”, using AGI's gains for “improved standards of living and opportunity for all” (Closing). The rubric's “Ownership of Gains” sub-criterion scores “broad-based societal distribution of AI-generated capital and productivity gains” (Agency table).

## Other dynamics
*Applies across the set.* Political salience: AI and jobs is “already a top concern for political candidates and for younger generations” (Introduction). Safety, geopolitics, environment and information ecosystem: Not addressed.
