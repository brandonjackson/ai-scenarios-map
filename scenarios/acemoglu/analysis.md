# The Simple Macroeconomics of AI — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Policy off for the estimate, with a pro-worker steer implied.** The central numbers assume AI is used as it is now: task-level cost savings at today's exposure. The upside case (AI creating new tasks and expertise for non-college workers) needs deliberate direction. Acemoglu points to his work with Autor and Johnson for the policy agenda.

## Big assumptions
1. **AI's effects come from task-level cost savings.** Hulten's theorem applies, so macro gains ≈ exposed share of GDP × average cost saving. This rules out effects outside that structure: a "singularity" (explicitly out of scope), much faster R&D, or wholesale reorganisation of industries.
2. **Exposure and savings estimates hold.** About 20% of US tasks are exposed (Eloundou et al.), about 23% of those are profitably automatable within ten years (Svanberg et al.), and cost savings average about 27%, from early experiments.
3. **Hard tasks are harder.** Tasks without objective success measures will show smaller gains than early lab studies suggest.
4. **Capability is roughly static over the decade.** The estimate uses 2023–24 evidence and doesn't project capability improvements.
5. **Elasticity of substitution between tasks is below 1,** so productivity gains in labour tasks lower labour's share.

## What must be true for this to occur
- Generative AI keeps improving only incrementally, so exposure doesn't widen much beyond today's estimates.
- Adoption stays limited by integration costs and the difficulty of context-heavy tasks.
- No large wave of new labour-using tasks is created (otherwise wages and productivity do better).

## Tensions and weak points
- **Dated by capability gains:** written in early 2024, before reasoning models and capable agents. If exposure and cost savings have since grown a lot (as Shumer and Citrini claim), the estimate understates the effect. It is most useful as a *lower bound*.
- The framework can't easily show sudden, cross-sector changes, such as the end of intermediation or SaaS that Citrini describes.
- His theoretical result (low-skill productivity gains can increase inequality) is a direct challenge to Agrawal et al. and Autor.
- **Map placement:** "Limited impact, CONC (warns)" is accurate. The concentration is between capital and labour, not among firms; the paper doesn't analyse market concentration.
