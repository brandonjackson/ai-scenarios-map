# The Simple Macroeconomics of AI — extract

## Summary

Generative AI and computer vision automate some tasks and make people more productive in others. The share of tasks that can be economically affected within ten years is small, and context-dependent tasks with poorly observed outcomes are harder to learn, so economy-wide gains are modest (§§2.5, 3.1–3.4). Even when AI complements workers, the gains need not reduce wage inequality, and capital income is likely to pull further ahead of labour income (§§2.9, 3.6, 4).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. The model evaluates automation, complementarity and investment without a new government policy scenario (§§2–3).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Not addressed.
- **Policies the source recommends:** Prioritise applications that generate useful new tasks and raise workers’ capabilities, and assess welfare effects separately from measured production (§4).

## Key dynamics explored

- Automation reallocates tasks from workers to capital. Complementarity raises productivity in tasks workers retain. Deepening improves already automated tasks, while AI-enabled new tasks or organisational changes can reinstate labour (§§1, 2.2, 2.7).
- First-order aggregate productivity gains are the cost reduction per affected task times the economic weight of the affected tasks. Capital accumulation amplifies the GDP response (§§2.4, 2.6, 3.4).
- Benchmarks and experiments tend to feature learnable tasks with clear success criteria. Tasks such as evaluating scholarship or providing care require context and hard-to-observe outcomes, which reduce feasible cost savings (§§2.5, 3.3).
- Economy-wide gains can count activities with negative social value in GDP, widening the gap between measured output and welfare (§§2.8, 3.5).

## State of technology

The analysis includes generative AI and computer vision, including text drafting and summarisation, classification and pattern recognition. Related systems can offer suggestions rather than complete entire tasks. Cost and reliability depend on available data, clarity of desired outcomes, context, programming requirements and complementary inputs (§§1, 2.2, 2.5, 3.1–3.3).

## State of adoption

The benchmark combines task exposure with an estimate of what fraction becomes economical within ten years. Adoption can be slower among small and medium-sized firms, particularly if tools are concentrated in few suppliers. Organisational change costs can delay realised gains, and falling chip costs do not reduce total implementation costs one-for-one (§§3.1–3.3).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

The benchmark estimates 0.66% higher TFP after ten years, or 0.53% after accounting for hard-to-learn tasks. Adding a higher estimate from a coding experiment raises the first-pass result to 0.89%. Under modest capital accumulation, GDP gains are 0.93–1.16% over ten years. A larger investment response can increase them to 1.4–1.56% (§§3.2–3.4, 4).

### Labour market dynamics

Automation reduces labour demand directly for the tasks transferred to capital. Cost savings can still support labour demand elsewhere. Complementarity can raise productivity or wages in a group but, through task reallocation and factor-price effects, can increase wage differences. The exercise estimates real wage declines for some low-education women and a rising capital share, with less demographic concentration than earlier automation (§§2.3, 2.9, 3.6, 4).

### Winners and losers

Owners of capital stand to gain relative to labour. Less experienced workers improved most in some experiments, but task-level evidence does not establish a narrowing wage distribution. Effects differ by exposure and skill composition (§§3.1, 3.6, 4).

### Household incomes and living standards

The estimated income distribution shifts towards capital, and welfare may rise less than GDP if new activities include costly manipulation or misinformation (§§3.5–3.6, 4).

### A fair share of progress

The wage exercise offers no evidence that new AI by itself will narrow labour income inequality, while the capital–labour income gap widens (§§3.6, 4).

## Other dynamics

The paper flags manipulative content and defensive expenditures as activities whose measured contribution can be positive even with a negative welfare effect (§§2.8, 3.5).
