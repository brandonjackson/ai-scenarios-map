# Big AI / Arms Race — extract

## Summary

In Big AI, increasingly useful agents spread across work and daily life, while compute, distribution and data advantages consolidate control among a few US providers (Big AI, main path). In its Agent Economy ending, a European open-weight alternative and adaptable users retain room to compete. In Silicon Blackmail, tightly integrated platforms gain leverage over governments and the US sees faster growth alongside displacement (Big AI, both endings). Arms Race is a separate trajectory in which U.S.–China competition concentrates frontier development in state-backed programmes. Chip dependencies and weak verification sharpen tensions, with endings of open conflict or a fragile multipolar truce that limits commercial use (Arms Race, main path and both endings).

## Key details

- **Source:** Advanced AI: Possible futures, Bengüsu Özcan, Daan Juijn, Jakob Graabak, Sam Bogerd, Centre for Future Generations, 2025-07-10. `sources/cfg-advanced-ai-possible-futures-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** The publisher’s scenario PDF and companion methodology, technical context and governance analysis are listed in the source manifest.
- **Reading notes:** Full publisher scenario text and companion material. This extract uses the catalogue-linked website for story chronology and market structure. Some Big AI details differ in the publisher’s scenario PDF.
- **Scenario set:** The study crosses speed of capability progress with concentration of frontier development. The five core narratives are Plateau, Big AI, Diplomacy, Arms Race, and Take-off, each with two alternative endings (“How to navigate the scenarios?”; narrative PDF §1). The catalogue combines two distinct core scenarios in this row. Big AI branches into The Agent Economy and Silicon Blackmail. Arms Race branches into Hot War and Multipolar World.
- **Geography:** Global, with particular attention to the US, China and Europe (scenario narratives).
- **Authors' framing:** Plausible foresight scenarios for stress-testing policy under uncertainty, constructed from expert consultation and structured identification of key parameters. The numbers for training compute and R&D automation are directional (“Key Takeaways”; “AI futures in the numbers”; Methodology).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned. The authors selected plausible, deliberately diverse cases for decision-making (“Key Takeaways”; “How to navigate the scenarios?”).

### Timeline

- Big AI, mid-2025 to early 2027 — U.S. agents become reliable enough for real workflows, including health and finance. China lags because of chip restrictions and European projects face political and permitting delays. OmniAI shifts frontier development from open to closed. The U.S. considers release restrictions, and a vertically integrated group of firms entrenches itself (Big AI, main path).
- Agent Economy, 2027–28 — the U.S. restricts open release at frontier compute scales. Europe mandates openness for publicly funded models, and a domestic provider gains government, privacy-sensitive and regulated-industry customers. Agents displace apps and become common inputs to work. Late-2028 GDP growth is modestly higher in several developed economies (Big AI, “The agent economy”).
- Silicon Blackmail, 2027–28 — US controls constrain the hosting of large open-weight runs globally. Europe increases enforcement of digital laws, several US providers depart, and the bloc struggles to sustain essential services. Some central banks raise rates amid strong markets and faster US growth, while public protests focus on displacement and corporate power (Big AI, “Silicon blackmail”).
- Arms Race, 2025 to mid-2028 — the US and China move frontrunners into secret state-backed programmes. Export controls tighten, access to advanced chips becomes a strategic concern, and commercial releases slow. Proposed limits break down over verification and competition around Taiwan (Arms Race, main path).
- Hot War, mid-2028 — conflict breaks out over Taiwan. Automated systems speed up military decision-making and the danger of escalation grows (Arms Race, “Hot war”).
- Multipolar World, 2028–29 — the crisis leads to a partial truce with trade concessions. International monitoring expands, new restrictions depress commercial AI and markets, and the EU gains a place as a third AI bloc (Arms Race, “Multipolar world”).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Mixed. Governments constrain AI deployments, promote national providers or conduct strategic development. Their policies differ by trajectory and ending (Big AI and Arms Race).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Big AI includes US semiconductor export controls and frontier open-weight restrictions. In Agent Economy, Europe requires openness for publicly funded models and pilots use agents in public services, while some governments test job guarantees and levies to fund retraining. In Silicon Blackmail the EU intensifies AI Act, Digital Services Act and Digital Markets Act enforcement and plans domestic investment. Arms Race includes state-led compute and industrial mobilization, tightened semiconductor restrictions, and, in Multipolar World, conditional pauses, trade concessions, independent monitoring and commercial restrictions (Big AI and Arms Race, main paths and endings).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* Stress-test policy across different trajectories. Invest in adaptive labour-market and education institutions, information resilience and cyber defence. Coordinate among institutions and countries and develop credible verification for international agreements. Build scalable safeguards alongside innovation rather than relying solely on access restrictions (“Key Takeaways”; governance analysis §§2–5 and conclusion).

## Key dynamics explored

- Big AI: advanced chips, cloud resources, talent, distribution, data and platform feedback give US incumbents compounding advantages. High switching costs, investor pressure, safety incidents and regulatory restrictions move the frontier toward closed oligopoly (Big AI, main path).
- Agent Economy: reliable agents absorb work previously performed in apps. The ability to manage agents becomes a dividing line across firms and workers. Competition remains meaningful where European and community open alternatives offer adequate capabilities, localization or transparency (Big AI, “The agent economy”).
- Silicon Blackmail: US firms move into high-value work enabled by their own agents. Foreign customers come to depend on the integrated ecosystems, giving suppliers the power to threaten withdrawal and pressure regulators (Big AI, “Silicon blackmail”).
- Arms Race: governments treat scarce frontier chips and compute as strategic assets, consolidating development under public-private schemes. Weak verification and competitive pressure favour secrecy over coordination. Further restrictions in the multipolar ending reduce economic deployment despite latent capability (Arms Race, main path and “Multipolar world”).

## State of technology

Big AI: advanced reasoning produces valuable specialized and workflow agents, including systems that can handle multi-hour tasks with guidance in Agent Economy. In the website’s Silicon Blackmail branch, agents speed engineering and research logistics but lack autonomous scientific hypothesis formation. Compute scarcity and high capital needs advantage US incumbent labs and encourage proprietary chips. Arms Race: leading systems increasingly flow to state-backed projects, with rapid software advances and less reaching civilian users. Taiwan fabrication, Dutch lithography and US chip designs are the key supply-chain dependencies (Big AI and Arms Race, main paths and endings).

## State of adoption

Big AI: early real estate, finance and some healthcare use is followed by everyday consumer assistants, subscription services and enterprise workflow redesign. Adoption varies with regulation, established infrastructure, institutional incentives, and skills. In Agent Economy some multinationals mandate widespread use, and governments experiment more slowly in healthcare, education and legal aid. In Silicon Blackmail the Big Five’s distribution advantage and service withdrawals constrain choices. Arms Race: desk-work adoption grows initially while national-security projects get privileged access. In Multipolar World restrictions freeze much commercial potential (Big AI and Arms Race, main paths and endings).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Big AI: the story describes firm-level improvements before the national data shift. Agent Economy ends with modest, measurable gains in GDP growth across many developed economies in late 2028. Silicon Blackmail adds one percentage point to U.S. GDP growth in 2028, with rising unemployment. The scenario PDF separately gives 40–70% corporate-reported productivity gains in knowledge-intensive roles for Big AI’s main path, a figure absent from the website. Arms Race emphasizes the cost of chip disruptions and later commercial restraints in Multipolar World, without a GDP estimate (Big AI, main path and endings, narrative PDF §1.2.2; Arms Race, 2027 and “Multipolar world”).

### Labour market dynamics

Big AI: people skilled in AI pull away from less adept colleagues. Orchestrating agents becomes a valued skill, generating opportunities in new firms and careers, while routine white-collar work faces substitution. Silicon Blackmail identifies software engineers, paralegals and digital-media workers, alongside falling computer-science enrolment and rising unemployment. Arms Race: entry-level software work is hit early, layoffs spread and universities question CS training. Some technology workers leave over militarization and some workers in the multipolar ending devote less effort to skills and more time to family (Big AI, main path and endings; Arms Race, main path and “Multipolar world”).

### Winners and losers

Big AI: U.S. platform shareholders, executives and AI-native professionals gain, while displaced communities and less adaptable employers and workers lose. In Agent Economy, adaptable SMEs, freelancers, creatives and developers prosper despite uneven access. In Silicon Blackmail concentration increases the disadvantage of European and other challengers. Arms Race: major-state programmes capture the strongest systems, while dependent countries and civilian sectors face tighter access and trade pressures (Big AI and Arms Race, main paths and endings).

### Household incomes and living standards

Big AI’s Silicon Blackmail describes prosperity accruing primarily to shareholders, executives and AI-native workers while automation-hit communities fall behind. It gives no household income path (Big AI, “Silicon blackmail”).

### Government finances

In Agent Economy, job guarantee and agent-tax pilots emerge, with the levy intended to fund retraining. The Arms Race branches describe public resources redirected to strategic data-centre and semiconductor projects without a fiscal path (Big AI, “The agent economy”; Arms Race, main path).

### International economic patterns

Big AI: US advantages in semiconductor access and distribution leave China and Europe behind. Under Agent Economy, EU open-weight procurement and a viable domestic provider supply alternatives for privacy-conscious consumers and regulated sectors. Under Silicon Blackmail, U.S. controls and provider exit threaten European sovereignty. Arms Race: competing national programmes, U.S. export controls on EU-made equipment sold to China and chip dependence on Taiwan worsen tensions. In Multipolar World agreements cover tariffs, minerals and further export restrictions. External audits and later triangular oversight accompany three emerging blocs (Big AI and Arms Race, main paths and endings).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Big AI: subscription and metered inference become revenue sources, while rising inference demand increases chip prices and prompts proprietary chips. Firms buy access despite lock-in and some offer discounts in exchange for interaction data. Arms Race: hardware restrictions squeeze chip supply (Big AI, main path; Arms Race, “Early 2027 – late 2027”).

### Financial markets and stability

Big AI analysts foresee possible revenue doubling for the Big Five within a few years. Strong markets outside Europe and higher rates in some countries follow the Silicon Blackmail branch. Arms Race includes flights into safe-haven assets during the chip squeeze and further market falls after widespread commercial restrictions in Multipolar World (Big AI, main path and “Silicon blackmail”; Arms Race, “Early 2027 – late 2027” and “Multipolar world”).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Not addressed.

### Affordable essentials

Uses include clinical diagnostic support where regulation permits, followed by public-service pilots. Silicon Blackmail leaves Europe struggling to maintain digital infrastructure and services when major firms withdraw (Big AI, main path and endings).

### Reliable safety net

Some governments test job guarantee programmes and retraining levies in Agent Economy (Big AI, “The agent economy”).

### Time for what matters

Many workers in Multipolar World deprioritize career development and choose to spend more time with family under continuing uncertainty (Arms Race, “Multipolar world”).

### A say in your future

Big AI platforms lobby governments, lock in users and can withdraw essential services to press demands. Displaced communities protest. Arms Race prioritizes secrecy, with protesters seeing little benefit from demands for transparency (Big AI, main path and “Silicon blackmail”; Arms Race, “Early 2027 – late 2027”).

### A fair share of progress

Big AI’s Agent Economy leaves alternative routes through European and community models with privacy, customization and transparency. Silicon Blackmail benefits a narrower group. Arms Race restricts the flow of frontier capabilities to the public when military secrecy or a fragile truce takes precedence (Big AI and Arms Race, endings).

## Other dynamics

*Applies across the set.* The authors argue that even existing AI capabilities can disrupt labour, education, media and political institutions if widely deployed. Resistance can impede beneficial uses. The pace of R&D automation, control of supply chains and the ability to verify safety commitments are critical uncertainties (“Key Takeaways”; governance analysis §§1–5). Big AI raises privacy and emotional-dependency concerns, including social withdrawal among some young people. Arms Race raises the likelihood of miscalculation, reduces European confidence in U.S. support, and subordinates safety to speed. A truce in Multipolar World requires disputed verification and gives outside monitors growing authority (Big AI and Arms Race, main paths and endings).
