# Mild Disruption — summary

*Scenario 1 of "Economic policy for AGI" by Julian Jacobs and Alex Imas, an essay from the DeepMind Institute (Google DeepMind) published on 16 September 2026. The essay sets out three scenarios by depth of labour-market disruption and matches each with a "least-regret" policy and an empirical trigger. Eleven household-facing policies are rated by a panel of 51 AI agents modelled on real economists. Read from the full essay. The longer SSRN working paper could not be read; see `extract.md`.*

**The scenario.** AGI "boosts productivity and creates familiar automation and augmentation dynamics across labor markets, as we have seen in prior technological waves". Wages polarise and the skills employers want shift, but "without a collapse in aggregate labor demand". Some workers lose their jobs and "cannot find another right away", and some are re-employed at lower wages. The matched response is "preemptive stabilizers", put in place now: unemployment insurance that replaces most of a worker's prior wage for longer and covers gig workers and freelancers; an Earned Income Tax Credit that reaches workers without dependents and is paid regularly; and employer-led retraining. These "automatically adjust in scale if disruption increases" and could later grow into a negative income tax.

## Hard work pays
Stable. Effort still pays because labour demand holds, but wage polarisation means the gains are uneven, and some displaced workers take lower-paid jobs. The expanded EITC is meant to keep work worthwhile: "If AI compresses wages without eliminating jobs, expanding the EITC directly cushions living standards while maintaining incentives to work." Retraining aims at "AI-complementary jobs", though the authors note public retraining "has historically been disappointing", which is why they favour employer-led models.

## Affordable essentials
Not addressed.

## Reliable safety net
Improving. The scenario widens today's US safety net, which the authors say "expires quickly, and typically excludes non-traditional workers". Unemployment insurance would guarantee "a majority of a person's prior wage for a set period". Denmark's system, which pays up to 90% of prior salary for up to two years, is the example. Benefits stay tied to work and scale up automatically if disruption deepens.

## Responsive government
Not addressed. The essay rates each policy's "Democratic Voice", but it says nothing about how politics works in this scenario.

## Time for what matters
Not addressed.

## Bottom line
The closest of the three scenarios to today: a familiar technology shock met with a stronger version of the existing safety net. Gains are uneven but no group is left without support.
