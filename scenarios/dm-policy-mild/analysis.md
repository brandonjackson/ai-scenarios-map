# Mild Disruption — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Mixed: the disruption is a given, and the stabilizers are assumed in place.** The essay's three scenarios are set by how deeply AGI disrupts labour, which happens whatever governments do. But each scenario comes with its matched policy, and the essay describes the policy path as part of the future it lays out. Scenario 1 is named for its "preemptive stabilizers": expanded UI, a broader EITC and employer-led retraining, introduced now and designed to "automatically adjust in scale if disruption increases". The labour-market story does not depend on policy. The household outcomes in the extract do. So the scenario contains both, and the links count the three stabilizers and their automatic design.

## Big assumptions
1. **Capability:** AGI arrives (or something close to it) but behaves like past general-purpose technologies, with "localized task automation with gradual reallocation".
2. **Adoption:** diffusion follows familiar patterns. The extract gives no pace, sectors or countries.
3. **Markets:** aggregate labour demand holds. Wages polarise and skill demand shifts, but no collapse follows.
4. **Politics:** the US expands UI (higher replacement, longer duration, gig workers covered) and the EITC (workers without dependents, regular payments) before disruption is visible. The panel rates the EITC as feasible (79.8) and UI less so (54.3, political support 35.5).

## What must be true for this to occur
- Reinstatement of new tasks keeps pace with automation, as it has since the Industrial Revolution.
- Wage compression, where it happens, leaves jobs in place, so a work-linked credit can cushion it.
- Congress expands UI and the EITC before data show a shock, which goes against the usual pattern of policy arriving "after the fact".
- Employer-led retraining scales, where public retraining has "historically been disappointing".

## Tensions and weak points
- The catalogue entry describes a world. The essay mostly describes a policy menu. Its scenario text is a few sentences, so the map position rests on short statements, not modelled numbers.
- The matched policies have low "Ownership of Gains" scores (UI 19.2, EITC 20.6). The scenario cushions workers but does not share AI's gains with them, and the essay leaves that for later scenarios.
- The essay links its scenarios to its Durability table columns only implicitly (the extract flags this as an inference).
- Close to `imf-baseline`, `anthropic-modest` and `dsit-steady`, which are all policy-off slow-diffusion worlds. This one differs by assuming a stronger safety net. Its counterpart in the other tiered framework is `anthropic-epf-tier1`. That tier leans a little more towards replacement (fast churn, skills compressed), and its matched policies are left as recommendations.

## Scoring notes
Scores are 3/–/4/–/–. Work is 3: labour demand holds, but wage polarisation and re-employment at lower wages mean uneven gains, with the EITC keeping work worthwhile. Safety net is 4 because the scenario widens today's US system: UI replaces "a majority of a person's prior wage" for longer and covers gig workers and freelancers who are now excluded, the EITC reaches workers without dependents, and both scale automatically. That is an improvement on current capacity, not just stability. Essentials, government and time are not addressed. The essay's "Democratic Voice" panel scores rate policies, not the state of politics in the scenario. With only two promises scored, the scenario has no headline rating.
