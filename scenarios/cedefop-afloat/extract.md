# Scenario C: staying afloat – AI opportunities missed — extract

## Summary

It is 2040. AI continues to rise "but at a modest pace", bringing moderate transformations of tasks and jobs rather than extensive disruption. Change is generally manageable for employers, workers and Member States, and incremental adjustments usually suffice (pp. 10, 73). AI's potential to improve economic and social conditions is "not fully tapped" because stakeholder investment and engagement vary across sectors, Member States and population segments. AI expansion is driven by the sectors that gain most financially rather than serving the greater public good (pp. 10, 73–74). There is no extensive replacement of workers. Technical skills stay relevant, jobs based on repetitive tasks persist, and traditional training institutions meet moderate upskilling needs (pp. 74–75). Social protection does not fully keep up with a modest rise in nontraditional work, union participation declines, and private training investment rarely extends beyond firms' own staff. The scenario warns of "stagnation and complacency" and missed opportunities (pp. 11, 51, 56, 75).

## Key details

- **Source:** Preparing for 2040: four AI-powered scenarios for the future of continuing skills development, Cedefop (European Centre for the Development of Vocational Training), Publications Office of the European Union, 2025-11. `sources/cedefop-preparing-2040-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None. The same report restates the scenario in its executive summary (p. 10). Results from the later scenario-exploration and vision-building phases are due to be published in 2026 (pp. 8, 18).
- **Reading notes:** Full text read: executive summary, Chapters 1–2 (method), Chapter 5 (Scenario C assumptions and semi-Delphi results), Chapter 7.3 (consolidated scenario), Chapter 8, Annexes 1, 3 and 4, and the Scenario C section of the Annex 5 questionnaire. Figure 1 (impact–uncertainty matrix) and Figure 2 (respondents) are images that are not in the text extraction. Locators are the report's printed page numbers.
- **Scenario set:** One of four Cedefop scenarios for continuing skills development by 2040. The others are A "a future of opportunities – technology-driven competition for talent", B "left alone to ride the tide – navigating the AI shock waves on jobs" and D "AI unleashed – dominating the world of work and societies" (pp. 9–11). The set is not built on a 2×2 grid. It comes from a five-step process: (1) identifying 21 trends with the STEEPV framework, (2) impact–uncertainty analysis, (3) morphological analysis of possible evolutions for each trend, (4) four scenario outlines built on four "evolution paths", and (5) validation in a semi-Delphi survey with 132 respondents (pp. 8, 19–28). AI is the central trend. In both rounds of trend analysis, experts ranked all AI-related trends as highly impactful and highly uncertain (pp. 8–9). Scenario C is built on evolution path 3: "AI leads to moderate transformations of tasks and jobs rather than extensive disruptions; changes are generally manageable and some parts of the population benefit" (pp. 27, 88). It is the only scenario with "Modest" rather than "Extensive" AI uptake (p. 95). Annex 4 compares the scenarios on AI uptake, benefits from AI, ability to cope with change, employment forms, labour relations, AI impact on jobs, skills most needed, inequality, learning landscape and sustainability (pp. 95–96).
- **Geography:** European Union / Europe, with variation across Member States (pp. 10, 73).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory planning scenario that feeds a stakeholder vision-building process. "These scenarios are not predictive but rather exploratory, aiming to identify areas that require attention, threats that need to be addressed and opportunities that should be leveraged" (p. 9). The scenario is a warning about modest uptake: "a lack of investment in AI and the failure to harness its potential leads to stagnation and complacency, ultimately resulting in a loss of competitiveness and innovation and a missed chance to address pressing societal challenges, such as climate change, social cohesion and education" (p. 11). It also presents a "middle ground" where "AI supports humans in a relatively smooth mode of coexistence" (p. 12).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated for the scenario as a whole. The survey's stated purpose was "not to see which scenario is more plausible" (p. 98). Respondents were split (48% vs 52%) on whether geopolitics and political choices would keep AI uptake modest, so the key assumption is "partly questioned" (pp. 49, 55). Only 9% thought AI adoption would become unstoppable, driven by economics alone (p. 50).

### Timeline

- Horizon: 2040 (p. 10). The survey framed it as "the next 15-20 years" (p. 98).
- No dated events within the scenario narrative.
- Study timeline: trend identification Sept–Oct 2023; trend analysis Oct–Nov 2023; morphological analysis Dec 2023–Mar 2024; scenario outlines Apr 2024; expert workshop May 2024; semi-Delphi survey Oct–Nov 2024; scenario exploration Dec 2024–Jan 2025; vision building Feb–Dec 2025 (p. 18).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Mixed. AI regulation protects citizens and mitigates risks, and the state and unions push for inclusiveness and fund broader adult training. But governments do not strongly promote AI, the state does not step up upskilling, and social dialogue fails to renew social protection (pp. 46, 49, 51, 56, 73–75).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:**
  - "AI-related regulation offers a framework that protects individuals (as citizens or consumers) and addresses legal and ethical considerations. Risks from AI systems are significantly mitigated" (p. 46). Regulations against AI's uncontrolled expansion "are commonly expected", which partly explains slow uptake (p. 73).
  - Geopolitical developments and EU countries' political decisions are one reason for slow uptake. Geopolitical security may still call for continued or intensified AI investment rather than diverting funds massively to defence (pp. 73–74). 50% of respondents expected the EU to regulate AI in more fields for security reasons. 30% expected geopolitics to intensify AI investment (p. 49).
  - EU countries "do not always have a strong motivation to incentivise AI advances or cover the (start-up) costs" for new technologies. "In the absence of a prominent state push across many policy domains", AI expansion is driven by the sectors with the most financial benefit (p. 74). Only one third of respondents expected governments to finance AI start-ups regardless of uptake (p. 50).
  - Social dialogue and policy attention to securing jobs may slow AI investment through regulations protecting employees from displacement (one in four respondents) (p. 50).
  - The labour force has stopped shrinking, "e.g. as a result of activation or migration policies" (p. 47).
  - Inclusiveness is "a lower priority". The state and trade unions are typically the ones pushing for it, to help under-represented and vulnerable groups (p. 49). Per evolution path 3, inclusiveness is "key only for states and trade unions and is not a priority for employers" (p. 90).
  - "The state does not need to step up its involvement in upskilling and reskilling" (p. 49). Yet the state "will bear the larger share of responsibility and costs for broader adult training", especially for less advantaged groups (p. 56).
  - Social dialogue "does not always manage to establish an updated network of social protection" (p. 75). It focuses on better identifying skills needs and developing suitable training offers so that "no learner group is left behind" (p. 14).
  - Sustainability policies influence only a limited number of sectors and policy areas. Green skills are not a priority for state or private training stakeholders (pp. 46, 75).
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - For this scenario: "the expansion of its potential benefits to more audiences will depend on the decision of governments to invest in and promote far-reaching applications for the general population and for smaller companies" (p. 55). The issue for repetitive jobs "is not only about securing jobs based on repetitive tasks, but safeguarding certain levels of quality, reward and social protection in such jobs" (p. 55). Scenario C "warns of missed opportunities for innovation if investment and engagement remain uneven" (p. 79).
  - *Applies across the set:* Stakeholders should co-create a vision that prioritises "lifelong and life-wide learning and continuing skills development, individual empowerment, equitable access to opportunities and robust governance of AI technologies" (p. 79). Integrated learning systems should leverage institutional, self-directed and workplace contexts (p. 79). Social dialogue should be willing to "experiment with new forms of worker representation and social protection", prioritise lifelong learning and make sure all workers have a voice (p. 14). Technological advancement should be balanced with social equity and environmental responsibility (p. 79).

## Key dynamics explored

- Modest AI uptake leads to moderate task and job change, so pressure on firms and workers to adapt is modest and incremental change suffices (pp. 46–47, 73–74).
- Without a strong state push, sectors with clear financial benefits drive AI expansion. Gains accrue to some sectors and workers and do not serve "all sectors or ... the greater public good" (p. 74).
- "In this scenario, AI is not an unstoppable force." The extent of uptake and of its benefits depends on political and geopolitical choices, including whether national stakeholders see AI mainly as an opportunity for security or a threat to it (pp. 55, 73).
- Firms decide whether to replace repetitive jobs by comparing the cost of AI with the cost of human labour, including the availability of cheap labour. Fear of AI can be used to push wages and conditions down, making human work cheaper than AI (pp. 52, 55, 74).
- Workers' skills do not quickly become obsolete, so organisations adapt with current staff. Lay-offs and outplacements are limited, and employers' loyalty to workers "is not further challenged" (pp. 47, 75).
- Hiring is not difficult, so companies rely on state-funded adult training and focus their own training on existing staff. The state bears more of the cost of broader adult training (pp. 53, 56).
- A modest rise in nontraditional work reduces trade union participation and weakens social dialogue's ability to renew social protection. This raises concerns for low-skilled workers, including those in repetitive jobs (pp. 51, 55–56).
- With fewer shocks, there is less urgency for a sustainability paradigm shift, though gradual adaptation may still allow greener practices to be established (pp. 54, 75).
- *Applies across the set:* the "individual responsibility gap" is less pronounced in Scenarios A and C, where individuals are "better equipped to cope with changes in the job market" (pp. 12–13).

## State of technology

- AI keeps advancing "at a modest pace" (p. 73). It supports humans in certain jobs or tasks "in a relatively smooth mode of coexistence", without full integration (pp. 46, 74).
- Higher-level technical skills (programming, data processing, interface design) gain importance for creating AI-supported solutions and making them widely applicable (pp. 50, 74).
- AI-related regulation significantly mitigates risks from AI systems (p. 46).
- In education and training, AI and digital technologies support learning "but with no major impact". Teachers use AI to a limited extent, for example for animation, videos and spoken narrative. In guidance, AI either does not interact directly with beneficiaries or provides basic services under human supervision (pp. 48–49, 74–75).
- 34% of respondents said AI will become increasingly efficient and profitable for repetitive tasks (p. 52). 17% said even modest AI uptake improves production efficiency and supports sustainability (p. 54).
- Market structure, compute, energy and pricing: Not addressed.

## State of adoption

- AI uptake is "Modest" (p. 95). Benefits are "Not widely available; mostly tapped by some sectors/industries" (p. 95).
- "Some sectors and workers manage to grasp the opportunities brought by AI to improve their efficiency, productivity and competitiveness", but AI advances are not spread across all sectors (pp. 46, 74). Investment and engagement vary across sectors, Member States and population segments (p. 73).
- Disruption is prominent only for sectors or regions most exposed to rapid technological change (pp. 47, 74).
- What slows adoption: political and geopolitical decisions; regulation against uncontrolled expansion, including for security and ethical reasons; weak state motivation to fund start-up costs; social dialogue protecting jobs; and cost–benefit calculations, since in industries reliant on repetitive tasks AI "might not be the profitable option" (33% of respondents) (pp. 49–50, 52, 73–74).
- Smaller companies and the general population need government promotion to benefit (p. 55).
- In education and training, AI use "increases slowly across all types of learning environments" or "rapidly, but only within certain types of learning environments" (pp. 92–93).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Some sectors and workers use AI to improve "efficiency, productivity and competitiveness", but AI's potential to improve economic and social conditions "is not fully tapped" (pp. 46, 73–74). The executive summary warns of "stagnation and complacency, ultimately resulting in a loss of competitiveness and innovation" (p. 11). No quantitative growth figures.

### Labour market dynamics

- "In general, there is no extensive replacement of the human workforce" (p. 74). Replacement "varies across sectors, as depends on cost-benefit analysis" (p. 95). Per evolution path 3, AI "brings moderate job transformation" (p. 88).
- The European labour force "has stopped shrinking", through activation or migration policies, and does not add pressure on economies (p. 47). Per evolution path 3, it "stabilises/decreases, but not significantly" (p. 88). Employers "have good prospects for finding a qualified workforce" (pp. 47, 74). Skills shortages do not grow (p. 114).
- Repetitive-task jobs: 61% of respondents agreed they will stay relevant and make up 15–20% of all jobs (pp. 52, 55). 73% said replacement depends on industries' perceived economic benefits. 18% said employers may use fear of AI replacement to push for higher output, lower wages and poorer conditions. 34% said AI will become efficient and profitable for these tasks regardless. 20% said regulations improving conditions will make human work less profitable and lead to replacement (p. 52). The report concludes that several factors favour these jobs surviving, "although often for wrong reasons that lower the bar, for example through actions that make these jobs more precarious" (p. 52). "Human workers might, however, be pushed to lower levels in this type of jobs" (p. 74).
- Skills: technical and vocational skills are as important as human-centric skills. 82% of respondents agreed (32% strongly, 50% somewhat). 63% said many jobs will keep relying on technical skills, and 44% that advanced technical skills such as programming and data processing gain importance (p. 50). Human-centric skills gain importance in some jobs, especially for innovation, "but are not the main path to keep people employed" (p. 74). About a third of respondents still rated human-centric skills as more important (p. 50). Annex 4: "Technical skills still highly relevant" (p. 96). Green skills are not in high demand (p. 75). Individuals may invest moderately in transversal skills (p. 49).
- Employment forms: "some modest increases in nontraditional forms of employment, although without an extensive overhaul across the board" (p. 75). Annex 4: "No push for major transformations" (p. 95). Platform-based work becomes more frequent (p. 75). More than half of respondents expected nontraditional forms to become more common and union participation to decline (p. 51).
- Mobility: lay-offs and involuntary career changes "are not extensive and do not represent a major social challenge". Changes are mostly voluntary (pp. 48, 75). Many workers prefer to stay loyal to their employers (p. 48). The frequency of career changes "remains stable" (p. 90). Self-directed, purpose-driven careers apply to some segments but are "neither a need nor a choice due to preference for the general population" (p. 48).
- Workplace skills development: "no widespread need for heavy investment in continuing skills development in the workplace". It matters only in sectors most exposed to the twin transitions (pp. 46–47, 75). 68% of respondents expected little private-sector investment in adult training, especially for the unemployed or low-skilled (18% strongly, 50% somewhat). 61% said it depends on jobs and sectors. 28% said firms will rely on state-led or state-funded training. 31% said training under-represented groups is a priority only for the state and unions (pp. 52–53). Companies decide whether to train existing staff, hire new staff or train the general population "on the basis of the cost-effectiveness of each option" (p. 75).
- Wages: employers may exploit fears of AI replacement to lower wages and conditions (18% of respondents) (pp. 51–52, 55). No wage figures.

### Winners and losers

- Sectors and workers able to use AI gain efficiency, productivity and competitiveness. Sectors facing acute shortages hire from abroad and may improve conditions through social dialogue (pp. 47, 51, 56, 74).
- "Some parts of the population benefit, but many miss out on the opportunities, and some keep struggling" (p. 10). Benefits are mostly tapped by some sectors and industries (p. 95).
- Low-skilled workers, including those in repetitive-task jobs, face concerns about protection, wages and job quality (pp. 52, 55–56). People outside or on the edge of the labour market get less private training investment (pp. 49, 52).
- Workers in sectors more exposed to AI replacement or with fewer hiring difficulties may not see the protective outcomes of sectors with shortages. 34% of respondents agreed with this argument (p. 51).
- Regions and sectors still going through major energy transitions feel sustainability pressure (p. 47).
- Smaller companies need government promotion to benefit (p. 55).
- Inequality: "No additional pressure" (p. 96). "AI benefits are not universally shared" (p. 13).

### Household incomes and living standards

Most individuals cope with change and remain active in the labour market (p. 49). Many employers meet "manageable" skills needs "without putting additional pressure on their staff and undermining their working conditions" (p. 48). In repetitive jobs, wages and conditions may be pushed down (pp. 52, 55). Income levels: Not addressed.

### Government finances

The state bears "the larger share of responsibility and costs for broader adult training", especially for less advantaged groups or groups that participate less in training or employment (p. 56). Governments may or may not finance AI start-ups (p. 50). Defence and security spending competes with AI funding (pp. 73–74). Tax bases, revenue and debt: Not addressed.

### International economic patterns

Europe keeps attracting non-EU nationals, including through "moderately increased irregular migration flows" driven by geopolitical and environmental turbulence or the prospect of a better life (p. 47). International competition for talent is "not significant for most parts of the economy" and affects only a few sectors (pp. 47, 89). Sectors with acute shortages hire from abroad (p. 47). AI uptake and engagement vary across Member States (p. 73).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Skills shortages are acute only in exposed sectors (p. 47). Cheap labour can be cheaper than AI for repetitive tasks (pp. 52, 55). Prices of goods, essentials and assets: Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Most individuals can cope with change, perhaps with moderate personal investment in transversal skills (p. 49). Workers' technical skills "often remain relevant" (p. 46). In repetitive jobs, the threat of AI may be used to lower the bar on wages and conditions (p. 55).

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

About 60% of respondents agreed that by 2040 workers' social protection "will have become less effective and will not have managed to keep up with the challenges brought by AI". 31% said social dialogue will fail to create an updated protection network. 30% expected new frameworks to be developed, and 25% expected the modest pace to let social dialogue deliver protective agreements (p. 51). The conclusion: "there are significant concerns that social protection is likely to lose ground" (p. 51). Social protection frameworks "may still exist, but not fully capable to address challenges related to AI expansion and access to jobs" (p. 55). Labour relations are "Modestly peaceful; social protection frameworks do not always keep pace with new forms of work" (p. 95).

### Time for what matters

Purpose-driven careers affect the choices of only some segments of the workforce (pp. 48, 90). Fewer forced career changes and less pressure on working conditions (p. 48). Otherwise Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Trade union participation declines as nontraditional employment spreads, and social dialogue is less effective at updating protection (pp. 51, 55–56, 75). In sectors with shortages, social dialogue may produce satisfactory conditions (pp. 51, 56). Social dialogue is "more nuanced and context-dependent", focused on skills needs and training offers (p. 14). AI regulation protects citizens and consumers (p. 46). Democratic processes: Not addressed.

### A fair share of progress

AI advances "are not at the service of all sectors or for the greater public good" (p. 74). Spreading benefits depends on government investment in "far-reaching applications for the general population and for smaller companies" (p. 55). The scenario is a "missed chance to address pressing societal challenges, such as climate change, social cohesion and education" (p. 11).

## Other dynamics

- **Environment:** Sustainability gains no further traction in the initial assumptions (p. 46). The consolidated scenario softens this: "sustainability does not entirely lose its traction". Citizens' eco-values remain important or grow, but "general awareness does not always translate into specific consumer behaviour". For states and companies, sustainability is a priority only in some cases (p. 75). Respondents were split 47%/53% on whether there will be no further push for a green paradigm shift (p. 53). With fewer shocks, "there may be sufficient time for new greener practices to be established ... without the need for a major shift by 2040" (p. 75). Annex 4: "Not losing traction, but mostly a concern of individuals" (p. 96).
- **Education and training:** Traditional stakeholders meet moderate upskilling needs. Boundaries between education and training strands stay where they are today. Content is still generated mostly by traditional stakeholders, and the roles of teachers, trainers and guidance practitioners remain largely unchanged (pp. 48, 74–75, 114). AI "supports learning, guidance and counselling practices, without challenging the roles of practitioners" (p. 12).
- **Geopolitics:** Geopolitical security considerations may either restrain AI (security-driven regulation) or intensify investment (pp. 49, 55, 73–74).
- Safety, loss of control, health and information ecosystem: Not addressed.
