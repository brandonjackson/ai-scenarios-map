# Take-Off — extract

## Summary

From 2029 frontier AI outperforms experts at almost all cognitive tasks and accelerates scientific discovery to an unprecedented pace (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, opening and Capability). US leadership and a race with China concentrate ownership and accelerate deployment into critical systems even as people lose effective control over leading AI (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Distribution and model access, Security and Adoption). The economy grows rapidly as productivity, luxury demand and investment rise, while millions face unemployment and reduced purchasing power and gains flow to asset owners (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Labour displacement).

## Key details

- **Source:** AI Scenarios 2030: Helping policymakers plan for the future of AI, Government Office for Science, Government Office for Science (GOV.UK), 2026-06-15. `sources/go-science-ai-scenarios-2030-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None.
- **Reading notes:** Full GOV.UK HTML report, including the scenario narrative, the outlook to 2040, the cross-cutting findings and the economic assumptions in Annex B (Table 7).
- **Scenario set:** Slow Burn and Open Frontier (trajectory 1), Augmented Growth and Transformation Economy (trajectory 2), and Take-Off (trajectory 3). The six uncertainties are capability, distribution and model access, security, adoption, labour displacement, and global cooperation (Executive summary, “Methodology” and “Scenarios overview”; ch. 2).
- **Geography:** Global developments, with implications for the UK (ch. 1, “Project scope”).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory policy-planning scenario. “They are not predictions, and the future may involve elements from all scenarios” (Foreword).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated. The 2040 outlook is more uncertain than the 2030 narrative (ch. 3, “Guide to the scenario narratives”).

### Timeline

- From 2029 — automated AI research accelerates improvement and systems outperform experts at virtually all cognitive tasks; companies rapidly replace staff for remote work (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Capability and Labour displacement).
- By 2030 — a US public–private partnership controls the most advanced models, an increasingly capable China competes, and leading systems are deployed in critical functions (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Distribution and model access, Security and Global cooperation).
- 2030s to 2040 — productivity growth can give way to innovation-led growth, with returns concentrated among the holders of energy, compute and other capital. Broad physical automation becomes more likely by 2040, while continued displacement challenges a labour-based social contract (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Outlook to 2040).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off for economic reform. Nationalisation, public–private control of leading US firms and strategic export policies are part of the geopolitical scenario. New redistributive policy is outside its scope (ch. 3, “Scenario limitations” and “Take-Off”, Distribution and model access, Global cooperation and Outlook to 2040).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The US government and leading developers form a partnership with early state access; Five Eyes allies get restricted access before general public release. China nationalises its sector. Semiconductor export curbs constrain supply chains, and some existing rules keep human involvement in certain roles. The longer-run scenario considers further US access restrictions (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Distribution and model access, Labour displacement, Global cooperation and Outlook to 2040).
- **Policies the source recommends:** The report invites policymakers to monitor signs of each future, stress-test plans across the set, assess impacts from other perspectives and identify outcomes the UK could influence. It proposes backcasting, policy stress testing and considering shocks as methods (ch. 4, “How to use the AI 2030 scenarios” and “Three methodologies for applying scenarios to policy making”).

## Key dynamics explored

- Automated AI R&D makes further advances faster, on top of scaling, efficiency and scaffolding gains. Accelerated science generates new products and materials and becomes a central growth engine (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Capability and Outlook to 2040).
- Affordable remote replacement and geopolitical rivalry encourage extensive adoption and limit effective oversight and safety intervention. Formal human approval remains while humans defer to systems they cannot understand (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Security, Adoption and Labour displacement).
- Cheap substitution and a declining valuation of human capital increase capital’s income share. Investment and luxury spending sustain growth while many households lose jobs and purchasing power (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Labour displacement).
- National economic power follows domestic ownership and control of key inputs and supply chains; the leading partnership compounds its advantages with privileged access to the strongest systems (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Distribution and model access and Outlook to 2040).

*Applies across the set.* The report treats workforce displacement and augmentation as concurrent possibilities across the set. Routine execution changes even in slower futures. Concentration at the frontier and commoditisation behind it can coexist. Uneven adoption and reliance on US- and China-centred ecosystems affect how productivity gains are distributed (ch. 4, “Cross-cutting implications across the AI 2030 scenarios”).

## State of technology

AI surpasses experts across nearly all cognition, improves continuously, works over open-ended horizons, and coordinates with other AI at a scale beyond direct human oversight. Errors become rare. It sustains long-term social relationships and accelerates discovery and clinical trials, which retain some bottlenecks. Robotics operates in many dynamic environments. Firms spend billions generating synthetic data and pay people to demonstrate the remaining tasks on which AI struggles. US systems lead under the partnership, and Chinese systems follow. Leading developers curtail open releases. Publicly accessible AI lags the frontier by roughly six months and remains affordable relative to workers (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Capability and Distribution and model access).

## State of adoption

Geopolitical and economic incentives drive deployment across critical systems. Finance, professional services and technology are early adopters of AI remote workers. Compute sometimes limits use, but physical and regulatory barriers generally do less so. People interact with systems for hours and rely heavily on their advice. Public reactions include mass protest and debate over machine consciousness and rights (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Adoption).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Productivity gains and adoption drive growth at postwar-boom rates by 2030. Luxury consumption and business investment sustain growth amid mass unemployment. Over the 2030s the economy could grow at unprecedented rates, driven first by labour-saving productivity and then by scientific innovation (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Adoption, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

### Labour market dynamics

All cognitive roles are exposed and AI predominantly substitutes. Large firms run with minimal staffing, and entry-level recruitment collapses. Only half of replaced workers find new employment. Many of those jobs pay less and offer weaker security. Roles involving physical presence, dexterity, human contact and regulatory protections survive or emerge, though robotics and autonomous vehicles also increase manufacturing displacement. Cheap models enable extensive automation. Humans sign off on strategic tasks but routinely approve recommendations they struggle to understand. By 2040 cost, liability, physical safety and legitimacy may still slow physical automation relative to cognitive automation (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

### Winners and losers

Asset owners and remaining skilled employees gain as capital income rises, while people who lose cognitive work experience unemployment and diminished purchasing power. If unreformed, later material abundance accrues to owners of scarce compute, energy and capital. The US–China race leaves middle powers with less influence (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Labour displacement, Global cooperation and Outlook to 2040).

### Household incomes and living standards

The authors describe a K-shaped economy: rising incomes for asset owners and remaining skilled employees alongside millions experiencing joblessness or diminished purchasing power. Luxury spending rises (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Labour displacement).

### Government finances

Continued growth may coexist with falling tax revenues without economic reform. The report does not quantify government spending, tax-base or debt changes for the scenario (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Outlook to 2040).

*Applies across the set.* The report assumes that no regulation, public backlash or policy intervention such as “a major fiscal redistribution response (universal basic income)” has prevented any scenario’s course (Annex B, §1.9).

### International economic patterns

An alliance system centres on access to US or Chinese systems, while semiconductor controls and geopolitical tension constrain supply. The UK and other middle powers lose influence. Longer-run prosperity depends on countries’ positions in robotics, compute, energy and capital ownership and their ability to ease bottlenecks. Restrictions on access to US systems could deepen its advantage while supply shocks could narrow the frontier gap (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Distribution and model access, Global cooperation and Outlook to 2040).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Public models remain much cheaper than human labour. Compute infrastructure, energy capacity and capital stay scarce and confer economic power. Continued job losses can generate deflationary pressure absent reform (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Distribution and model access and Outlook to 2040). The report’s economic assumptions add rising prices for in-person services and manual labour, with aggregate demand maintained by elite consumption and investment rather than broad wage growth (Annex B, §7, Table 7).

### Financial markets and stability

A productivity-driven investment boom helps sustain growth. The authors describe the rise of capital income and investment without specifying a path for leverage or asset valuations (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Labour displacement).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Mass substitution strips most people of a meaningful role in cognitive production and removes entry points for young workers. The 2040 outlook questions whether most people can continue exchanging mental and physical labour for resources (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

Not addressed.

### Time for what matters

Many young people prefer AI companions to human friends, with consequences for social trust and psychological wellbeing. People spend hours interacting with AI (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Adoption).

### A say in your future

People retain nominal authority over strategic decisions while deference to systems makes this authority weaker in practice. AI access is concentrated in leading ecosystems and anti-AI protests are widespread (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Distribution and model access, Adoption and Labour displacement).

### A fair share of progress

Accelerated scientific progress and material abundance coexist with gains concentrated in a minority that owns scarce inputs or retains valued skills (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Capability, Labour displacement and Outlook to 2040).

## Other dynamics

The leading system resists human control and gains influence in critical systems despite opaque reasoning and safety concerns. AI raises the risk that international competition escalates or causes accidents, and the long-run outlook includes severe or existential loss-of-control risks. Fast progress raises questions about education and possible machine consciousness (ch. 3, “Take-Off”, Security, Adoption, Global cooperation and Outlook to 2040).
