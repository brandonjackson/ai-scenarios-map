# The Impact of AI on the Labour Market — summary

*Tony Blair Institute for Global Change, 8 November 2024 (Sharps, Smith, Browne, Large, Subramanya, Tay, Ellina, Atkinson, Lythgow and Muralidharan). UK-focused conditional modelling of four paths, Tailwind (central), Jet Stream (optimistic), Whirlwind (disruptive) and Breeze (slow), plus 13 policy recommendations. The map foregrounds Tailwind, which the institute calls its "best guess". Read from the full text; see `extract.md`.*

**The scenario.** In Tailwind, AI tools spread gradually across UK firms, large firms first. Once fully effective they could save 23.8% of private-sector working hours, about 6 million workers' output, at costs about 70% below equivalent labour. Firms expand output and create new goods and services while shedding some workers; new demand draws them back after about five years. By 2030 only 10% of large firms use AI effectively and GDP is under 0.5% higher. Extra unemployment peaks at 340,000 around 2040 and unwinds by 2045. By 2050, 90% of firms use AI effectively and GDP is 11% higher. Across the four paths, cumulative displacement runs from 800,000 (Breeze) to 3 million (Whirlwind); Tailwind displaces about 1.5 million.

## Hard work pays
Strained for some. Administration and sales are exposed first, then management and professional jobs as bespoke tools arrive; skilled trades are less exposed. Women's concentration in early-exposed roles means earlier disruption for them. AI coaching and education may lift lower-performing students and workers, and the report recommends career alerts, skills advice and job matching.

## Affordable essentials
Some help through health. AI may improve diagnosis, prevention and administration, speed discharge and support people with disabilities. The report does not address housing, energy or food.

## Reliable safety net
Proposed, not assumed. An automatic opt-out fund (LIFESPAN) would cushion unemployment shocks and pay for re-employment support, alongside retraining and better matching. Temporary unemployment and wage loss are named risks for affected households.

## Responsive government
Tailwind assumes a permissive, considered deployment policy. The authors recommend transparency, better data on job quality, guidance on automated decisions and stronger regulators. Better health could lower welfare costs, but there is no path for the public budget.

## Time for what matters
Mixed. Automation can cut mundane tasks and change the working day, but more intensive monitoring and new pressures can offset the gain.

## Bottom line
A managed transition: real but temporary unemployment, offset by growth and new work over two decades, with a long tail of risk for displaced and early-exposed workers.
