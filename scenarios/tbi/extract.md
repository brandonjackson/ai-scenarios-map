# The Impact of AI on the Labour Market — extract

## Summary

The report models four paths for UK labour demand under AI, with Tailwind as the central case (“The Macroeconomic Impact of AI on Labour Demand”). In Tailwind, applications gradually spread across UK firms, with potential private-sector time savings equivalent to nearly a quarter of the workforce’s hours once AI is fully effective. Firms expand output and develop new goods and services while displacing some workers, and new demand eventually draws displaced workers back into employment (“Executive Summary”; Tailwind, Figs. 6–8). The report also examines ways AI can improve education, health and job matching and help people remain in the workforce (“Labour Supply”).

## Key details

- **Source:** The Impact of AI on the Labour Market, Sam Sharps, Thomas Smith, James Browne, Oliver Large, Rhea Subramanya, PeiChin Tay, Danae Ellina, Isabel Atkinson, Jess Lythgow, Rithika Muralidharan, Tony Blair Institute for Global Change, 2024-11-08. `sources/tony-blair-institute-ai-labour-market-2024/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** Tailwind (central), Jet Stream (optimistic), Whirlwind (disruptive), and Breeze (slower and limited), varying technology cost, adoption, labour shedding and the lag to new task creation. The catalogue entry captures the set and foregrounds Tailwind.
- **Geography:** United Kingdom, principally the private sector for the labour-demand model (“Definition and Scope”; “Labour Demand”).
- **Authors' framing:** Conditional modelling of four illustrative paths plus policy recommendations (“The Macroeconomic Impact of AI on Labour Demand”; “Executive Summary”).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** The institute calls Tailwind its best guess and judges Whirlwind less likely. It gives no probabilities (chapter 2, “Summary”).

### Timeline

2030 — in Tailwind, 10% of large firms use AI effectively, GDP is less than 0.5% higher and unemployment is about 30,000 higher. 2035 — GDP is around 2% higher. 2040 — additional unemployment peaks at 340,000. 2045 — the increase unwinds. 2050 — GDP is 11% higher and 90% of firms make effective use of AI (Tailwind, Figs. 6–8; chapter 2 “Summary”).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** On in Tailwind and Jet Stream. Policy facilitates adoption and can support workers through more rapid transitions (“The Macroeconomic Impact of AI on Labour Demand”; recommendations 1–13).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Tailwind assumes permissive and considered deployment policy, Jet Stream also assumes government leads by example by adopting AI early, Whirlwind allows regulatory barriers to slow commercialisation and economic re-employment, and Breeze assumes regulatory barriers hinder take-up (chapter 2, scenario descriptions).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Update education and curricula, support AI-enabled workplace training, create firm pathfinders and public-sector labour-shortage challenge prizes, provide workers with career alerts, an opt-out LIFESPAN fund, adult retraining and matching tools, improve data on job quality, create a disclosure taskforce, clarify automated decision-making rules, strengthen regulators and explore contingencies through scenario analysis (recommendations 1–13).

## Key dynamics explored

- Classify tasks for over 900 occupations, estimate time savings for each AI technology and vary economic take-up according to cost, suitability, firm size and time. Combine those estimates with assumptions on labour shedding, recruitment and investment (chapter 2, Figs. 2–6).
- The labour-substitution effect raises initial job losses. Higher productivity and wages support demand, and new products create tasks and jobs with a lag that differs across runs (chapter 2, “The Macroeconomic Impact of AI on Labour Demand”).
- AI can separately affect labour supply by improving skills, health and matching. These channels are discussed separately from the main GDP and unemployment estimates (chapter 3).

## State of technology

The report covers contemporary data-driven software and AI-enabled hardware, including free tools, subscription software, bespoke systems, lower-cost equipment and higher-cost equipment. It excludes AGI and more advanced capability from the main modelling. On average, cost savings relative to equivalent labour are about 70% in Tailwind, varying by product type (“Definition and Scope”; Tailwind discussion).

## State of adoption

Large firms can experiment first; free tools, subscription software and lower-cost hardware spread sooner than bespoke AI or higher-cost equipment. Specialist providers can develop repeatable products for smaller firms. Tailwind has 90% effective adoption by around 2050, Jet Stream receives stronger investment and faster platformisation, Whirlwind matches Jet Stream’s deployment, and the failure of platformisation for costly tools in Breeze limits adoption to just above 50% in 2050 (Figs. 6, 9, 14).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Tailwind combines an 8% labour-productivity effect with a 3% GDP boost from the induced increase in investment and capital, yielding about 11% higher GDP by mid-century and a peak annual-growth boost near 0.7 percentage points in the late 2030s. By 2050 gains are about 14% in Jet Stream and Whirlwind and 5% in Breeze. Whirlwind has separate waves of growth as deployment and re-employment arrive (Figs. 8, 11, 13, 15, 17). AI-enabled education could separately add around 6% cumulatively over 60 years (chapter 3, “AI-Enabled Education”).

### Labour market dynamics

Tailwind and Jet Stream displace about 1.5 million jobs cumulatively, with peak annual redundancies of about 100,000 and 137,000, respectively. Peak additional unemployment is 340,000 around 2040 in Tailwind and 290,000 in 2035 in Jet Stream. Whirlwind displaces 3 million jobs cumulatively, adds up to 274,000 annual redundancies and temporarily raises unemployment by about 1.5 million around 2040. Breeze displaces 800,000 jobs cumulatively, with roughly 60,000 annual redundancies at peak and a 400,000 unemployment increase by the late 2040s. New-task lags are five years in Tailwind, three in Jet Stream and ten in Whirlwind and Breeze (chapter 2, Figs. 7, 10, 12, 16, 18).

### Winners and losers

Cognitive task exposure is largest in administration and sales for general tools and later extends to management and professional jobs with bespoke tools. Complex manual and skilled-trade work affords less scope for time savings. Women’s concentration in early-exposed roles brings earlier labour-market disruption. AI education and coaching may raise the performance of lower-performing students and employees (chapter 2, “Summary”; chapters 3–4).

### Household incomes and living standards

Higher worker income channels productivity improvements into demand; temporary unemployment and wage loss create risks for affected households (Tailwind discussion; recommendation 6).

### Government finances

Better health can lower welfare costs, and prevention or faster care may reduce the costs of treatment. The recommended LIFESPAN fund would support re-employment and cushion income shocks. The report provides no comprehensive path for the public budget (chapter 3, “AI-Enabled Health Care”; recommendation 6).

### International economic patterns

The analysis focuses on the UK. It cites greater near-term exposure in services-led developed economies but also greater readiness, plus international examples of policy and faster new-technology uptake (“Our Starting Point”; chapter 5, policy recommendations).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Deployment economics depend on AI cost relative to labour. Lower-cost standard tools are more accessible to small firms, and competition, uptake and repeatable products are expected to lower unit costs (chapter 2, Tailwind, Jet Stream and Breeze discussions).

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Learning and training tools can improve outcomes for lower-performing students and workers; early warnings, skills advice and job matching can help workers navigate changes (recommendations 1–2, 5, 7–8).

### Affordable essentials

AI may improve diagnosis, prevention and administration in healthcare, including faster discharge and assistive services for people with disabilities or short-term health conditions (chapter 3, “AI-Enabled Health Care”).

### Reliable safety net

The authors propose an automatic opt-out contribution fund that would cushion unemployment shocks and finance re-employment support, along with revised training and better matching (recommendations 6–8).

### Time for what matters

Automation can reduce mundane tasks and change how people spend the working day. More intensive monitoring and new work pressures can offset improvements (“How AI Could Change the Workplace Experience”).

### A say in your future

Worker agency in job matching and automated decisions matters. The authors recommend transparency, better worker-welfare data, guidance on automated decisions and stronger enforcement (chapter 3, “AI-Enabled Job Matching”; recommendations 9–12).

### A fair share of progress

Broad adoption, coordinated public-sector projects and wider access to education can help make benefits accessible across firm sizes and worker groups (recommendations 1–4).

## Other dynamics

The paper treats workplace inclusion, health and safety, and surveillance or biased decision-making as distinct from the modelled quantity of work (chapter 4; recommendations 9–12).
