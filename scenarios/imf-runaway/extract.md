# Runaway Diffusion — extract

## Summary
AI, assumed to reach human expert level within five years and to be technically able to perform most cognitive and physical tasks by the early 2030s, "diffuses rapidly across sectors under minimal regulatory constraints, in part propelled by intelligent AI agents that drive the process" (pp. 13, 25–26). A small number of large firms with better models, data and compute dominate. A profit-driven private sector deploys AI in finance, health care and education with minimal human supervision and in manufacturing, construction and mining through robotics, where "robots produce more robots"; AI-enabled firms run with few or no employees, and humans become "an optional choice rather than a necessity" (p. 26). Under current policies, growth is stronger than in the baseline, but displacement is broad and "more front-loaded," capital owners capture a large share of gains, capital and production move to favourable jurisdictions, and job losses weaken household balance sheets and banks (pp. 2, 14–15). Rapid, widespread automation intensifies pressures that potentially outpace "the adaptive capacity of labor markets, fiscal systems, and financial oversight" (p. 15).

- **Scenario set:** Two scenarios — Baseline Diffusion ("Slower AI diffusion due to various frictions or societal pushback") and Runaway Diffusion, which Figure 7 labels "Runaway diffusion: Rapid AI diffusion with limited friction" (p. 13). They "shared a common assumption on frontier AI capability, differing only in diffusion speed and institutional absorption" (p. 13). "The decisive difference between the scenarios was how quickly societies absorb and deploy these technologies" (p. 25). Scenarios were developed with the Windfall Trust (p. 25).
- **Geography:** Global, with distinctions between advanced economies (the United States named as home to major AI service providers), emerging markets, and low-income countries (pp. 14–15, 26). Capability advances are "driven by technological advancements in major economies, particularly in the US and China" (p. 25).
- **Authors' framing:** Planning scenario / stress test. The exercise "aimed at mapping out a range of plausible yet challenging economic and financial implications of transformative AI development" (p. 25); "In the spirit of preparing for out-of-distribution events, both scenarios assumed AI advances on the accelerated timelines projected by the heads of leading AI companies, which were significantly faster than what is embedded in median economic forecasts" (p. 25). "The central policy challenge is not to predict a single AI future" (p. 3). PIIE: "The real preparedness gap lies in the alternative: rapid transformation that outpaces the resilience of current institutions."
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated. The capability timelines are "significantly faster than what is embedded in median economic forecasts" (p. 25), and "current real interest rates provide limited evidence that markets are pricing in nearterm, extremely rapid AIdriven growth" (p. 7). PIIE: "a stress test"; "Some technologists would consider this pace modest; many economists consider it unusually fast."

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Off — diffusion proceeds "under minimal regulatory constraints," "no strong steps are taken to meaningfully slow down its advance," and fiscal and monetary policy stay within existing frameworks (pp. 25–26).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:**
  - "Minimal regulatory constraints" on diffusion (p. 26).
  - "Education and training programs focus on leveraging AI technologies" (p. 26).
  - "Although AI has moved to the center stage of societal debate, no strong steps are taken to meaningfully slow down its advance" (p. 26).
  - "AI technologies are adopted into fiscal and monetary policy operations, such as data analysis, forecasting, fiscal operations, and policy decisions" (p. 26).
  - "Fiscal and monetary policies were assumed to operate under existing frameworks" (p. 25). The "Status Quo" session assumed no policy changes; the "Governments Respond" session let participants assume policy changes "that they perceive as likely or important given the details and assumptions of the scenario" (p. 25). The note does not report Governments Respond results separately for this scenario.
  - Political incentives to restrict immigration strengthen (p. 15). *[Stated as an incentive, not an enacted policy.]*
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - *This scenario:*
    - Tax-base erosion issues "are more pronounced in the runaway scenario and most acute for countries that primarily consume AI services, rather than produce them"; hence rebalance tax "toward better capturing economic rents while minimizing distortions on investment decisions, supported by a stronger AI-enabled tax administration" (p. 16).
    - Employment-linked social insurance becomes "less effective under rapid AI diffusion," pointing to "transfer systems that can scale quickly, target vulnerable groups, and operate with weaker ties to formal employment"; "In more extreme cases... universal basic income (UBI)–type programs" (p. 17).
    - "Limited fiscal space may restrict government policy options, particularly under the runaway diffusion scenario" (p. 17).
    - Monetary: the value of "scenario-based analysis and more explicit consideration of alternative policy options" rises, "particularly in the runaway scenario" (p. 17).
    - In both scenarios: support adoption by financially constrained SMEs and state-owned enterprises via shared infrastructure or public-good AI models (p. 17); develop "public-good" capabilities and regulatory bodies to oversee AI deployment (p. 18).
    - Day 1: "if automation accelerates or diffuses across multiple sectors simultaneously, broader income support mechanisms may be required to preserve aggregate demand and social cohesion" (p. 8); "Under more extreme scenarios... prompting consideration of nonstandard policies such as universal basic income" (p. 11).
  - *Applies across the set:*
    - Four headline areas: "(1) strengthening social spending and tax systems; (2) upgrading monetary diagnostics and scenario tools under noisier price signals and uncertainty about the neutral rate; (3) expanding supervisory technology and macroprudential monitoring to new transition risks; and (4) reinforcing competition, diffusion support, and international coordination on standards and taxation" (p. 2; restated p. 16).
    - Figure 8 (p. 16). Fiscal: erosion of labour tax bases; rising spending in social welfare and cyber security; de-linking safety nets from employment; shift toward capital and rent taxation; targeted public investment in AI infrastructure. Monetary: noisier inflation signals; uncertainty about stance and transmission; greater reliance on scenario analysis; new tools for a digitalised economy and finance. Financial stability: AI-linked investment booms and leverage cycles; concentration, interconnectedness and solvency risks; expanded regulatory and supervisory perimeter; forward-looking, scenario-based risk management; enhanced monitoring of AI exposures; expanded scope for lender of last resort. Structural and international: competition and governance of AI services; skills upgrading and labour reallocation; public-good AI capabilities; international cooperation on standards and taxation.
    - Tax: "Avoiding blunt 'robot taxes,' strengthening capital income taxation at the individual level with safeguards against base erosion, and leveraging AI to improve compliance and enforcement are important across scenarios" (p. 16).
    - Social protection: "De-linking social protection and safety nets from employment status, facilitating labor market adjustment, and supporting skill adaptation are central to building public support for AI adoption" (p. 17).
    - Public investment in energy, data centres and connectivity, with "coordinated diffusion support" (p. 17).
    - Labour market (Day 1): monitor task composition, expertise intensity and sectoral employment rather than aggregate "AI exposure"; facilitate reallocation "through retraining, improved job matching, and wage insurance—rather than attempting to impede technological adoption"; public investment in complementary sectors; transitional adjustment assistance and expanded wage insurance (p. 8). "Humancentric" upgrades to unemployment insurance; unions and sectoral bargaining (p. 11).
    - Monetary: adapt to uncertainty around equilibrium rates and noisier inflation signals (p. 20); rethink central bank mandates and governance if digital money spreads (p. 17).
    - Financial: better visibility of AI-related systemic risk, efficient macroprudential buffers, contain leverage at banks and systemically important nonbanks, better data on AI exposures (pp. 17–18); lender-of-last-resort frameworks may need to consider systemically important nonbanks, with safeguards against moral hazard; forward-looking, scenario-based supervision; robust capital and liquidity buffers; stress tests against valuation and concentration shocks (p. 18); "Macroprudential oversight should extend to third party and vendors" (p. 20).
    - Structural: preserve "contestability in models, data, and compute"; upgrade education and training; reform labour contracts "including mechanisms for broader sharing of gains" (p. 18); industrial policy for clean power and smart grids; avoid regulation that reinforces concentration; interoperable safety and reporting standards (p. 20).
    - International: "Rapid AI diffusion risks concentrating production, capital, and rents in a subset of economies"; cooperate "on taxation principles and interoperable assurance frameworks" (p. 18); multilateral surveillance should treat AI "as a potentially asymmetric global shock" (p. 21).
    - Implementation and trust: build social consensus "particularly when distributional impacts are front-loaded and concentrated"; clear communication; account for "low take-up, administrative complexity, and social stigma" (p. 20).
    - Data and diagnostics: track AI diffusion, sectoral concentration and labour market exposure "in near real time"; close gaps to "distinguish between gradual and more rapid diffusion paths" (pp. 20–21); fiscal analysis should include persistent declines in labour income share; "regular scenario refreshes" (p. 21).
    - Sequencing: "Countries should carefully consider sequencing and prioritization considering their capacity and policy space" (p. 19).
    - PIIE adds: begin "developing playbooks"; new frameworks for aggregate demand and inflation "when traditional labor market indicators diverge from AI-driven productivity trends"; "tax systems or other capital-sharing mechanisms"; adapt competition and data-governance frameworks; track four indicator groups (AI research breakthroughs including automated AI research, product releases and benchmarks, diffusion and deployment, and financial market trends) to signal "when to shift from incremental adaptation to more transformative policy responses."

## Key dynamics explored
*This scenario:*
- Minimal regulation plus AI agents that drive diffusion lead to rapid adoption across sectors (p. 26).
- A private sector "focused on cost optimization and profit maximization" drives diffusion, so humans become optional in customer-facing jobs and firms operate with few or no employees (p. 26).
- AI integrated into robotics expands production capacity, and with growing demand for this new "work" force, "robots produce more robots" (p. 26).
- Greater confidence in and social acceptance of AI lead to stronger growth through productivity and capital accumulation (p. 14).
- Task automation and AI-powered robots displace routine cognitive and physical jobs in manufacturing and services (p. 14) *[paragraph opens "Rapid AI diffusion"; not labelled by scenario name]*; displacement is "more front-loaded" (p. 2).
- Reduced frictions increase firm mobility, directing capital to countries with favourable tax regimes, regulatory clarity, reliable energy and data access; robotics compressing labour costs accelerate reshoring of manufacturing to advanced economies; services concentrate in digital hubs (p. 15).
- Extensive automation reduces migrants' economic role and strengthens political incentives to restrict immigration (p. 15).
- Large-scale job losses weaken household balance sheets and raise default risk, pressuring banks with high consumer-credit exposure (p. 15).
- Fast supply expansion generates both upward and downward price pressures, complicating monetary policy (p. 17).
- Lower employment and wage income erode personal income tax bases, which higher corporate taxes only partly offset because of capital mobility, concentration and tax competition (p. 16).
- Rapid, widespread automation intensifies pressures, potentially outpacing the adaptive capacity of labour markets, fiscal systems and financial oversight (p. 15); when diffusion is economy-wide, aggregate productivity growth "can mask growing distributional pressures, concentrated pricing power, and balance sheet vulnerabilities" (p. 19).

*Applies across the set:*
- AI raises output by automating more tasks, raising productivity within tasks and accelerating R&D; low elasticities of substitution, bottlenecks and diminishing returns limit aggregate gains (p. 6). "The share of tasks automated is likely to be more important for growth than productivity gains within individual tasks" (p. 6).
- Automating AI research may create strong feedback, but "intelligence explosions are not inevitable" (p. 7).
- Market expectations of transformative AI raise real interest rates ahead of realised gains, which worsens debt dynamics for highly indebted sovereigns (p. 7).
- Automating high-expertise tasks lowers barriers to entry (more jobs, lower wages); automating low-expertise tasks raises the expertise of remaining work (higher wages, fewer jobs) (pp. 7–8).
- AI substitutes for labour in "intelligence" tasks, moving workers to "physical" tasks; wages may rise and then fall at higher adoption if displaced labour crowds into sectors with fixed capital; output and wages may plateau if physical constraints bind (p. 8).
- High data and compute requirements create barriers to entry and concentration, which could dampen innovation and spillovers (p. 14).
- Expanded supply plus higher investment demand set against weaker household consumption make net price effects uncertain (p. 14).
- AI-driven trading "could heighten herding risks, decouple market movements from fundamentals, amplify bubbles, and elevate volatility" (p. 15).
- Expectations that adjust faster than realised productivity lead to "asset repricing, leverage stress, and feedback loops between the real economy and financial markets" (p. 19).
- High perceived risks make adoption hesitant and uneven (p. 20). *[This mechanism runs counter to this scenario's premise and is stated generally.]*

## State of technology
*This scenario:*
- "AI technologies continue to be dominated by a small number of large firms that train better models, collect better data, and have a disproportionate share of computation resources at their disposal" (p. 26).
- "Intelligent AI agents" help drive diffusion (p. 26).
- AI performs cognitive tasks in finance, health care and education "under minimal human supervision" (p. 26).
- AI is "deeply integrated into automated robotic and machinery systems"; "robots produce more robots" (p. 26).
- Supply-chain management and transportation "benefit greatly" (p. 26).

*Applies across the set (shared assumption):*
- Capability is held fixed across the two scenarios so discussion can "abstract from uncertainty about technical feasibility" (p. 13).
- AI attains "human expert-level capabilities over the next five years, driven by technological advancements in major economies, particularly in the US and China"; by the early 2030s it can perform "most cognitive and physical tasks" and replace workers across sectors via robotics (p. 25); body text: "a wide range of cognitive and manual tasks" (p. 13).
- These timelines follow "the accelerated timelines projected by the heads of leading AI companies" (p. 25).

*Applies across the set (Day 1 state of play):*
- AGI estimates of "8–10 years now viewed as conservative" by the "San Francisco Consensus" (p. 4); drivers are falling compute costs, data-centre investment and hardware–software feedback loops, despite "jagged" capabilities (pp. 4–5, footnote 2); AGI is the point at which AI becomes "a broadly deployable, autonomous 'dropin- worker'" (p. 5).
- Inference prices for some frontier models have fallen "by over 99 percent" (p. 5).
- In multi-agent systems, "rules of the game" matter more than marginal intelligence gains (p. 5).
- Generative AI needs heavy compute investment, concentrating power among "a small number of dominant firms and 'hyper-scalers'" (p. 10).
- Energy, grid and data-centre bottlenecks (pp. 2, 6, 17); clean power and smart grids needed for rising compute demand (p. 20).
- PIIE: AI systems are "remarkably capable at programming" (Claude Code was used to build Claude Cowork), raising "the real possibility of recursive self-improvement."
- Not addressed: open vs closed models; pricing to users beyond inference-cost declines.

## State of adoption
*This scenario:*
- "AI diffuses rapidly across sectors under minimal regulatory constraints" (p. 26); "adoption is rapid and widespread, leading to economy-wide automation" (p. 13); "adoption accelerates broadly, with rapid expansion of automation across services and industry" (p. 2).
- Sectors: finance, health care and education (cognitive tasks); customer-facing jobs; manufacturing, construction and mining (robotics); supply-chain management and transportation (p. 26).
- Firms: the private sector drives diffusion; "AI-enabled firms operate with a small number of or no employees" (p. 26).
- Countries: "Cross-country differences in AI-related preparedness (for example, digital infrastructure, human capital, institutions) remain, with some nations catching up and others falling behind" (p. 26). Countries lacking favourable conditions see "slower AI diffusion" (p. 15).
- Governments: AI is adopted into fiscal and monetary operations (p. 26).
- Drivers: minimal regulation; AI agents; cost and profit motives; "greater confidence in and social acceptance of AI technologies" (pp. 14, 26).
- Skills: education and training "focus on leveraging AI technologies" (p. 26).
- Societal debate: AI is "center stage," but nothing meaningfully slows it (p. 26).

*Applies across the set:*
- Adoption is generally limited by "regulatory uncertainty, compliance burdens, organizational inertia, and trust issues" and behavioural barriers (p. 5); "a sequence of human decisions" (p. 4).
- Early movers and digitally mature firms and countries consolidate advantages (p. 19).
- Many emerging and developing countries have lower exposure and weaker readiness (p. 9).
- Financially constrained SMEs and state-owned enterprises may need government help in both scenarios (p. 17).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
*This scenario:*
- "AI is expected to boost productivity and output over the next five years, with stronger effects under runaway diffusion"; global growth rises through productivity and capital accumulation, "particularly in the runaway diffusion scenario, supported by greater confidence in and social acceptance of AI technologies" (p. 14).
- Robotics expands production capacity in manufacturing, construction and mining (p. 26).
- When diffusion is rapid and economy-wide, "aggregate productivity growth alone becomes an insufficient guide for policy" (p. 19).

*Applies across the set:*
- "Across both trajectories, productivity gains could be substantial but unevenly distributed within and across countries" (p. 2); "substantial but structurally uneven" (p. 19).
- Bottlenecks and low substitution elasticities can make aggregate growth modest (p. 6); research automation may yield "bounded or explosive outcomes" (p. 7); "neither sharp wage collapses nor explosive growth is likely without a major structural change" if physical constraints bind (p. 8).
- Figure 5 (from Cerutti and others 2025) shows cross-country real-GDP deviations after 10 years under high- and low-TFP-growth cases (p. 10; US and the two other advanced-economy groups shown are above the corresponding world averages; the rest of the world, low-income groups, emerging groups and China are below them. These are Cerutti and colleagues’ cases, distinct from the note’s Day 2 scenarios).
- Even if AI raises aggregate income, "the transition may involve prolonged periods of labor displacement, income concentration, and financial stress" (p. 19).

### Labour market dynamics
*This scenario:*
- "Rapid AI diffusion leads to broad job displacement. Task automation and AI-powered robots displace routine cognitive and physical jobs in both manufacturing and service sectors" (p. 14) *[unlabelled "rapid diffusion" passage]*.
- "More front-loaded labor displacement" (p. 2).
- "AI is displacing many jobs. In many parts of the economy, humans are still paid to perform work but are increasingly seen as an optional choice rather than a necessity" (p. 26); in customer-facing jobs, "human workers become optional" (p. 26).
- "Some high-skilled workers in occupations that complement AI technologies benefit disproportionately from new AI capabilities" (p. 14) *[unlabelled]*.
- Labour tax bases erode "particularly under rapid diffusion, even after accounting for potentially higher wages for remaining workers" (p. 16).
- Employment-linked social insurance becomes less effective "as job opportunities decline" (p. 17).
- Slack indicators are less informative "as human employment plays a minor role in defining capacity constraints"; AI adoption may reduce "labor's bargaining power" (p. 17).
- Migrants' economic role shrinks (p. 15).
- PIIE: "Jobs are displaced faster than labor markets can adapt."
- Not addressed for this scenario: age-specific effects. The note's statements on older and younger workers and participation sit in the baseline-scenario passage (p. 14).

*Applies across the set:*
- Effects depend on task composition and expertise, not only headline displacement (p. 2); expertise explains "roughly one‑third of observed wage variation" (p. 8).
- Reskilling is less reliable when target occupations are shrinking, reflecting "the accelerating depreciation of human capital" (p. 8, footnote 3).
- "Under more extreme scenarios, the traditional link between employment and income may weaken" (p. 11).
- Displacement costs fall on incumbent workers and borrowers; gains go to new entrants and AI-intensive firms (p. 15).
- Persistent declines in labour income share are flagged for fiscal analysis (p. 21).

### Winners and losers
*This scenario:*
- Winners: a "small number of large firms" with better models, data and compute (p. 26); capital owners, who "capture a large share of the gains" (p. 14) *[unlabelled]*; high-skilled AI-complementary workers (p. 14) *[unlabelled]*; countries with favourable tax regimes, regulatory clarity, reliable energy and data access; advanced economies (reshoring); digital hubs (p. 15); the United States as home to major AI providers (p. 14).
- Losers: workers in routine cognitive and physical jobs (p. 14); customer-facing workers (p. 26); migrants (p. 15); countries lacking enabling conditions, which "face capital outflows and slower AI diffusion" (p. 15); "others falling behind" in preparedness (p. 26); countries that consume rather than produce AI services, on tax revenue (p. 16); households and banks with high consumer-credit exposure (p. 15).
- "AI also has significant impact on wealth inequality" (p. 14). PIIE: "Economic power concentrates in a few firms and locations. Inequality spikes."

*Applies across the set:*
- Concentration among core AI service providers (p. 14); "winner-take-most" dynamics (pp. 2, 9).
- Metropolitan innovation hubs gain while regions with weak digital and energy infrastructure fall behind (p. 14).
- Advanced economies gain more and emerging markets and low-income countries less; advanced economies may face more political resistance (p. 14).

### Household incomes and living standards
*This scenario:*
- "Large-scale job losses weaken household balance sheets and raise default risks" (p. 15).
- "Sharp declines in household and firm income during periods of rapid adoption weaken debt servicing capacity" (p. 15).
- Humans still paid to work in many parts of the economy, but as "an optional choice" (p. 26).

*Applies across the set:*
- "Household consumption weakens as job displacement increases" (p. 14).
- Broader income support may be needed "to preserve aggregate demand and social cohesion" (p. 8).

### Government finances
*This scenario:*
- Personal income tax bases weaken "particularly under rapid diffusion"; the problems "are more pronounced in the runaway scenario and most acute for countries that primarily consume AI services, rather than produce them" (p. 16).
- Employment-linked social insurance becomes less effective; "in more extreme cases, social spending needs could rise sharply," increasing pressure to consider UBI-type programmes (p. 17).
- "Limited fiscal space may restrict government policy options, particularly under the runaway diffusion scenario" (p. 17).
- AI is used in fiscal operations (p. 26).
- PIIE: "Fiscal systems strain under eroding labor tax bases and rising social demands."

*Applies across the set:*
- Shifting tax from labour to capital "may only partially offset these losses given capital mobility, market concentration, and international tax competition" (p. 16).
- Spending pressure on social welfare and cyber security (Figure 8, p. 16).
- Higher real rates priced ahead of gains worsen sovereign debt dynamics (p. 7); fiscal policy must manage "pressures on public debt dynamics during the transition" (p. 20).
- Preexisting fiscal constraints limit options, "particularly for low-income countries and fragile states" (p. 19).
- PIIE: "approximately three-quarters of federal tax revenue" in the United States comes from labour income; many countries "may struggle to tax AI-driven gains given mobile capital and the concentration of AI profits in a small number of jurisdictions."

### International economic patterns
*This scenario:*
- "Reduced frictions increase firm mobility, directing capital to countries with favorable tax regimes, regulatory clarity, reliable energy supply, and data access. Reshoring of manufacturing to advanced economies accelerates as robotics compress labor costs, while services concentrate in digital hubs. The timing and scale of this substitution depend on technology prices and cost efficiency. Countries lacking these enabling conditions face capital outflows and slower AI diffusion" (p. 15).
- "Extensive automation reduces the economic role of migrants and strengthens political incentives to restrict immigration" (p. 15).
- Preparedness gaps remain, "with some nations catching up and others falling behind" (p. 26).
- "Rapid AI diffusion risks concentrating production, capital, and rents in a subset of economies with strong infrastructure and AI capabilities" (p. 18).
- Stronger infrastructure, human capital and finance let advanced economies, especially the United States, "benefit more from rapid diffusion" (p. 14).

*Applies across the set:*
- "Cross-country inequalities widen" (p. 14); emerging markets and low-income countries gain less (p. 14).
- Migration from lower- to higher-income countries "likely to decline as labor demand in advanced economies falls" (p. 15).
- Day 1: reshoring undermines export-led growth; capital outflows from less-prepared economies; "persistent cycle of industrial decline" (p. 11); the digital frontier as "a new barrier to global convergence" (p. 11).
- Risk of emerging and developing economies being "locked into low diffusion equilibria" (p. 18).

### Market pricing and scarcity
*This scenario:*
- "AI-driven productivity gains can generate both upward and downward price pressures, especially under runaway diffusion where supply capacities expand quickly" (p. 17).
- Robots produce more robots to meet growing demand for this new "work" force (p. 26).
- PIIE: "Central banks face new challenges as inflation dynamics shift."

*Applies across the set:*
- Net price effect "uncertain and depends on the balance between expanded supply and subdued demand" (p. 14).
- "Large relative price changes complicate inflation diagnostics" (p. 17).
- Rising demand for energy and critical raw materials (p. 14).
- Uncertainty over the "level and volatility of the neutral interest rate" (p. 17).
- PIIE: rapid productivity growth "could push up the neutral interest rate"; "efficiency gains may exert disinflationary pressure"; relative price changes include "changes in the relative value of labor."

### Financial markets and stability
*This scenario:*
- "In the runaway scenario, large-scale job losses weaken household balance sheets and raise default risks, putting pressure on banking systems with high exposure to consumer credit" (p. 15).
- The same paragraph continues (not restricted to runaway by name): AI-driven trading could "heighten herding risks, decouple market movements from fundamentals, amplify bubbles, and elevate volatility and uncertainty"; "elevated leverage to finance AIrelated investment, coupled with rapid capital obsolescence risks, increases uncertainty around future earnings and asset valuations, complicating credit risk assessments by banks"; "greater concentration in the technology sector elevates single-source exposures, undermining risk diversification and amplifying macrofinancial feedback loops" (p. 15).
- Falls in household and firm income "during periods of rapid adoption" transmit into "banking losses and tighter credit conditions" (p. 15).
- Rapid automation may outpace the adaptive capacity of financial oversight (p. 15).
- AI used in policy operations (p. 26). Not addressed: AI adoption within the financial sector specifically for this scenario (contrast the baseline, where it is regulation-constrained).
- PIIE: "financial markets grow volatile."

*Applies across the set:*
- AI adoption "can amplify investment booms and market concentration, creating new transition risks even when long-term productivity prospects improve"; "vulnerabilities may differ across scenarios" (p. 17).
- Valuation cycles, data-centre and energy financing structures, and concentration propagate shocks; many vulnerabilities arise "well before any new steady state is reached" (p. 19).
- Systemically important nonbanks may need lender-of-last-resort coverage; reduced diversification and uncertain collateral complicate buffer calibration (p. 18).
- PIIE: "stranded assets, debt overhang, and the potential for sharp corrections if expectations disappoint"; "highly levered systems are vulnerable to sudden shifts in expectations, even when long-run fundamentals remain solid."

## Impact on the social contract

*Applies across the set:* Expertise is "a central determinant of wages" (p. 8); reskilling may be unreliable as human capital depreciates faster (p. 8, footnote 3). PIIE: detaching safety nets from employment "would reduce work incentives."

### Affordable essentials

### A say in your future
*This scenario:* "Although AI has moved to the center stage of societal debate, no strong steps are taken to meaningfully slow down its advance" (p. 26). Political incentives to restrict immigration strengthen (p. 15). AI is used in "policy decisions" (p. 26).

*Applies across the set:* Public trust "determines the stability of the transition" (p. 2); governance should include "the perspectives of workers, firms, civil society, and affected communities" (p. 12); unions and sectoral bargaining support "participatory governance" (p. 11); advanced economies could face more political resistance (p. 14); adjustment mechanisms should "build and sustain social consensus, particularly when distributional impacts are front-loaded and concentrated" (p. 20). PIIE: displacement "could outpace the adaptation of our institutions and generate social and political pressures that complicate policy responses."

### A fair share of progress
*This scenario:* Capital owners capture a large share of gains (p. 14) *[unlabelled]*; a small number of large firms dominate (p. 26); production, capital and rents concentrate in a subset of economies (p. 18). PIIE: "Inequality spikes."

*Applies across the set:* Without proactive institutional responses, gains "are likely to remain concentrated" (p. 12); public-good AI capabilities (pp. 16, 18); "mechanisms for broader sharing of gains" in labour contracts (p. 18); whether gains become "durable and broadly shared improvements in welfare" is central (p. 19). PIIE: capital-sharing mechanisms; "steer the transition toward broad, shared prosperity."

## Other dynamics
*This scenario:*
- "Intelligent AI agents" partly drive the diffusion process (p. 26).

*Applies across the set:*
- Safety and governance: limited standards, reporting and enforcement for frontier models; no harmonised international safety and transparency standards; third-party audits, disclosure, licensing and liability regimes, with oversight shifting "toward pre-deployment"; poorly calibrated frameworks "risk slowing diffusion and reinforcing concentration" (p. 11). There is uncertainty about "the pace, controllability, and concentration of AI development" (p. 12).
- Existential risk: anticipation of "heightened existential risk" could raise real rates (p. 7).
- Misuse and cyber: "misuse" as a cross-border risk (p. 11); cyber security spending (Figure 8, p. 16).
- Geopolitics: the US and China drive capability (p. 25); competition for "technological supremacy" (pp. 10–11); energy and critical raw materials "could further heighten geopolitical tensions" (p. 14).
- Environment and energy: clean power and smart electric grids (p. 20).
- Monetary system: digital money and CBDC-type instruments; pegged digital currencies could weaken transmission in smaller, more open economies and affect central bank independence (p. 17).
- Information ecosystem: "public narratives" shape adoption (p. 4); expectations management (p. 20).
