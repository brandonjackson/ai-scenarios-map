# Copilot Empire (constructive) — extract

## Summary

Concentrated AI providers can coexist with a productive, labour-augmenting pattern of adoption (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”). The constructive version pairs augmentation with adaptable competition rules: interoperability and portability support switching, and prepared triggers address bottlenecks or abuse (“Priority 3”). Worker-centred procurement, innovation and training support complementary uses, while tax changes can reduce the relative cost of hiring and safety rules can protect the legitimacy of deployment (“Priorities 1, 2 and 4”).

## Key details

- **Source:** Titans, Swarms, or Human Renaissance? Technological Revolutions and Policy Lessons for the AI Age, Ramin Toloui, The Digitalist Papers, Vol. 2, 2025-12-11. `sources/toloui-titans-swarms-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full essay, including the typology, historical analogues, AI outlook, and five policy priorities.
- **Scenario set:** Two axes form four quadrants. Winner-take-all versus fast-follower market structure crosses labour replacement versus labour augmentation. The four quadrants are Titan’s Dominion, Copilot Empire, Disruption Swarm and Promethean Fire. The constructive Copilot row covers an expressly mentioned policy-dependent form of Copilot Empire (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Priority 3”).
- **Geography:** The policy agenda is US-centred, with international comparison, economic rivalry and historical examples from other countries (“Applying the Matrix to the Technological Past”; “Complicating Factors”; “Five Major Policy Implications”).
- **Authors' framing:** A typology and adaptable policy agenda for transformative AI, grounded in historical analogues. The quadrants are conceptual possibilities (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Conclusion”).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probabilities. The author finds evidence for all four combinations and says the outcome depends on interaction between the technology and institutions (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

### Timeline

No dated scenario storyline. Evidence used to motivate the axes includes ChatGPT’s late-2022 launch and China’s DeepSeek entering in early 2025 (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** On. The constructive form embodies intervention to retain the productivity gains of concentrated AI while preventing monopolistic abuses (“Priority 3”).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** An advance roadmap of competition guardrails and escalation triggers across cloud/compute, model and application layers defines the constructive variant. The author presents the other priorities as measures that can promote responsible adoption and augmentation, without specifying a fixed implemented package (“Five Major Policy Implications”).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* Prepare adjustable policy levers with different settings for different quadrants. (1) Shift the tax burden from hiring to capital or super-normal rents, revisit a public health insurance option and adjust wage subsidies or transfers if needed (“Priority 1”). (2) Back worker-augmenting research, procurement and training via federal R&D, NAIRR compute and data, SBIR/STTR prototypes, WIOA, apprenticeships and employer–college partnerships, and use adoption measures and voluntary certification (“Priority 2”). (3) Start with interoperable APIs, portable customer data, limits on exclusivity and cloud switching barriers, and expand DOJ/FTC capacity for stronger interventions if bottlenecks emerge (“Priority 3”). (4) Require reporting of advanced training and compute, independent evaluations, material incident reporting, disclosure, meaningful human review and appeals. Consider due-process withdrawal powers and updated tort standards with safe harbours (“Priority 4”). (5) Expand energy, grid and chip supply while protecting households and small businesses from utility cost shifts. Modernize lawful, privacy-protective data sharing, run regulatory sandboxes and extend diffusion assistance to small firms (“Priority 5”).

## Key dynamics explored

- Non-discriminatory, stable APIs and machine-readable data portability make switching viable and limit lock-in while protecting privacy, security, IP and trade secrets (“Priority 3”).
- Restrict exclusivity or terms obstructing use of rival providers, assess egress fees and affiliated self-preferencing, and preserve the ability to intervene as industry structure changes (“Priority 3”).
- Government research funding and measurable procurement performance can encourage in-house worker capabilities. Training partnerships strengthen the skills that make augmentation work (“Priority 2”).
- A reduced hiring wedge makes people cheaper to hire relative to capital. Stronger wage or income supports and narrowly targeted rent taxes can respond to actual displacement and consolidation (“Priority 1”).

## State of technology

High fixed training and infrastructure costs and low marginal provision costs favour large incumbents. Proprietary models, network effects, first-mover advantages and data-driven feedback can entrench them. Continued scale gains with slowing algorithmic efficiency could push training and serving beyond all but a few large firms (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

## State of adoption

*Applies across the set.* Integrating a general-purpose technology requires workflow and process redesign, skills and complementary investments. Institutional inertia and social resistance may slow replacement, though automating may be easier initially than developing effective augmentation. Organizational differences, task requirements and worker readiness mean adoption can vary sharply (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”). Integration into workflows confers an incumbency advantage, but worker and managerial skills and redesign determine realized augmentation (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”). The author recommends tracking output per hour, time to proficiency and customer satisfaction in public procurement to encourage in-house capacity (“Priority 2”).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

The author’s historical analogues show that concentrated ownership of enabling technologies can expand markets and enable new industries even as abuse occurs. AI augmentation studies show productivity gains whose incidence varies by field. The essay gives no growth path for the AI quadrant (“Copilot Empire” historical examples; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

### Labour market dynamics

Augmentation can increase demand for operators, maintenance and integration workers, lower costs to expand demand, and enable products and industries requiring new work. Potential complements include human empathy, personal connection, physical presence, judgement, imagination and leadership. Early evidence favours less experienced workers in call centres, software, writing, law and consulting, and experienced workers in accounting and entrepreneurship (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”). Subsidized training, registered apprenticeships, worker-centred public R&D and lower labour taxes can make complementarity more attractive (“Priorities 1 and 2”).

### Winners and losers

Platforms can keep market power while workers whose skills complement AI gain. The historical communications and computing experience shows aggregate gains can coexist with sharp skill polarization, as high-skill wages rose and lower-skill wages stagnated or fell (“Copilot Empire” historical examples).

### Household incomes and living standards

The historical Copilot examples raised living standards, incomes and opportunity while concentrating gains unevenly across skills. The author stresses heterogeneity in the actual AI task evidence (“Copilot Empire” historical examples; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

### Government finances

*Applies across the set.* Accelerated depreciation and expensing expanded in 2025, and low effective taxes on profits, dividends and capital gains contrast with the income/payroll tax wedge and employers’ health-benefit costs. That mix can favour automation of workers even for small gains. Toloui suggests funding Social Security and Medicare payroll tax cuts with earmarked capital revenue, possibly focusing on super-normal AI rents if concentration dominates (“Priority 1”).

### International economic patterns

*Applies across the set.* US–China competition for AI leadership and global influence pushes towards rapid research or deployment. Misuse, misalignment and economic disruption favour caution. The US, EU and China approach regulation differently. Chip resilience depends on investment at home and on allied partnerships for critical inputs (“Complicating Factors”; “Priority 5”).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Monopolists can use captive customer relationships, exclusivity, switching barriers or bottleneck control to extract rents. Interoperability and portability can lower the barriers to switching (“Copilot Empire” historical examples; “Priority 3”).

### Reliable safety net

*Applies across the set.* Larger supports could be required with strong displacement and unequal gains. Examples are an expanded earned income tax credit, wage insurance and transfers independent of employment. A public option for health insurance could lower nonwage hiring costs (“Priority 1”).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Enforcement requires AI and cloud expertise at DOJ and FTC. Disclosure, meaningful human review and appeal mechanisms should cover consequential government decisions and be encouraged in industry. The author also discusses incident reporting, appropriate civil liability and due-process powers to withdraw harmful systems (“Priorities 3 and 4”).

### A fair share of progress

Competition rules limit bottleneck abuse without assuming concentration disappears. Training and access to compute help diffuse opportunities, while capital or rent taxes can finance a lower payroll-tax burden (“Priorities 1–3”).

## Other dynamics

*Applies across the set.* Transformative AI can amplify misuse, bias, discrimination and misalignment. Incidents can also undermine public support for useful diffusion. Demographic decline and a shrinking labour force in many countries could offset some labour-saving effects (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”; “Complicating Factors”; “Priority 4”).
