# Escaping the Turing Trap — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Policy on.** This is the trap's policy-on twin. It needs tax reform (equal treatment of labour and capital, or favouring wages), public funding or incentives for training, and a change in what technologists and investors aim for. "The solution is not to slow down technology, but rather to eliminate or reverse the excess incentives for automation over augmentation."

## Big assumptions
1. **Direction responds to incentives.** Remove the tax wedge and subsidise training, and firms and researchers choose augmentation.
2. **Augmentation is worth more.** A market with corrected incentives would pick it, because new capabilities beat cheaper old goods.
3. **Jobs resist full automation.** They are bundles of tasks, many of them hard for machines.
4. **Training externalities can be fixed** with public provision or incentives, since firms can't capture the full return on training workers who may leave.

## What must be true for this to occur
- US-style tax codes are reformed against strong capital-side interests.
- Frontier labs stop treating human-like general capability as the goal, or general systems are deployed as tools rather than substitutes.
- Firms make the roughly nine dollars of complementary intangible investment per dollar of machine learning that augmentation needs.

## Tensions and weak points
- Written before large language models. Frontier AI has since pursued exactly the human-like generality Brynjolfsson warned about, so the window for this branch may be narrowing.
- If general AI makes augmentation and automation converge, tax incentives alone may not steer it.
