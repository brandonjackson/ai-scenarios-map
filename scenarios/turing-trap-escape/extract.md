# Escaping the Turing Trap — extract

## Summary

AI tools complement human capabilities, enabling new products, services and tasks that were previously out of reach (pp. 274–275, 278–280). Workers remain necessary to the process of value creation and can bargain for a share of the proceeds and a role in decisions (pp. 275–277). Changes to tax and training policy and the benchmarks that guide technical work help steer development and deployment toward this path (pp. 280–282).

## Key details

- **Source:** The Turing Trap: The Promise & Peril of Human-Like Artificial Intelligence, Erik Brynjolfsson, Dædalus / American Academy of Arts & Sciences, 2022-04-19. `sources/brynjolfsson-turing-trap-2022/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** [The Turing Trap (author preprint)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04200)
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** Two paths distinguished by whether AI primarily substitutes for people in existing tasks (the Turing Trap) or complements them to expand what they can do (escaping the Turing Trap). Both can raise productivity, with different consequences for income and power (pp. 272–276, 281–282).
- **Geography:** General economic argument with US evidence and tax examples (pp. 275–276, 280–281).
- **Authors' framing:** Prescriptive alternative to the Turing Trap (pp. 281–282).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Not stated

### Timeline

The author expects machine intelligence to grow more powerful and pervasive in the coming decade from the 2022 publication. The two paths have no dated scenario milestones (pp. 273–274, 281–282).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** On. Public policy and institutions shift incentives toward complementary technology and human capital (pp. 280–282).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Technology, business practices and institutions favour the use of AI to complement workers. Education and labour markets support the skills needed for complementary jobs (pp. 280–282).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Rebalance capital and labour tax incentives, consider expanding the earned income tax credit, support education and retraining and change technical benchmarks to reward what humans and machines can do together. Build institutions resilient to the growing power of AI (pp. 280–282).

## Key dynamics explored

- Human–AI teams have a wider set of possible tasks, as machines and people have different strengths. New products and modes of production expand possibilities for profitable employment (pp. 274–275, 277–280 and Fig. 1).
- Firms can redesign the production system instead of swapping individual people for machines. Organisational change can yield a different service and expand demand rather than simply reducing its staffing requirement (pp. 279–280).
- When knowledge stays with people and is indispensable, workers retain leverage over income and decisions. The decentralised ownership of useful knowledge can help disperse political power (pp. 275–277).
- Employers underinvest in skills when trained workers can move to competitors, and workers face cash or credit constraints. Education provision or training incentives can help correct this (p. 281).

## State of technology

*Applies across the set.* AI is a general-purpose technology, and human-like performance captures only some dimensions of its capabilities. Machines can perceive, manipulate or process information beyond the level of unaided humans in particular domains; tools can also improve the process of invention (pp. 273–275, 277–278 and Fig. 1).

## State of adoption

Organisations combine technology, complementary human capital and changes in production systems. Amazon’s combination of people and machines illustrates a different shopping service, with wider selection, reviews and continuous access (pp. 279–281).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Augmentation can raise labour productivity and generate value from new products and services. The author argues this ultimately offers more scope for value creation than producing existing products without labour (pp. 274–275, 278–280).

### Labour market dynamics

Complementarity raises the value of worker input. New products create new job categories, and in the example of passenger aviation, productivity increased demand enough that more pilots were employed. Remaking organisations also increases the need for training and retraining (pp. 275, 279–281).

### Winners and losers

Workers who remain indispensable gain bargaining power and can capture a larger share. The complementary approach offers room to spread gains while owners and consumers still benefit from more valuable products (pp. 275–277, 281–282).

### Household incomes and living standards

Rising productivity can lift average incomes and living standards, while continued demand for workers creates a mechanism for sharing gains (pp. 274–275, 281–282).

### Government finances

Rebalance effective taxes on labour and capital. The author argues that the social benefits from more widely shared prosperity may justify favourable treatment for wage income, including a larger earned income tax credit. Public provision of skills or incentives for employer-financed retraining can offset the effects of mobility and credit constraints (pp. 280–281).

### International economic patterns

The trade-policy experience shows that larger aggregate gains do not by themselves secure distribution of gains to affected workers and regions (p. 282).

### Market pricing and scarcity

New services matter in addition to the cost of old products. The retail example adds selection, access and information to consumers’ choices (pp. 278–280).

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Employment, skills development and bargaining power provide ways for more people to improve their economic situation (pp. 275–277, 281–282).

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

An expanded earned income tax credit is offered as a possible way to support wage income, and education and retraining can lower adjustment burdens (p. 281).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Workers who possess indispensable knowledge can demand a voice in decisions. Wider dispersion of useful knowledge helps decentralise economic and political power, and the author envisages institutions that remain robust as AI grows more powerful (pp. 276–277, 282).

### A fair share of progress

More of technology’s gains can accrue to workers when AI makes them more productive and necessary. The author links this to the wider moral imperative to share the gains of automation (pp. 275, 281–282).

## Other dynamics

New engineering benchmarks should reward capabilities beyond what people can do alone. The author connects a stronger record of broad-based gains to the possibility of reversing a political backlash against technology (pp. 277–278, 282).
