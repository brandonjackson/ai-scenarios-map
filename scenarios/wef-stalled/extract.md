# Stalled Progress — extract

## Summary
AI improves incrementally, while costly breakthroughs and a shortage of AI-ready workers make applications brittle. Firms use narrow automation to offset talent shortages, hollowing out routine and early-career work. Gains cluster among a few highly prepared businesses and regions. Weak income growth, eroding protection and high costs deepen economic frustration (pp. 12–13).

## Key details
- **Source:** *Four Futures for Jobs in the New Economy: AI and Talent in 2030*, World Economic Forum, 7 January 2026. `sources/wef-four-futures-jobs-2030/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** Sam Grayling and Kateryna Karunska, two report contributors, summarize the scenario in *Here are four ways AI and talent trends could reshape jobs by 2030* (World Economic Forum, 7 January 2026).
- **Reading notes:** Full 20-page white paper, including the graphical direction-of-change indicators in Table 1. The publisher’s PDF was captured from a public mirror after its PDF host timed out.
- **Scenario set:** Supercharged Progress, The Age of Displacement, Co-Pilot Economy and Stalled Progress. The axes are incremental versus exponential AI advancement and limited versus widespread workforce readiness. This branch combines incremental advancement and limited workforce readiness (Figure 2, p. 7).
- **Geography:** Global, with variation by country, industry and workforce (Table 1, pp. 12–13).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory scenarios built from research, workshops and discussions with chief strategy officers and subject specialists to stress-test business decisions (pp. 3, 7, 14).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probabilities are assigned. Directions in Table 1 are illustrative rather than numerical forecasts (p. 8).

### Timeline
- Mid-2020s — AI valuation correction raises regulatory caution (p. 13).
- By 2030 — adoption remains selective, and routine work is displaced in a bifurcated economy (pp. 12–13).
- 2030 — scenario horizon (p. 7).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Mixed. Guardrails and labour policies expand in response to risk, while weak public finances and trust inhibit implementation and coordination (p. 13).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Governments and firms choose conservative deployments. Governments tighten standards and guardrails, but international alignment is limited and supervision capacity, technology protection, censorship and data privacy rules diverge. Labour policies attempt to address platform and informal work, weak safety nets and cross-border movement amid exhausted budgets (p. 13).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* The recommendations are chiefly for businesses: run controlled pilots, align talent and technology strategy, embed training in work, invest in human–AI collaboration and data governance, build mobility and talent pipelines with education providers and governments, create multigenerational teams, build trust and safeguards, and use partnerships to learn across workflows and sectors (p. 16).

## Key dynamics explored
- Higher compute costs, fragile AI applications and talent shortages weaken realized productivity and shift deployment toward small efficiency gains (p. 13).
- Firms automate routine work to offset labour scarcity, and some displaced workers move into less protected, lower-productivity work (p. 13).
- Weak gains and declining trust tighten the fiscal and investment space needed to provide better support and broader adoption (pp. 13, 15).

## State of technology
Breakthroughs are rare and costly. General-purpose and generative tools serve routine tasks or outsourced functions, while cost and uncertain returns limit specialized models. End-to-end agentic networks stay confined to a few frontier companies (p. 13).

## State of adoption
Legacy processes have been partly digitized without deep redesign. Firms and governments deploy selectively, especially to fill gaps in ageing regions. Uptake and infrastructure remain patchy, and stronger companies capture the gains from earlier investments (p. 13).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
Global growth is patchy, and productivity gains remain concentrated among a few frontier firms able to combine AI with digitized workflows. The global productivity-growth indicator is flat in the illustrative table (Table 1; pp. 12–13).

### Labour market dynamics
Routine work is hollowed out rather than eliminated across most occupations. Early-career and administrative routes narrow. Many workers move into lower-skill, lower-productivity and less-protected service or gig roles, and outdated training constrains redeployment and augmentation (p. 13).

### Winners and losers
High-skilled workers and some skilled trades gain scarcity value and bargaining power on increasingly complex residual tasks. Many other wages are squeezed, and inequality widens across firms, regions and countries (pp. 12–13).

### Household incomes and living standards
Households face higher costs of living, declining wages and weak benefits, lowering confidence. Job insecurity, fewer opportunities and polarization fuel frustration with unmet prosperity expectations (p. 13).

### Government finances
Exhausted fiscal space and low trust stall expanded labour policy and efforts to address eroding safety nets (p. 13).

### International economic patterns
Productivity gains cluster in better-prepared geographies while cross-border AI coordination stays limited; restrictions and talent protectionism threaten mobility (pp. 13, 15).

### Market pricing and scarcity
Compute and capital are more expensive, high-skilled labour is scarce, and households face rising costs of living (p. 13).

### Financial markets and stability
After the AI valuation correction, caution and weak macroeconomic prospects thin margins and inflate capital costs. The table indicates a downward margin and confidence trajectory, with no scenario level stated (Table 1; p. 13).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Declining early-career and administrative opportunities narrow access to work, and weak training leaves few routes to more secure roles (p. 13).

### Affordable essentials
Rising costs of living, eroding wages and weaker benefits lower consumer confidence. Individual essentials are not quantified (p. 13).

### Reliable safety net
Safety nets erode and informal and platform jobs grow. Attempts to extend labour protection stall under fiscal constraints and weaker trust (p. 13).

### Time for what matters
Not addressed.

### A say in your future
Job insecurity and fading prospects heighten polarization and weaken trust. Oversight capacity, censorship and data rules diverge (p. 13).

### A fair share of progress
The gains accrue largely to firms, sectors and geographies with AI expertise and complementary digital investments, widening differences across and within countries (pp. 12–13).

## Other dynamics
Slower progress offers time for standards coordination, localized innovation and lower-risk trials. The report recommends financial and operational buffers, focused skills development, data infrastructure and partnerships to close capability gaps (p. 15).
