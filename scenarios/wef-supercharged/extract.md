# Supercharged Progress — extract

## Summary
Exponential AI advances transform industries, and widespread workforce readiness lets people harness an agentic leap. Many occupations disappear, while some workers shift into oversight and orchestration of machines. Global GDP growth nears double digits, but wage gaps widen and social safety nets and regulatory frameworks struggle to keep up (pp. 8–9).

## Key details
- **Source:** *Four Futures for Jobs in the New Economy: AI and Talent in 2030*, World Economic Forum, 7 January 2026. `sources/wef-four-futures-jobs-2030/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** Sam Grayling and Kateryna Karunska, two report contributors, summarize the scenario in *Here are four ways AI and talent trends could reshape jobs by 2030* (World Economic Forum, 7 January 2026).
- **Reading notes:** Full 20-page white paper, including the graphical direction-of-change indicators in Table 1. The publisher’s PDF was captured from a public mirror after its PDF host timed out.
- **Scenario set:** Supercharged Progress, The Age of Displacement, Co-Pilot Economy and Stalled Progress. The axes are incremental versus exponential AI advancement and limited versus widespread workforce readiness. This branch combines exponential advancement and widespread workforce readiness (Figure 2, p. 7).
- **Geography:** Global, with variation by country, industry and workforce (Table 1, pp. 8–9).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory scenarios built from research, workshops and discussions with chief strategy officers and subject specialists to stress-test business decisions (pp. 3, 7, 14).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probabilities are assigned. Directions in Table 1 are illustrative rather than numerical forecasts (p. 8).

### Timeline
- 2025–2030 — AI capital expenditure exceeds $1.3 trillion, and the productivity return from integration exceeds the early 1.3 percentage-point projection (p. 9).
- 2030 — scenario horizon (p. 7).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Mixed. Early investment in training enables adjustment while social protection and regulatory frameworks lag behind technical progress (p. 9).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Education and training are radically redesigned. Some governments experiment with AI dividends, wage insurance and universal basic income. Others face tightening fiscal space as social strain increases (p. 9).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* The recommendations are chiefly for businesses: run controlled pilots, align talent and technology strategy, embed training in work, invest in human–AI collaboration and data governance, build mobility and talent pipelines with education providers and governments, create multigenerational teams, build trust and safeguards, and use partnerships to learn across workflows and sectors (p. 16).

## Key dynamics explored
- Open-source competition accelerates agents and keeps costs low, enabling broad commercialization (p. 9).
- Rapid worker adaptation and new occupations partly contain displacement; a shift from task execution to AI design and oversight still leaves job quality under pressure (p. 9).
- Rising productivity drives AI deployment and investment, with infrastructure, talent and adjacent technology reinforcing the shift toward agentic operations (p. 9).

## State of technology
Rapid breakthroughs shorten the expected timeline to AGI. Open-source model competition lowers the cost of agents. Quantum advances, automated end-to-end coordination and digital twins support agentic operating layers as central to production as the electricity grid (p. 9).

## State of adoption
Growth in public and private investment goes primarily to compute, data networks, quantum, talent and complementary technology. AI moves into industry workflows at scale and tools make entrepreneurship and creative work accessible to people previously excluded by geography or education (p. 9).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
Global GDP growth approaches double digits. Labour productivity growth and operating margins show the strongest upward arrow. Investors put more into infrastructure and AI-complementary technologies, while longer-run confidence is tempered by further automation and AGI risks (Table 1, pp. 8–9).

### Labour market dynamics
Work becomes modular and AI-mediated. The share of tasks performed by technology rises from 22% in 2025. Job scope shrinks and many occupations disappear. Some people oversee hundreds of digital employees or portfolios of specialised agents; others move into shrinking human-centred niches. Digital readiness and early training curb mass unemployment in some countries and industries, but displacement and job quality degradation remain widespread (p. 9).

### Winners and losers
AI-ready workers’ wage premium nearly doubles from an earlier estimate of 56%. Workers in care, hospitality, public and third sectors face more competition and wage erosion. Many other workers lose bargaining power as wages fall even amid productivity gains (p. 9).

### Household incomes and living standards
Wage outcomes diverge sharply by readiness and occupation. The report also identifies access to creative work, entrepreneurship and AI-tailored services as opportunities for people previously excluded (pp. 9, 14).

### Government finances
Some governments trial income-support mechanisms. Others face tightening fiscal space amid growing social strain (p. 9).

### International economic patterns
Innovation leapfrogs and spreads across sectors and geographies. AI-native networks could reduce geographic barriers to labour, markets and critical value chains, though countries enter with uneven levels of readiness (pp. 9, 14).

### Market pricing and scarcity
Open-source competition helps keep agent costs low. Grid pressure and critical-material price spikes are risks in the absence of a green-transition breakthrough (pp. 9, 14).

### Financial markets and stability
Corporate margins rise strongly. Longer-term investor confidence faces pressure from accelerated automation and looming AGI risks, though the table shows little change in consumer confidence (Table 1, pp. 8–9).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Early investment in training opens some new roles rapidly while market value shifts toward AI-ready skills and bargaining power declines elsewhere (p. 9).

### Affordable essentials
Potential breakthroughs include hyperpersonalized services, education, healthcare and improvements in longevity and wellbeing. Energy and materials constraints remain risks (p. 14).

### Reliable safety net
Existing safety nets struggle with the pace and scale of disruption. AI dividends, wage insurance and basic income are being explored in some jurisdictions (pp. 8–9).

### Time for what matters
Not addressed.

### A say in your future
Ethical and regulatory frameworks lag behind workplace deployment. The report calls for involving workers, governments and industry stakeholders in deployment (pp. 9, 14).

### A fair share of progress
AI widens access to creative and entrepreneurial activity, and breakthroughs spread through energy, health and materials. The report also describes widening inequality and wage polarization (pp. 9, 14).

## Other dynamics
Energy and environmental spillovers, regulatory overconfidence, complexity, rapid obsolescence, concentration and weaker oversight of autonomous systems are significant risks. The Forum advises redesigning business models, improving grid efficiency and data infrastructure, and investing in governance and resilience (p. 14).
