# The Intelligence Curse — extract

## Summary

Highly capable AI first removes layers of corporate work and makes it easier for those with capital to buy exceptional performance (chs. 2–3). As machines replace human effort in more domains, companies and governments lose the economic incentive to invest in ordinary people, resembling states that can fund themselves from resource rents (ch. 4). Production can grow while wages, mobility and broad political leverage decline, with outcomes ranging from distributed material abundance to permanent hierarchy or worse (§§3.5, 4.3).

## Key details

- **Source:** The Intelligence Curse, Luke Drago, Rudolf Laine, intelligence-curse.ai, 2025-04. `sources/intelligence-curse-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text.
- **Scenario set:** Default intelligence-curse path with better and worse distributional variants, and a proposed alternative in which human participation remains valuable (chs. 3–6).
- **Geography:** Global, with examples from industrial and resource-dependent economies (chs. 4–5).
- **Authors’ framing:** Political-economy warning about a default post-AGI path, paired with a proposed positive alternative (chs. 4–6).
- **Authors’ view of likelihood:** The authors see the outcome as the default if labour-replacing AI develops along current incentives. They quote another author’s probability estimates for below-subsistence wages and give no numerical probability of their own (§§2.4, 3.5, 5.4).

### Timeline

First, AI compresses professional job pyramids and displaces white-collar labour. Physical labour persists longer, with subsequent displacement as robotics advances. Once ordinary labour is no longer needed, states and firms adjust the way they raise and spend money (chs. 2–4).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off in the default path. Competition directs investment to AI and scarce complementary inputs. The authors separately propose an intervention path (chs. 4–6).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** No transformative redistribution of capital between or within countries in the default, and firms and states remain under competitive pressure (§§3.5, 4.3, 5.1).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Develop technologies that protect safety and liberty, diffuse AI and human capability, and strengthen democratic control. Upskill people around real bottlenecks, strengthen institutions, prevent autonomous AI from holding assets or senior corporate positions, and prepare public ownership or ways to fund basic needs (§§5.3–5.4, 6.2–6.3).

## Key dynamics explored

- AI performs a greater share of junior and professional work, letting a small group of senior staff manage more output and eliminating traditional entry routes (§§2.2–2.4).
- When capital buys cheap, high-quality artificial labour, money overcomes today’s scarce human talent bottleneck. Those who arrive with capital retain an advantage that newcomers cannot readily overcome through exceptional work (ch. 3).
- Rents from AI, physical bottlenecks and taxes on successful firms replace the economic returns to equipping and employing people. States and firms invest where returns and geopolitical leverage are highest (§4.3).
- Falling household demand does not stop production because owners, firms and states can trade and produce for their own ends (§4.3.1).

## State of technology

AI advances unevenly, making progress first on digital tasks with shorter horizons and checkable results, and later on less tractable tasks and the physical world. AGI and general-purpose robotics are assumed to cover more of the remaining tasks. Compute, power, land, factories and robotics remain meaningful inputs (chs. 2, 4, §6.2).

## State of adoption

Initial gains come from assigning tasks to AI under human supervision. Competition pushes firms to use more AI across roles and to delegate decisions. States also have incentives to adopt it in administration and other activities. Capability differences mean physical occupations can retain a temporary advantage (chs. 2, 4).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

A fully automated economy could produce unprecedented output. The authors expect larger gains once robotics covers physical work and warn that GDP can be devoted to competition or elite projects instead of ordinary consumption (§§2.4, 4.3–4.3.1).

### Labour market dynamics

Broad substitution begins in white-collar roles. Workers may temporarily shift into manual occupations, before vehicles, specialised robots and eventually general robotics reduce that option. Abundant machine labour and scarce complementary capital or land put downward pressure on wages and the labour share (§2.4).

### Winners and losers

Frontier labs can earn rents as an economy-wide layer and integrate into customer sectors. Pre-existing capital holders and owners of scarce inputs gain. Workers, consumer-facing firms and people relying on career mobility lose bargaining power and income (chs. 3–4).

### Household incomes and living standards

Many people may earn little or lose work altogether. A relatively benign outcome could sustain widespread high living standards through shared returns while freezing social mobility. An adverse outcome leaves households dependent on charity or below subsistence (§§3.5, 4.3).

### Government finances

Revenue shifts away from labour and towards taxes on AI-dependent enterprises. Governments have weaker fiscal incentives to maintain public education, services and infrastructure for people who are no longer needed as workers; global competition can discourage UBI that diverts resources from AI investment (§§2.4, 4.3).

### International economic patterns

Competition between states can reward reallocating resources into AI, even when such a shift weakens the domestic social contract. Countries entering AGI with control of capital or technology have an advantage. Economic incentives for immigration can weaken as human labour loses relevance (§§3.5, 4.3, 5.1).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Labour becomes plentiful relative to fixed inputs. Land, energy, compute, manufacturing facilities and robots retain or gain value because the powerful still need them to realise their objectives (§§2.4, 4.3).

### Financial markets and stability

Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Outstanding work and talent lose their ability to overcome inherited wealth when artificial labour can be bought throughout the economy. The authors expect less scope for upward mobility even in a wealthy outcome (§§3.3–3.5).

### Affordable essentials

In a worse baseline, income falls below subsistence and goods needed by people lose investment; institutional reforms could guarantee provision of basic needs (§§4.3, 6.3.2).

### Reliable safety net

Fiscal and competitive pressure could prevent broad UBI. The authors propose public ownership stakes with citizen dividends, safeguards on basic needs, and continued human market participation (§§2.4, 4.3, 6.3.2).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

As states depend less on labour and tax payments, citizens lose material leverage. Proposed responses include competent and responsive courts and legislatures, reforms to campaigning and corruption law, and tools that strengthen democratic participation (§§4.1–4.3, 6.3).

### A fair share of progress

Even a large increase in output may be distributed like natural-resource income in a poorly governed rentier state. The positive alternative includes broad living standards, continuing human economic relevance and dispersed control of AI (§§4.3, 5.4).

## Other dynamics

The authors consider effects on human ambition, status and cultural significance when even exceptional achievements are cheaply reproducible. Their policy programme also addresses concentration of power and catastrophic risks at a high level (chs. 3, 5–6).
