# Supercooperative AI — summary

*"Organizing Intelligence", Alex Imas, Andrew Koh and Shane Legg (Google DeepMind), arXiv:2611.05417, 31 October 2026, with the companion DeepMind Institute essay "The Economics of Intelligence" (29 October 2026). A theoretical model, explicitly not a forecast, that sorts AGI into four regimes by task competence and coordination. This is the regime where AI is only as competent as the average human but coordinates so well that bigger AI firms keep getting more productive per agent. Read from the paper's main sections and Appendix E and from the essay in full; see `extract.md`.*

**The scenario.** A phase transition. Once AI coordination crosses the threshold, "bigger becomes better at every scale, and firm size is only constrained by the size of the market." Deep, standardised markets concentrate into "one giant AI firm in the large demand limit", or "a few large AI-only firms" in the essay, "even if no individual agent is particularly impressive". Nearly identical AI on either side of the threshold gives completely different economies. Niche markets with bounded demand still sustain human and mixed firms.

## Hard work pays
Broken for all. Wages "drop abruptly": once AI firms undercut human employers, "Human wages fall to the outside option, and larger markets make the decline sharper." In the example, specialists earn 0.41 (from 2.90 under minimal AGI) and coordinators earn the outside option of 0.25. Human coordinators are "made redundant by advances in AI coordination". Workers who flee to niche and relational sectors "drive down wages in those markets", so even the sheltered sectors are hit.

## Affordable essentials
Not addressed. Deep-market unit costs tend to zero, but the source does not discuss housing, food, energy or care.

## Reliable safety net
Broken for some, by implication. The model has no taxes or transfers, and human employment in deep markets disappears, while about half the coordinators in the example are unemployed. Nothing in the scenario catches them.

## Responsive government
Not addressed. The model has no government. The authors name regulation, competition among AI providers, capacity constraints and rigid supply contracts as real-world factors that would likely prevent a "firm singularity", but none is part of the scenario.

## Time for what matters
Not addressed.

## Bottom line
The sudden regime: a small gain in coordination, too small to show on any benchmark, can turn a familiar economy into one dominated by a few AI-only firms.
