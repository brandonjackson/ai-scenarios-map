# AGI and the End of Human Employment — extract

## Summary
AGI, operating at near-zero marginal cost and improving through self-learning, can fully replace both cognitive and physical human labour, so firms substitute it for human workers and human wages are driven toward zero (Abstract; §1, p. 1). Using three extended Cobb-Douglas production models, the paper argues that human labour drops out of the production function, all income and economic power flow to the owners of AGI capital, and inequality becomes extreme while social mobility falls (§§2–4, pp. 2–5). Because consumption depends on wages, aggregate demand collapses, producing a "Keynesian crisis" in which firms produce more with AGI but fewer consumers can afford to buy (§2.3, p. 3; §4.2, p. 5). A "human economic power" index shows the economy shifting from a decentralised, human-labour system to a centralised one in which AGI capital owners control all productive output (§5, pp. 5–7). The author calls the transition a "mathematical inevitability" and argues the social contract must be rewritten through UBI, public or cooperative AGI ownership and progressive AGI capital taxation before crisis forces it (§6, p. 7).

## Key details
- **Source:** "Artificial General Intelligence and the End of Human Employment: The Need to Renegotiate the Social Contract", Pascal Stiefenhofer (Department of Economics, Newcastle University), arXiv:2502.07050v1 [econ.GN], dated January 2025 on the paper, posted 10 Feb 2025, CC BY 4.0. `sources/stiefenhofer-agi-end-of-employment-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None. (The paper cites the author's own earlier Cobb-Douglas/general-equilibrium work, refs [2], [10]–[14], p. 8, but none restates this scenario.)
- **Reading notes:** Full text read (pp. 1–8, including references) from `fulltext.txt` (pdftotext -layout). Equations are partly garbled by text extraction (superscripts split across lines, e.g. eqs. 15–21, 27); they were read against the layout and described in words below. Figure 1 ("Decline of Human Economic Power with Increasing AGI Labor", p. 6) is an image and its plotted values are not in the text file; only its caption was read. Refs [2] and [10] are the same entry duplicated (p. 8). One sentence has a missing word: "With the advancement of , the fundamental nature of production is shifting" (§2, p. 2).
- **Scenario set:** Standalone. The paper builds one trajectory through three nested models — Model I (AGI as capital, §2), Model II (AGI as a second labour input, §3), Model III (AGI labour plus AGI capital, §4) — and a decentralised-to-centralised power index (§5). These are successive formalisations of the same future, not alternative scenarios.
- **Geography:** Global / unspecified. No country or region is named. *[Inference: the model is of a single closed economy with no country specified.]*
- **Authors' framing:** Warning and prescription, presented through a theoretical model. "Without proactive policy responses, unchecked AGI-driven automation risks economic stagnation, extreme wealth polarization, and widespread social disruption." (§1, p. 2). The paper frames the outcome as conditional on no intervention ("If left unchecked", §6, p. 7) while calling the underlying transition inevitable: "The mathematical inevitability of this transition underscores an urgent reality: the window for intervention is rapidly closing." (§6, p. 7). It also says "the inevitability of AGI-driven automation is not a catastrophe—it is a challenge of governance, policy, and economic design" (§6, p. 7).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** The author treats AGI-driven automation and the shift to a centralised AGI-dominated structure as inevitable: "The mathematical inevitability of this transition" (§6, p. 7); "the inevitability of AGI-driven automation" (§6, p. 7). The collapse outcomes are stated as what happens without new institutions ("without alternative income distribution models", §3.2, p. 4; "Without proactive intervention", §6, p. 7). No probability given.

### Timeline
No horizon year and no dated events. The paper describes a sequence without dates:
- AGI "marks a paradigm shift" and is described as emerging rapidly (§1, p. 1).
- Initial phase: AGI complements human labour; human wages "initially rise" and human economic power stays relatively high (§3.1, p. 4; §4.1, p. 5; §5.1, p. 7).
- AGI transitions from complement to substitute; firms gradually replace human labour; decline of human economic power accelerates exponentially (§3.1, p. 4; §4.1, p. 5; §5.1, p. 7).
- Long-run equilibrium: human labour fully replaced, human wages → 0, all income to AGI capital owners, aggregate demand collapse, social instability (§2.2, p. 3; §3.2, p. 4; §4.1, p. 5).
- Timing statements: "the window for intervention is rapidly closing" (§6, p. 7); "The time to act is not when systemic collapse is upon us, but now" (§6, p. 7).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Off within the scenario, on as recommendation — the modelled future assumes no new distribution mechanism, and the author argues that UBI, public/cooperative AGI ownership and AGI capital taxation are needed to avoid collapse.
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** None. The collapse outcomes are explicitly conditional on the absence of "alternative income distribution models (e.g., universal basic income or AI taxation)" (§3.2, p. 4) and of "alternative economic mechanisms such as wealth redistribution or ownership structures that allow broader access to AGI-generated profits" (§4.1, p. 5). Firms are assumed to minimise costs in competitive markets with marginal-product wages (§2.2, p. 3; §3.1, p. 3; §4.1, p. 4).
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - Universal Basic Income, "which would distribute AGI-generated wealth to all citizens, ensuring a baseline income regardless of employment status" (§4.2, p. 5; also Abstract; §6, p. 7).
  - Public or cooperative AGI ownership, "where AGI-driven profits are collectively shared rather than concentrated among a small group of capital owners" (§4.2, p. 5; Abstract; "cooperative ownership of AGI", §6, p. 7).
  - Progressive AGI capital taxation, "to tax AGI-driven wealth accumulation and redistribute the gains, mitigating economic inequality and sustaining aggregate demand" (§4.2, p. 5; Abstract; §6, p. 7).
  - "entirely novel economic paradigms" as a further option (§6, p. 7).
  - "AI taxation" is named alongside UBI as an alternative income distribution model (§3.2, p. 4).
  - Overall: renegotiate / rewrite the social contract (Title; Abstract; §1, p. 2: "the existing social contract must be reimagined"; §6, p. 7: "the social contract must be rewritten"), with "a fundamental restructuring of wealth distribution" (§6, p. 7), and act now rather than after crisis (§6, p. 7).
  - Aim stated in §1: "To ensure that AGI's productivity gains benefit society as a whole rather than an elite minority" (§1, p. 2).
  - No design details (levels, rates, funding mechanics, institutions, sequencing) are given. Not addressed.

## Key dynamics explored
- **AGI substitutes for human labour because it is cheaper and better:** AGI operates "at near-zero marginal cost", improves "through self-learning", and can "fully replace both cognitive and physical labor", so it offers firms "an overwhelmingly efficient alternative to human workers" and labour demand "is poised to collapse" (§1, p. 1).
- **AGI as capital (Model I):** AGI is classified as capital, not labour, because it is owned and deployed by firms, depreciates and needs continuous investment, updates and maintenance, raises productivity by optimising decision-making, research and production, and "operates without wages, benefits, or rest" (§2 Definition, p. 2). It enters the production function as an addition to the traditional capital stock (eqs. 2–3, p. 2).
- **Unbounded AGI capital → AGI capital dominates:** "As AGI improves, its effective capital stock grows indefinitely" (K_AGI → ∞, eq. 4); because traditional capital does not scale at the same rate, total capital ≈ K_AGI (§2.2, p. 3).
- **Cost minimisation → full substitution:** "Since AGI does not demand wages, the optimal decision for firms is to substitute human workers entirely", so L → 0 (eq. 5) and output depends only on TFP and AGI capital (eq. 6) (§2.2, p. 3).
- **Marginal-product wages → zero wages:** Wages equal the marginal product of labour (eq. 7); the paper states that since β − 1 < 0, as L → 0, w → 0 (eq. 8), so "in the long-run equilibrium, human labor is redundant" (§2.2, p. 3). *[Inference: as printed, a negative exponent on L in eq. 7 would make the marginal product rise, not fall, as L → 0; the paper's conclusion w → 0 is reported here as stated. See Reading notes / report.]*
- **No wages → income concentration → demand collapse:** "only capital owners benefit from economic output"; "all income is concentrated among AGI capital owners"; with no wages "aggregate demand collapses because consumers lack purchasing power", creating "a Keynesian crisis where firms produce goods but cannot sell them" (§2.3, p. 3).
- **Complement-then-substitute (Model II):** With AGI labour as a second labour input, human wages "initially rise if AGI complements human labor" because AGI labour enters the human wage as a positive multiplier (eq. 12); "as AGI transitions from complementarity to substitutability, firms gradually replace L1 with L2", and in the extreme (L1 → 0) human wages → 0 (eq. 13) (§3.1, pp. 3–4).
- **Full automation as human elasticity → 0:** If AGI "can replace all human tasks (β1 → 0)", the human labour term disappears from production (eq. 14) and "economic power shifts entirely to capital owners who control AGI-driven production" (§3.2, p. 4).
- **Self-reinforcing demand collapse:** Without alternative income distribution, "the economy faces a self-reinforcing collapse in demand, triggering a Keynesian-style economic crisis"; mass unemployment and inequality "drive the economy toward potential collapse" (§3.2, p. 4).
- **AGI capital accelerates substitution (Model III):** Increases in AGI capital "raises the productivity of all labor inputs but accelerates the substitution of human labor"; as K_AGI → ∞, "the relative importance of human labor diminishes, leading to long-run wage stagnation or collapse" (§4.1, p. 5).
- **Loss of economic agency:** "Without an alternative income mechanism, humans lose economic agency" (§4.2, p. 5).
- **Production–consumption paradox:** "firms produce more using AGI, but there are fewer consumers who can afford to buy goods" (§4.2, p. 5; Abstract).
- **Decentralised → centralised economy (§5):** Human economic power, the human share of total labour income (eq. 22), falls from 1 when only humans work to ≈ 0 when AGI does all labour; decline is slow while AGI complements, then accelerates exponentially as AGI substitutes, until human power "rapidly vanishes" (§5.1, pp. 6–7). AGI-driven automation shifts the system "to a centralized structure, where capital owners of AGI control all productive output" (§5.1, p. 7).
- **TFP / allocation channel:** AGI's "ability to dynamically adjust these elasticities in real time could significantly enhance total factor productivity TFP, improving overall efficiency and addressing resource misallocation" (§1, p. 2). This channel is named in the introduction but not developed in the models.
- **Wage labour as the foundation of the social contract:** "AGI threatens to dissolve the economic foundation upon which that contract was built—wage labor. If labor no longer guarantees access to economic participation, then the social contract must be rewritten." (§6, p. 7).

### Model structure (described in words)
- **Eq. 1 (p. 2):** Standard Cobb-Douglas: output equals TFP times capital raised to its elasticity α times human labour raised to its elasticity β.
- **Eqs. 2–3 (p. 2):** New capital = traditional capital plus AGI capital (accumulated investment in AGI-powered automation); output = TFP × (K + K_AGI)^α × L^β.
- **Eqs. 4–6 (p. 3):** K_AGI grows without bound; K + K_AGI ≈ K_AGI; L → 0; output = TFP × K_AGI^α.
- **Eqs. 7–8 (p. 3):** Wage = marginal product of labour = β × A × (K + K_AGI)^α × L^(β−1); stated to go to zero.
- **Eq. 9 (p. 3):** Model II: output = A × K^α × L1^β1 × L2^β2, with L1 human labour and L2 AGI labour ("equivalent productive capacity to human labor", a "direct substitute or complement").
- **Eqs. 10–11 (p. 3):** Human wage w1 and AGI wage w2 equal the respective marginal products.
- **Eqs. 12–13 (pp. 3–4):** Raising L2 with K fixed raises w1 (complement case); with L1 → 0, w1 → 0.
- **Eq. 14 (p. 4):** With β1 → 0, output = A × K^α × L2^β2.
- **Eq. 15 (p. 4):** Model III: output = A × K^α × K_AGI^γ × Lh^β1 × L_AGI^β2 (traditional capital, AGI-driven capital — "self-improving AI systems that function as an owned asset" — human labour, AGI labour).
- **Eqs. 16–19 (pp. 4–5):** Human wage wh and AGI-labour wage w_AGI as marginal products; raising L_AGI or K_AGI with other inputs fixed raises wh in the complement case, but substitution follows.
- **Eqs. 20–21 (p. 5):** wh → 0 on complete replacement; output = A × K^α × K_AGI^γ × L_AGI^β2.
- **Eq. 22 (p. 5):** Human economic power Ph = human labour income ÷ (human labour income + AGI labour income).
- **Eq. 23 (p. 6):** Human wage declines exponentially in the AGI labour fraction: wh = w0 × e^(−λ L_AGI), with w0 the initial wage normalised to 1 and λ a decay constant setting the rate of displacement.
- **Eq. 24 (p. 6):** AGI-labour wage = w∞ × (1 − e^(−λ L_AGI)); text says "AGI labor wages grow in proportion to AGI capital" and that w∞, the asymptotic AGI wage, is "typically approaching zero".
- **Eqs. 25–27 (p. 6):** Total labour normalised to 1 (Lh = 1 − L_AGI), substituted into Ph.
- **Eq. 28 (p. 6):** Decentralised system (L_AGI = 0): Ph = 1.
- **Eq. 29 (p. 6):** Fully centralised system (L_AGI = 1): Ph = e^(−λ) / (e^(−λ) + (1 − e^(−λ))) ≈ 0 for large λ. The text later calls (29) "The index function" (p. 7).

## State of technology
- **Intellectual and physical capability:** AGI "possesses the capability to fully replace both cognitive and physical labor" (§1, p. 1); "capable of performing cognitive and physical tasks at a superhuman level" (§2, p. 2); AGI labour is "machine intelligence capable of cognition and performing economic tasks with equivalent productive capacity to human labor" (§3 Definition, p. 3); in Model III AGI performs "both cognitive and physical tasks" (§4, p. 4). Full automation means AGI "can replace all human tasks" (§3.2, p. 4). Robotics is not discussed separately beyond references to "physical labor" and "physical tasks".
- **Self-improvement:** AGI is "continuously improving through self-learning" (§1, p. 1); AGI capital is "self-improving AI systems" (§4, p. 4); "As AGI improves, its effective capital stock grows indefinitely" (§2.2, p. 3); AGI "scales indefinitely without wages, collective bargaining, or human dependency" (§6, p. 7).
- **Economic characteristics:** "near-zero marginal cost" (Abstract; §1, p. 1; §4.2, p. 5); "operates without wages, benefits, or rest" (§2, p. 2); depreciates and requires "continuous investment, updates, and maintenance" (§2, p. 2). AGI could "dynamically adjust these elasticities in real time", raising TFP (§1, p. 2). The asymptotic AGI-labour wage w∞ is "typically approaching zero" (§5.1, p. 6).
- **How it is provided / market structure:** AGI is an asset "owned and deployed by firms" (§2, p. 2). Ownership of AGI concentrates among "AGI capital owners", "a small group of capital owners" (§4.2, p. 5), "a privileged few" (§6, p. 7). Number of providers, open vs closed models, compute, energy and pricing: Not addressed.

## State of adoption
- Adoption is driven by firm cost minimisation: firms "seek to minimize costs by reducing human labor"; "the optimal decision for firms is to substitute human workers entirely" (§2.2, p. 3).
- Adoption is gradual within the model: "firms gradually replace L1 with L2" (§3.1, p. 4); "firms gradually replace Lh with LAGI" (§4.1, p. 5); at high AGI labour levels "firms prioritize AGI over human workers" (§5.1, p. 7).
- Breadth: human employment threatened "across numerous industries" (§1, p. 1); "the complete displacement of traditional wage-based employment" (§4.1, p. 5).
- Specific sectors, firm sizes, countries, governments, and blockers (regulation, skills, infrastructure, trust): Not addressed.

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
- Firms achieve "unprecedented productivity" (§1, p. 1); "firms produce more using AGI" (§4.2, p. 5); "boundless productive capacity coexists with widespread economic exclusion" (§6, p. 7).
- AGI "enhances productivity by optimizing decision-making, research, and production processes, thereby improving capital efficiency" (§2, p. 2).
- AGI could "significantly enhance total factor productivity TFP, improving overall efficiency and addressing resource misallocation" (§1, p. 2).
- In the long run output depends only on TFP and AGI capital (eq. 6, p. 3) or on TFP, capital and AGI labour (eqs. 14, 21, pp. 4–5).
- Against this, the paper warns of "economic stagnation" without policy (§1, p. 2) and a demand-driven Keynesian crisis (§2.3, p. 3; §3.2, p. 4). No growth rates given.

### Labour market dynamics
- Labour demand "is poised to collapse, triggering a downward spiral in wages" (§1, p. 1); human employment rendered "obsolete across numerous industries" (§1, p. 1).
- Human labour input goes to zero (L → 0, eq. 5, p. 3; L1 → 0, p. 4); human wages go to zero (eqs. 8, 13, 20; "w1 = 0", §3.2, p. 4); "human employment unviable in the long run" (§3.1, p. 4); "human employment obsolete" (§4.1, p. 5); "structural unemployment" and "mass unemployment" (§4.1, p. 5; §3.2, p. 4; §1, p. 1).
- Transitional effect: human wages "initially rise if AGI complements human labor" before substitution sets in (§3.1, p. 4; §4.1, p. 5).
- Labour share: human share of total labour income Ph falls from 1 to ≈ 0, exponentially, as the AGI labour fraction rises (§5, pp. 5–7). In Model I, "all income is concentrated among AGI capital owners" (§2.3, p. 3).
- Human wage decays exponentially with AGI labour fraction at rate λ (eq. 23, p. 6).
- AGI scales "without wages, collective bargaining, or human dependency" (§6, p. 7).
- Specific occupations, groups, ages, entry-level effects, hours, participation and reallocation to new work: Not addressed.

### Winners and losers
- Winners: owners of AGI and capital — "only capital owners benefit from economic output" (§2.3, p. 3); "the owners of AGI and capital accumulate all economic surplus" (§3.2, p. 4); "economic power concentrates entirely in the hands of AGI capital owners" (§4.1, p. 5); "ownership of AGI assets—rather than human labor—becomes the primary determinant of wealth and power" (§1, p. 1).
- Losers: human workers, whose wages and economic power go to zero; "those excluded from economic participation" (§1, p. 1); "the rest are relegated to economic irrelevance" (§6, p. 7).
- Outcome described as "a stark divide between AGI capital owners and those excluded" (§1, p. 1) and a risk of "a technological aristocracy" (§6, p. 7).
- Firms as a group face the paradox of rising output but falling buyers (§2.3, p. 3; §4.2, p. 5).
- Differences across sectors, regions, skill levels or ages: Not addressed.

### Household incomes and living standards
- With wages → 0 and no alternative distribution, "consumers lack purchasing power" (§2.3, p. 3); "consumer purchasing power erodes due to mass unemployment" (§1, p. 1); "The absence of employment-driven income further weakens consumer purchasing power" (§4.1, p. 5).
- "employment-based earnings obsolete" (§3.2, p. 4); "humans lose economic agency" (§4.2, p. 5).
- Recommended UBI would ensure "a baseline income regardless of employment status" (§4.2, p. 5).
- Living standards levels or consumption figures: Not addressed.

### Government finances
- The paper recommends progressive AGI capital taxation and AI taxation to fund redistribution (§3.2, p. 4; §4.2, p. 5; §6, p. 7). It does not discuss tax bases, revenue, spending, debt or social insurance funding in the scenario. Otherwise Not addressed.

### International economic patterns
Not addressed.

### Market pricing and scarcity
- Firms "produce goods but cannot sell them" (§2.3, p. 3); the collapse of purchasing power "threatens to destabilize markets" (§1, p. 1) and ultimately disrupts "market equilibrium" (§4.1, p. 5).
- Prices of goods, essentials, scarcity/abundance and asset prices: Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability
- "This dynamic threatens to destabilize markets" (§1, p. 1); "the policy interventions necessary to prevent systemic collapse" (§1, p. 2); "systemic collapse" (§6, p. 7). These refer to macroeconomic/demand collapse; valuations, credit, leverage and financial-sector risk: Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
- Wage labour ceases to be a route to income: "Should human wages approach zero, traditional mechanisms of wealth distribution and economic participation become unsustainable" (§1, p. 1); "employment-based earnings obsolete" (§3.2, p. 4); "labor is no longer the foundation of individual agency" (§6, p. 7); "If labor no longer guarantees access to economic participation, then the social contract must be rewritten" (§6, p. 7).
- Reduced social mobility (Abstract; §4.2, p. 5; "eroding social mobility", §6, p. 7).

### Affordable essentials
Not addressed. (The paper says consumers cannot afford goods in general — "fewer consumers who can afford to buy goods", §4.2, p. 5 — but does not discuss housing, food, energy or care.)

### Reliable safety net
- In the scenario, "Traditional economic models based on employment income distribution fail" (§2.3, p. 3), and no alternative income mechanism exists (§3.2, p. 4; §4.1, p. 5).
- Recommended: UBI ensuring "a baseline income regardless of employment status" (§4.2, p. 5).

### Time for what matters
Not addressed.

### A say in your future
- Economic power and agency: "human agency in economic decision-making diminishes, ultimately leading to a complete collapse of human economic power" (§5.1, p. 7); "humans lose economic agency" (§4.2, p. 5); economic power shifts to AGI capital owners (§3.2, p. 4; §4.1, p. 5).
- Social instability arises with no employment income (§2.3, p. 3; §3.2, p. 4; §4.1, p. 5).
- The social contract, after Rousseau, is "the foundation of legitimate governance, built upon reciprocal obligations between individuals and the state", which emerged "to balance power, secure individual freedoms, and prevent exploitation"; AGI "threatens to dissolve the economic foundation upon which that contract was built" (§6, p. 7; footnote 1, p. 1).
- Risk of "a technological aristocracy" (§6, p. 7). Voting, democratic institutions or political power: Not addressed beyond these statements.

### A fair share of progress
- Without intervention, AGI productivity gains go to "an elite minority" (§1, p. 2); "the benefits of AI-driven production are monopolized by a privileged few" (§6, p. 7); owners "accumulate all economic surplus" (§3.2, p. 4).
- Recommended: UBI distributing "AGI-generated wealth to all citizens"; public/cooperative ownership so profits are "collectively shared"; progressive AGI capital taxation (§4.2, p. 5); goal that AGI's gains "benefit society as a whole" (§1, p. 2).

## Other dynamics
- **Historical analogy:** "Society has faced such turning points before: the collapse of feudalism, the Industrial Revolution, and the transition to modern capitalism all demanded new frameworks of economic organization to maintain stability and prevent systemic collapse." (§6, p. 7).
- **Philosophical framing:** the challenge "is not merely technological but philosophical and ethical: How do we redefine economic participation in a world where labor is no longer the foundation of individual agency?" (§6, p. 7); human dignity named as at stake (§6, p. 7).
- **Contrast with past technologies:** "Unlike past technological advancements, which primarily enhanced human productivity, AGI possesses the capability to fully replace both cognitive and physical labor" (§1, p. 1).
- Safety and loss of control, geopolitics, culture, health, environment, information ecosystem: Not addressed.
