# The Turing Transformation: Artificial Intelligence, Intelligence Augmentation, and Skill Premiums — extract

## Summary

Low-cost AI automates specialised tasks that once required rare skills, opening the remaining complementary work to a wider pool (Abstract; §2). Firms expand output and hire more workers in the affected sector when the new arrangement makes it economical to employ workers who previously could not do the whole job (§2, Table 1). The resulting gains may narrow wage differences even when specialists whose tasks were automated lose bargaining power (§§2–3).

- **Stance:** Off. Firm adoption depends on profitability in the model (§2).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** No specific government intervention enters the model (§2).
- **Policies the source recommends:** Evaluate AI by whose skills it complements or substitutes for, including whether it removes barriers to using a skill (§§1, 5).

## Key dynamics explored

- Production requires two strongly complementary tasks. Skilled workers have an advantage only on task 1, whereas generic workers can perform task 2. Separate workers incur a coordination cost; the alternative is for specialists to do both tasks (§2).
- Automating task 1 relaxes the constraint imposed by scarce skilled labour. Generic workers can perform the remaining task and become productive in a sector they previously could not enter (§2).
- A small supply of generic workers, a large coordination penalty or a sufficiently costly AI can limit or reverse the employment and wage gains. The same task automation can complement one type of worker and substitute for another (§§2, 5).

## State of technology

AI can perform an existing specialised human task at a unit cost below the model’s normalised benchmark. Language, translation, diagnosis and navigation illustrate how a formerly rare capability can become cheap and widely available (§§2–3).

## State of adoption

Firms adopt when AI plus generic labour produces greater surplus than the prior organisation. The adoption threshold depends on AI cost, the outside wage, coordination costs and whether both worker types were initially employed (§2, Table 1).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

In the main model case with a sufficiently large supply of generic labour, output rises as more people can do task 2. The result is conditional. If all workers are skilled and coordination costs are high or AI is costly, output or total surplus can fall even when firms adopt (§2, Table 1).

### Labour market dynamics

Aggregate sector employment rises in the transformation case as the binding skill bottleneck disappears. Skilled workers lose their distinctive wage premium and workers doing the remaining task face the same wage range. Workers outside the sector have a self-employment option. Employment can be unchanged or fall under alternative assumptions (§2).

### Winners and losers

Navigation opens paid driving to workers without London taxi expertise. Real-time call-centre suggestions improve novice agents’ performance by making successful practices available more broadly. Automated diagnosis could empower nurses and other clinicians while medical specialists face competition. AI-assisted writing helps workers whose jobs had been blocked by written communication and can increase competition for professional writers (§3).

### International economic patterns

Machine translation reduced language frictions for eBay sellers and was associated with a 17.5% increase in exports in the study the source cites (§3, “Language Translation”).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Digital maps made route knowledge free to users with phones. For large-scale written translation where fidelity and speed matter more than style, consuming machine translation becomes much cheaper than contracting for human translation (§3).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

The study’s cases identify barriers to paid opportunities that arise from rare ancillary skills, including writing and navigation. Automating the ancillary skill can make the worker’s existing expertise economically useful (§3).

### A fair share of progress

The authors expect distribution to depend on whose skills become more valuable and whose scarcity rents erode. Earlier computerisation and digital communication sometimes complemented already scarce skills and widened inequality (§§4–5).

## Other dynamics

The paper calls for more granular data on tasks, skills and variation in worker performance to test the mechanism. It presents the model and examples as plausible evidence (§5).
