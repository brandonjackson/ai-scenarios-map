# The Turing Transformation — summary

*Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb, "The Turing Transformation: Artificial Intelligence, Intelligence Augmentation, and Skill Premiums", Harvard Data Science Review (MIT Press), 31 May 2024; an earlier version appeared as NBER w31767. A formal model with illustrative cases, read from the full text; see `extract.md`.*

**The scenario.** Cheap AI automates a specialised task that once needed a rare skill. The rest of the job can then be done by "generic" workers who previously could not do the whole thing, so firms expand output and hire more people in the sector. The skilled workers lose their distinctive premium and everyone doing the remaining task earns within the same wage range. The paper's own line: "One worker's automation is another's augmentation." The result is conditional: a small pool of generic workers, a large coordination cost or expensive AI can limit or reverse the gains.

## Hard work pays
Widened, if the conditions hold. The cases are about barriers: rare ancillary skills such as navigation or writing kept people out of paid work. Digital maps open driving to people without London taxi expertise; real-time suggestions lift novice call-centre agents; automated diagnosis could empower nurses; AI-assisted writing helps those whose jobs were blocked by written communication. The losers are the specialists whose scarcity rents erode, who face more competition and lose bargaining power.

## Affordable essentials
Not addressed. The paper notes route knowledge became free and bulk translation much cheaper, but says nothing about housing, food, energy or care.

## Reliable safety net
Not addressed.

## Responsive government
Not addressed. The authors' only policy point is that AI should be judged by whose skills it complements or substitutes for, not by whether it automates a human task.

## Time for what matters
Not addressed.

## Bottom line
A market mechanism that could narrow the skill premium and open work to more people. The authors stress that distribution depends on whose skills gain value and whose scarcity rents erode, and that earlier computerisation sometimes widened inequality instead.
