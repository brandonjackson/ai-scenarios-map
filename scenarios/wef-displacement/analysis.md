# The Age of Displacement — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Off, or overwhelmed.** Some governments try to regulate deployment, but competition makes it "untenable". Some tax AI, but too late. Policy fails rather than being absent.

## Big assumptions
1. **Exponential capability** with agents and fully autonomous systems scaling fast.
2. **Education fails to adapt**, so workers cannot move into new roles.
3. **Automation is cheaper than reskilling**, so firms automate as a stopgap for scarce talent.
4. **Concentration.** A few state-like firms control models, compute and data.
5. **Global scope.** Wages fall everywhere, at different speeds.

## What must be true for this to occur
- Capability and cheap deployment arrive before training systems change.
- Firms choose automation even where skilled people could be found.
- Robotics advances enough to threaten the care and gig work that displaced people move into.

## Tensions and weak points
- Talent shortage is the trigger: firms automate because they cannot find skilled workers. That is a different story from cost-cutting substitution, and suggests training policy could change the outcome.
- It is a global scenario, so national safety nets and tax bases vary much more than the narrative allows.
- It closely matches `gos-transformation-economy` and `dsit-rapid` in where it sits on the map.

## Scoring notes
Draft scores are 1/2/1/2/2. Work and the safety net are 1 because displacement spans all skill levels and safety nets "stretch beyond capacity". Government is 2 rather than 1 because the source describes declining trust and eroding oversight, not total loss of democratic control.
