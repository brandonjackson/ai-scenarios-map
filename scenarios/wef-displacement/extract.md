# The Age of Displacement — extract

## Summary
AI advances faster than schools and training systems can prepare workers. Firms automate to compensate for shortages, and technical productivity rises as unemployment and poverty grow. A few companies control crucial AI inputs and access, consumer confidence falls, and safety nets and public budgets come under strain (pp. 10–11).

## Key details
- **Source:** *Four Futures for Jobs in the New Economy: AI and Talent in 2030*, World Economic Forum, 7 January 2026. `sources/wef-four-futures-jobs-2030/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** Sam Grayling and Kateryna Karunska, two report contributors, summarize the scenario in *Here are four ways AI and talent trends could reshape jobs by 2030* (World Economic Forum, 7 January 2026).
- **Reading notes:** Full 20-page white paper, including the graphical direction-of-change indicators in Table 1. The publisher’s PDF was captured from a public mirror after its PDF host timed out.
- **Scenario set:** Supercharged Progress, The Age of Displacement, Co-Pilot Economy and Stalled Progress. The axes are incremental versus exponential AI advancement and limited versus widespread workforce readiness. This branch combines exponential advancement and limited workforce readiness (Figure 2, p. 7).
- **Geography:** Global, with variation by country, industry and workforce (Table 1, pp. 10–11).
- **Authors' framing:** Exploratory scenarios built from research, workshops and discussions with chief strategy officers and subject specialists to stress-test business decisions (pp. 3, 7, 14).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probabilities are assigned. Directions in Table 1 are illustrative rather than numerical forecasts (p. 8).

### Timeline
- Late 2020s — rising commitments to AI capital expenditure lower deployment barriers (p. 10).
- By 2030 — labour mobility has nearly dried up and opportunities for human–AI complementarity have diminished (pp. 10–11).
- 2030 — scenario horizon (p. 7).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Mixed. Governments attempt to constrain deployment, adapt welfare and find new tax bases while competitive pressure and fragmented standards constrain those responses (pp. 10–11).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Some governments and sectors attempt to regulate mass deployment; competition makes restrictions economically and politically untenable. Some seek new tax revenue from AI technology and business applications. Harmonization efforts around safety, agent governance and data deadlock (pp. 10–11).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* The recommendations are chiefly for businesses: run controlled pilots, align talent and technology strategy, embed training in work, invest in human–AI collaboration and data governance, build mobility and talent pipelines with education providers and governments, create multigenerational teams, build trust and safeguards, and use partnerships to learn across workflows and sectors (p. 16).

## Key dynamics explored
- Skills shortages make automation a stopgap. Lower deployment costs make automation cheaper than reskilling, which entrenches dependence on AI (p. 10).
- Weak occupational mobility leaves displaced workers without access to complementary jobs, increasing unemployment and compressing wages (pp. 10–11).
- Control of foundational models, compute and proprietary data creates concentrated market and governance power. Dependence on those systems magnifies failures and disruption (pp. 10–11).

## State of technology
Competing models, agents and autonomous systems scale rapidly; robotics takes on tasks that were more resistant at first. Automated decision-making can occur with little human oversight. A handful of companies determine automation costs and access through control of foundation models, compute and datasets (pp. 10–11).

## State of adoption
Public and private actors race to automate. End-to-end automation absorbs more than half of tasks overall and nearly 90% in highly exposed sectors. Value chains organize around AI-native self-optimizing workflows. Some industries shift to local infrastructure and sovereign stacks but lack the talent to operate them effectively at scale (pp. 10–11).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
The productivity upsurge coexists with global disruption, higher volatility and uncertainty. Profit margins rise, concentrated among a handful of firms. No GDP growth rate is projected (Table 1, p. 10).

### Labour market dynamics
Displacement persists across skill levels, entry pathways narrow, and some occupational families disappear. New complementary roles emerge without enough suitably trained applicants. Human-centred work and gig work absorb only some displaced people and become exposed to further automation (pp. 10–11).

### Winners and losers
Companies controlling AI infrastructure gain pricing and governance power. Wages decline globally at different rates. Poverty and inequality reach historic levels, and human-centred workers face pressure as their labour markets become oversupplied (pp. 10–11).

### Household incomes and living standards
Global consumer confidence falls below the cited historical low of 44 as mass displacement and declining wages worsen poverty (pp. 10–11).

### Government finances
Tax bases shrink while unemployment, retraining and basic-services costs rise. Some governments consider taxes on AI technologies and applications (p. 11).

### International economic patterns
National or regional infrastructure localization and geopolitically restricted compute and network access create instability. Countries that fail to adapt welfare see persistent unemployment and stronger polarization (p. 11).

### Market pricing and scarcity
Automation becomes cheaper than mass training, but access and prices depend on powerful providers. Data-centre demand outruns grid capacity as critical talent and energy become scarce (p. 10; p. 15).

### Financial markets and stability
Volatility and macroeconomic uncertainty rise, consumer confidence hits new lows, and firm margin gains concentrate among companies with control of foundational inputs (p. 10).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Entry routes shrink across skill levels, while skills shortages and retraining failures prevent many displaced workers from taking complementary roles (p. 10).

### Affordable essentials
Public provision of basic services faces fiscal pressure and a stretched safety net (p. 11).

### Reliable safety net
Safety nets exceed capacity, while polarization erodes informal support. Countries that fail to adapt welfare to the AI-centric economy face persistent unemployment and polarization (p. 11).

### Time for what matters
Not addressed.

### A say in your future
Trust falls as misinformation and AI-generated content displace traditional media. Governments question democratic oversight when autonomous systems make key decisions (p. 11).

### A fair share of progress
Global wages fall, inequality and poverty reach historic levels, and a small group of firms captures growing margins and control of access (pp. 10–11).

## Other dynamics
Reliance on autonomous decisions creates oversight blind spots and systemic risks. Grid and environmental impacts intensify. The Forum flags AI-native operations, responsible deployment and redesign of work, education and redistribution as opportunities; it advises diversified suppliers, adaptive demand planning, human oversight and engagement with regulators (p. 15).
