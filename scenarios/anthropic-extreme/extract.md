# Extreme — extract

## Summary
AI automates most affected task instances, and the model assumes that it creates no new cognitive tasks performed by humans. In January 2030, GDP is 32.4% above the no-AI path and its annual growth rate is 15.4%. Cognitive employment contracts by 21.5% from mid-2026, cognitive unemployment rises to 17.9%, and capital captures almost all of the additional output (§4.3, Table 3).

## Key details
- **Source:** Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI, Anton Korinek, Charles I. Jones, Szymon Sacher, Tess Cotter and Peter McCrory, The Anthropic Institute Working Paper No. 2026-02, September 2026. `sources/anthropic-economic-scenarios-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** Anthropic’s *Scenarios for our Economic Future*, the companion web explorer. It presents the same scenarios and links Anthropic’s separate June 2026 *Economic Policy Framework*.
- **Reading notes:** Full 57-page working paper, companion web explorer text and linked policy framework. Technical results use the January 2030 model horizon. Dates and results are from the paper when the explorer uses different rounding or prose.
- **Scenario set:** Modest, Substantial and Extreme. The five survey-facing drivers are capability, adoption, automation versus augmentation, productivity per affected task and re-employment friction. Task reinstatement and hiring-posting speed also vary in the working paper (§§2.1–2.3, 3.3–3.5; Tables 1–2).
- **Geography:** United States, calibrated to US occupation mix, labour flows and GDP. The model groups cognitive occupations separately from all other occupations (§§2.1, 3; Table 1).
- **Authors' framing:** Conditional model scenarios that map measurable assumptions about AI and worker adjustment into paths, rather than probability forecasts (§§1, 5).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned. The authors say none of the three scenarios can be ruled out. The paper surveys 10,980 US adults. Among the 3,259 answering all five items, the median implied GDP is 8.6% above the no-AI path and total unemployment is 4.6%, close to Substantial. The middle half of implied GDP gains spans 3% to 19%, and of total unemployment 4% to 6%. The explorer says around one in ten have views consistent with Extreme. These results summarize survey expectations (§§3.5, 4.4, 5; Table 4; explorer, “People’s expectations about future AI capabilities vary”).

### Timeline
- Mid-2026 — common anchor for exposure and business use. The model sets affected task mass at 14% and use at 10% of eligible instances (Table 1).
- After 2027 — most divergence between scenario paths occurs as reach and use depart from the common starting data (§5, Figs. 2–4).
- January 2030 — comparison date for output, labour and capital outcomes (Table 3).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Off. The model isolates technology and adjustment channels and leaves policy responses outside the simulated paths (explorer, “Disclaimer and thanks to reviewers”; §5).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Not addressed.
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* The authors favour preparing for possible disruption and name retraining, income support, universal basic capital and universal basic income as mechanisms worth examining (§5). The linked Anthropic policy framework separately calls for better measurement and delivery capacity across outcomes, phased responses tied to the severity of disruption, and consideration of sequencing rollout if labour-market adjustment cannot keep pace (policy framework, pp. 3–5).

## Key dynamics explored
*Applies across the set*

- AI raises task productivity through augmentation and automation. Automation shifts task payments from workers to capital, while new tasks can reinstate cognitive work. An ideas-production channel increases research inputs, but physical research tasks constrain growth in the ideas stock. The authors regard the modest innovation effect as a likely lower bound because some automation and research feedback is outside this version (§§1, 2.1–2.2, 5).
- Complements between modelled task categories mean that rising cognitive productivity can raise demand for other labour. The explorer illustrates this when quicker design and permissions enable more physical construction work (§2.1; explorer, “Wages”).
- Displaced cognitive workers search outside their old occupations. Search delays create unemployment, and slower real-wage adjustment shifts more of the cost into unemployment (§§2.3, 4.6, 5).

*This scenario*

- In this scenario, 90% of affected instances are automated, with no new cognitive tasks reinstated (§4.3, Table 1).

## State of technology
By January 2030, useful AI capability reaches 50% of all task mass. AI boosts productivity on impacted tasks to about 2.2 times the pre-AI level. The explorer says this combination would likely require recursively self-improving AI and rapid knowledge-work adoption (explorer, “A profound economic transformation”). The model assigns direct effects to cognitive tasks. Physical robotics breakthroughs are outside the explorer’s model (§4.3, Table 1; explorer, “The future is not predetermined”).

## State of adoption
AI is used for 60% of eligible task instances by January 2030; reach times use gives 30% of all task instances affected. The assumed cross-occupation search discount is 0.04, and vacancy posting adjusts at 0.50 per month. Occupation switching depends on skills, willingness to switch and available matches (§§2.3, 4.3; Table 1; explorer, “Job reallocation”).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
The model reports GDP growth of 15.4% over the twelve months to January 2030 versus 2.0% on the no-AI path, and a measured TFP growth rate of 7.3% versus 1.0% without AI (Table 3, Figs. 2–4). The explorer presents $44.4T of output at 2025 price levels (explorer, “GDP growth”). Higher task productivity accounts for much more of the effect by 2030 than accelerated ideas production (Table 3; §2.2).

### Labour market dynamics
The cognitive unemployment rate of 17.9% exceeds any US postwar aggregate rate. The total rate, 11.9%, exceeds the nearly 10% annual peak after the global financial crisis and the roughly 8% rate in the Covid-19 recession. Cognitive wages fall slightly even against mid-2026 in absolute terms, while other labour becomes comparatively scarce and earns more (§4.3, Table 3).

### Winners and losers
The wage bill of cognitive occupations falls 31% relative to no AI with the unemployed counted at zero; the wage bill of all other occupations rises by almost the same amount. The capital income gain of 81.4% accounts for almost all additional GDP, and the net return to capital rises from 6.5% to 8.3% (§4.3, Table 3).

### Household incomes and living standards
GDP is roughly 40% above its mid-2026 level. Sustaining the model’s late growth rate would double per capita incomes about every five years, versus 35 years historically. The aggregate gain is almost three times the cognitive occupations’ loss, but the paper points to past regional displacement to explain why gains do not automatically compensate those displaced (§§4.3, 5).

### Government finances
A transfer in principle could compensate displaced cognitive workers. The authors leave the design of institutions to do that outside the model (§4.3).

### International economic patterns
Not addressed.

### Market pricing and scarcity
Rising demand for capital pushes up its rental price and draws additional capital into production. The model assumes a net return of 6.5% without AI and a baseline supply elasticity of 3. Noncognitive labour becomes relatively scarce as cognitive tasks are automated (§§2.1, 4.5; Tables 1, 3). With capital supply elasticity ranging from 1 to perfectly elastic, 2030 GDP gains range from 21.3% to 43.3%, the net return to capital from 10.3% down to 6.5%, average wages from −9.2% to +30.1% versus no AI, and the labour share from 41.2% to 49.1%. Moving from flexible wages to the most rigid variant shifts the cognitive wage change from −42.2% to +2.8%, cognitive unemployment from 2.6% to 24.0%, and total unemployment from 3.1% to 15.2%. A separate low-productivity, inelastic-capital illustration yields a 7.2% GDP gain, a labour-income loss of 4.3% of no-AI GDP and an implied compensation cost of 84% of the gains (§§4.5–4.6, Tables 5–6 and n. 14).

### Financial markets and stability
Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Cognitive workers face lower wages, fewer jobs and prolonged search. Labour becomes comparatively scarce and wages rise in occupations with little direct exposure (§4.3, Table 3).

### Affordable essentials
Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net
A transfer in principle could compensate displaced cognitive workers. The authors leave the design of institutions to do that outside the model (§4.3).

### Time for what matters
Not addressed.

### A say in your future
Not addressed.

### A fair share of progress
Labour gets 45.2% of output versus 60% without AI. The authors estimate that a transfer of around 9% of GDP, about the size of Social Security and Medicare combined, could restore cognitive workers’ aggregate income to its no-AI level while leaving the rest of the economy over 20% ahead. Transfers of that size in response to technological change have no historical precedent (§4.3, Table 3).

## Other dynamics
The explorer leaves aggregate demand effects, business cycles, financial disruption and catastrophic risks outside the model. The authors caution that worker-level displacement costs and the response of exposed occupations are represented coarsely, and the explorer’s research team notes that AI could accelerate technological progress more than the current implementation assumes (explorer, “Disclaimer and thanks to reviewers”).
