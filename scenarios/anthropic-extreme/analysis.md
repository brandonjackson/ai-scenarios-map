# Extreme — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Policy off.** The model omits policy, and the authors say the question of what institutions could share the gains "is beyond our framework." The scenario quantifies the size of the problem a policy-on version would have to solve: transfers of about 9% of GDP.

## Big assumptions
1. **Very fast capability and adoption.** Half of cognitive tasks are within reach and 60% diffusion by 2030.
2. **Near-total automation of affected work** (90%), with no reinstatement of new human tasks.
3. **Capital responds.** The capital stock grows 56% above its no-AI path, and the return on capital rises.
4. **No robotics.** Physical work is not automated, which is why non-cognitive wages soar.
5. **No demand feedback or financial disruption** in the model.

## What must be true for this to occur
- AI that more than doubles productivity on half of cognitive work arrives and is adopted by most firms within four years.
- Firms replace rather than redeploy workers.
- Investment in compute and capital grows fast enough to use the new capability.

## Tensions and weak points
- Leaving out demand effects makes the outcome look better than other sources expect for the same shock: GO-Science's Transformation Economy has lost spending "almost entirely counteracting" the growth.
- Omitting robotics props up non-cognitive wages. If robotics follows, the 33.6% gain for other workers would not hold (compare `wf-high-substitution`).
- About 10% of the authors' survey respondents hold views in line with this scenario.

## Scoring notes
Draft scores are 2 on every promise. Work and the safety net could be 1 for the cognitive workforce, but non-cognitive workers gain substantially, so the harm is severe but concentrated: "broken for some."
