# Extreme — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Off: it sizes the transfer needed.** The model leaves policy responses out, and the authors leave the design of compensating institutions outside it. What the scenario does is put a number on the problem: a transfer of about 9% of GDP would restore cognitive workers' aggregate income. The authors' suggestions (retraining, income support, universal basic capital, universal basic income) and the policy framework's phased responses and possible sequencing of rollout are recommendations, not part of the scenario.

## Big assumptions
1. **Capability: very fast.** Reach rises from 14% to 50% of task mass by 2030 and productivity on affected tasks more than doubles; the explorer says this likely needs recursively self-improving AI. Effects are confined to cognitive tasks.
2. **Adoption: rapid.** Use rises from 10% to 60% of eligible instances, so 30% of all task instances are affected.
3. **Markets: near-total automation of affected work.** 90% is automated with no new human tasks, capital grows 56.3% above the no-AI path and its return rises from 6.5% to 8.3%.
4. **Politics: not modelled.** No compensation happens inside the scenario.

## What must be true for this to occur
- AI capability accelerates sharply, probably through AI improving AI, within about three years.
- Firms adopt it across most knowledge work and replace rather than redeploy staff.
- Investment in capital and compute grows fast enough to use the new capability.
- Physical work stays out of reach, so non-cognitive workers gain.

## Tensions and weak points
- The catalogue says AI "performs almost half of today's cognitive work by 2030". The extract gives 30% of all task instances affected and 50% of task mass within reach; it does not state a cognitive-work share, so the description should be checked. The old likelihood reason ("near-total automation of cognitive work") overstated it and has been reworded.
- Results swing widely with assumptions: with flexible wages cognitive unemployment is only 2.6% but cognitive wages fall 42%; with rigid wages unemployment reaches 24%. The average wage change ranges from −9.2% to +30.1% depending on how elastic capital is.
- No demand feedback, business cycles or financial disruption are modelled, so the outcome may look better than sources that include them (GO-Science's `gos-transformation-economy` has lost spending largely offsetting growth).
- No robotics: if physical work were automated too, the 33.6% wage gain for other workers would not hold (compare `wf-high-substitution`).
- The fiscal y position (0.1) reflects the authors' point that the economy could afford the transfer, not any modelled public finances.
- About one in ten survey respondents hold views consistent with this scenario.
- It sits just below `korinek-agi` on replacement because it stops in 2030, before full automation. Other neighbours: `cfg-central`, `sit-awareness` and `imf-runaway`.

## Scoring notes
Draft scores are 2/3/2/2/3. Work is 2: cognitive workers lose jobs and pay while other workers gain a third, so the harm is severe but concentrated. The safety net is 2: unemployment passes any post-war peak and the transfer needed has no precedent. Government is 2: fifteen points of national income move from labour to capital. Essentials and time are 3 because the extract does not address them; the earlier 2s rested on inference beyond the source.
