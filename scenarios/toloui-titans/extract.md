# Titan's Dominion — extract

## Summary

Monopoly controls a transformative AI technology that replaces labour on a broad scale (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”). Scale economies and durable first-mover advantages can entrench a few suppliers, while broad AI capability reduces demand for labour at prevailing wages (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”). Toloui uses historical examples to illustrate how alliances between technological and political elites can preserve control and prevent wider sharing of gains (“Titan’s Dominion” historical examples).

## Key details

- **Source:** Titans, Swarms, or Human Renaissance? Technological Revolutions and Policy Lessons for the AI Age, Ramin Toloui, The Digitalist Papers, Vol. 2, 2025-12-11. `sources/toloui-titans-swarms-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full essay, including the typology, historical analogues, AI outlook, and five policy priorities.
- **Scenario set:** Two axes form four quadrants. Winner-take-all versus fast-follower market structure crosses labour replacement versus labour augmentation. The four quadrants are Titan’s Dominion, Copilot Empire, Disruption Swarm and Promethean Fire. The constructive Copilot row covers an expressly mentioned policy-dependent form of Copilot Empire (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Priority 3”).
- **Geography:** The policy agenda is US-centred, with international comparison, economic rivalry and historical examples from other countries (“Applying the Matrix to the Technological Past”; “Complicating Factors”; “Five Major Policy Implications”).
- **Authors' framing:** A typology and adaptable policy agenda for transformative AI, grounded in historical analogues. The quadrants are conceptual possibilities (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Conclusion”).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probabilities. The author finds evidence for all four combinations and says the outcome depends on interaction between the technology and institutions (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

### Timeline

No dated scenario storyline. Evidence used to motivate the axes includes ChatGPT’s late-2022 launch and China’s DeepSeek entering in early 2025 (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. The market quadrant couples control with displacement, while democratic safeguards are presented as a policy response (“Five Major Policy Implications”).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** No specific US intervention is stipulated for the AI quadrant. The historical analogues include deliberately enforced monopoly and restrictions on adaptation (“Titan’s Dominion” historical examples).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* Prepare adjustable policy levers with different settings for different quadrants. (1) Shift the tax burden from hiring to capital or super-normal rents, revisit a public health insurance option and adjust wage subsidies or transfers if needed (“Priority 1”). (2) Back worker-augmenting research, procurement and training via federal R&D, NAIRR compute and data, SBIR/STTR prototypes, WIOA, apprenticeships and employer–college partnerships, and use adoption measures and voluntary certification (“Priority 2”). (3) Start with interoperable APIs, portable customer data, limits on exclusivity and cloud switching barriers, and expand DOJ/FTC capacity for stronger interventions if bottlenecks emerge (“Priority 3”). (4) Require reporting of advanced training and compute, independent evaluations, material incident reporting, disclosure, meaningful human review and appeals. Consider due-process withdrawal powers and updated tort standards with safe harbours (“Priority 4”). (5) Expand energy, grid and chip supply while protecting households and small businesses from utility cost shifts. Modernize lawful, privacy-protective data sharing, run regulatory sandboxes and extend diffusion assistance to small firms (“Priority 5”). The quadrant-specific priority is to protect democratic governance against exploitative alliances between technology owners and government (“Five Major Policy Implications”).

## Key dynamics explored

- Upfront scale costs, closed technologies and feedback from users can entrench technological monopoly (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).
- Broad, rapidly advancing capital substitutes reduce labour demand and weaken gains for workers (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).
- The historical cases illustrate that political institutions can sustain exclusive control and divert gains away from the people using the technology (“Titan’s Dominion” historical examples).

## State of technology

High fixed training and infrastructure costs and low marginal provision costs favour large incumbents. Proprietary models, network effects, first-mover advantages and data-driven feedback can entrench them. Continued scale gains with slowing algorithmic efficiency could push training and serving beyond all but a few large firms (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

## State of adoption

*Applies across the set.* Integrating a general-purpose technology requires workflow and process redesign, skills and complementary investments. Institutional inertia and social resistance may slow replacement, though automating may be easier initially than developing effective augmentation. Organizational differences, task requirements and worker readiness mean adoption can vary sharply (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”). Proprietary ecosystems and installed platforms can lock in adoption (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Not addressed.

### Labour market dynamics

Autonomous systems may have an absolute advantage across enough tasks to reduce labour demand at prevailing wages. Fast advances shorten the adjustment period. Companies and AI suppliers may find replacement the easiest way to monetize investments. Early payroll evidence suggests lower employment for young workers in exposed occupations, especially where AI automates tasks. Examples include entry-level work in software, law and other professional services (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”).

### Winners and losers

The AI quadrant gives strong market power to capital controlling the technology and exposes displaced labour to losses. Medieval milling and British colonial textile policy are analogues for owners or imperial centres appropriating gains while workers and colonial producers face limits on adaptation (“Titan’s Dominion” historical examples).

### Household incomes and living standards

Not addressed.

### Government finances

*Applies across the set.* Accelerated depreciation and expensing expanded in 2025, and low effective taxes on profits, dividends and capital gains contrast with the income/payroll tax wedge and employers’ health-benefit costs. That mix can favour automation of workers even for small gains. Toloui suggests funding Social Security and Medicare payroll tax cuts with earmarked capital revenue, possibly focusing on super-normal AI rents if concentration dominates (“Priority 1”).

### International economic patterns

*Applies across the set.* US–China competition for AI leadership and global influence pushes towards rapid research or deployment. Misuse, misalignment and economic disruption favour caution. The US, EU and China approach regulation differently. Chip resilience depends on investment at home and on allied partnerships for critical inputs (“Complicating Factors”; “Priority 5”).

### Market pricing and scarcity

The author links concentration to pricing power and motivates rules to prevent AI rent extraction. His historical analogue of compulsory milling involved high user fees (“A Typology for Technological Revolutions”; “Titan’s Dominion” historical examples; “Priority 3”).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Not addressed.

### Affordable essentials

Not addressed.

### Reliable safety net

*Applies across the set.* Larger supports could be required with strong displacement and unequal gains. Examples are an expanded earned income tax credit, wage insurance and transfers independent of employment. A public option for health insurance could lower nonwage hiring costs (“Priority 1”).

### Time for what matters

Not addressed.

### A say in your future

Avoid alliances between government and AI corporate interests that could distort democratic control. Toloui draws a parallel with political authorities enforcing monopolies historically (“Titan’s Dominion” historical examples; “Five Major Policy Implications”).

### A fair share of progress

The historical monopolies used political and economic restrictions to impede the spread of productivity gains. Toloui stresses the importance of democratic governance and directs the option of taxing concentrated AI rents at winner-take-all market dynamics (“Titan’s Dominion” historical examples; “Priorities 1 and 4”).

## Other dynamics

*Applies across the set.* Transformative AI can amplify misuse, bias, discrimination and misalignment. Incidents can also undermine public support for useful diffusion. Demographic decline and a shrinking labour force in many countries could offset some labour-saving effects (“Which Quadrant Is Our AI Future?”; “Complicating Factors”; “Priority 4”).
