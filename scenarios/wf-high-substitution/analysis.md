# High Substitution, Far Fewer Good Jobs — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Policy off.** Today's tax and benefit systems stay in place while the economy changes underneath them. The crisis comes from institutions built for a world where "most people work" not adapting: no shift of taxation to capital, AI rents or land, no universal income, no sovereign wealth fund.

## Big assumptions
1. **Capability is broad and includes robotics.** AI covers "most office work" and robots "a growing share of physical work", which needs both general cognitive capability and affordable, reliable robots.
2. **Firms choose substitution.** Where both are possible, firms replace workers rather than augment them. Toloui and the DSIT scenarios explain why: tax codes favour capital, labour is expensive, and replacement is the easiest ROI story.
3. **Displacement outruns job creation.** New industries and tasks (care, in-person services, AI oversight) don't absorb displaced workers fast enough.
4. **The tax base is wage-based and static.** Revenue depends on income and payroll taxes, and governments don't quickly capture the profits flowing to AI owners.
5. **Demand for support rises with unemployment.** Displaced workers can't fall back on savings, family or capital income.

## What must be true for this to occur
- AI progress continues on roughly the paths described by AI 2027, Situational Awareness or Korinek's AGI scenarios, at least for office work.
- Robotics costs fall fast enough to reach physical work within the same window.
- Fiscal reform lags: no capital-based revenue replaces lost income tax before the crisis hits.
- AI profits are booked by a few firms, often abroad, so national treasuries can't easily tax them.

## Tensions and weak points
- If substitution is this broad, output and corporate profits should rise sharply. The crisis is distributional and fiscal, not one of scarcity. That makes it solvable in principle (compare Altman's American Equity Fund and Korinek's capital-tax proposals), which is why it is a *policy-off* scenario.
- Prices: mass automation should push down the cost of many goods, partly offsetting lost income. The scenario assumes the offset is small, or swallowed by land and rents.
- Speed matters: the same endpoint reached over 30 years rather than 10 leaves room for fiscal adaptation.
