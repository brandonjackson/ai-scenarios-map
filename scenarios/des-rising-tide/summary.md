# A Rising Tide Lifts All Boats Proportionally — summary

*Scenario #1 of four in "How Might Fiscal Policy Respond to the Rise of Artificial Intelligence?" by Karen Dynan, Douglas Elmendorf and Louise Sheiner (Brookings Institution, August 2026), written for the Brookings–University of Utah conference on America's fiscal future. The four scenarios are layered thought experiments for US federal fiscal policy over CBO's 30-year horizon, not predictions; the authors assign no probabilities. Read from the paper's full text; see `extract.md`.*

**The scenario.** AI adds half a percentage point a year to productivity (TFP) growth in the US nonfarm business sector for 30 years, lifting it from CBO's 1.1% to 1.6%. GDP ends about 16% higher by 2056. No workers lose their jobs, and the extra income goes to capital owners and workers "in proportion to their incomes without AI". Under current law, federal debt ends 39 points of GDP below CBO's baseline of 175%, offsetting about half of the projected rise. Most of that gain comes from spending that grows more slowly than GDP: existing retirees' benefits are price-indexed, discretionary spending falls from 6.1% to 4.5% of GDP and some lower-income people stop qualifying for benefits. The authors call the improvement "at least partly illusory" if policymakers keep spending and revenue at their historical shares of GDP.

## Hard work pays
Holds and improves modestly. There is no fall in the number of workers, and "all incomes higher": labour income rises in proportion across the distribution. The extract says nothing about entry-level jobs, credentials or the pathway effort offers.

## Affordable essentials
Not addressed. The only related note is that healthcare costs have historically outpaced GDP and that faster healthcare spending growth would raise Medicare Part B premiums.

## Reliable safety net
Holds in absolute terms, shrinks relative to incomes. No job losses test it. Under current law, retirees' benefits fall relative to workers' incomes and lower-income people lose eligibility as their incomes rise. The authors ask whether that is "a continuation of the existing safety net or ... a weakening of the redistribution inherent in our social insurance programs", and whether retirees falling behind would be "an inappropriate breach of the generational social contract".

## Responsive government
A choice left to elected policymakers. Faster growth eases the debt outlook but does not fix it: debt still rises significantly relative to GDP. The central question is "whether faster economic growth should lead to a smaller government relative to GDP or instead support higher levels of public benefits and services". Public support for raising taxes or cutting benefits "is quite limited".

## Time for what matters
Not addressed. The authors set aside the "most benign" case in which workers take their gains as more leisure.

## Bottom line
The best case in the set. Everyone gains a little, nobody loses a job, and the budget improves by default. The risk is quieter: by default the welfare state shrinks relative to everything else.
