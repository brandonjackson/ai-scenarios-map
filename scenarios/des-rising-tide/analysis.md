# A Rising Tide Lifts All Boats Proportionally — assumptions analysis

## Policy on or off?
**Off.** The budget results apply current law and CBO's projection method, with one change: the authors let discretionary spending rise with GDP after ten years. Raising spending and cutting taxes to restore historical budget shares is put forward as a choice, not built into the scenario.

## Big assumptions
1. **Capability.** AI appears only as a half-point increment to annual TFP growth on top of the 0.1 point CBO already includes. Nothing is said about what AI can do.
2. **Adoption.** Not modelled. The increment is assumed to last 30 years. The authors do not explore public resistance damping adoption.
3. **Markets.** The gains are split in proportion to existing incomes, so the labour share and the income distribution stay as they are. Interest rates rise by roughly the TFP increment, as in CBO's analysis.
4. **Politics.** Current law runs unchanged for 30 years, including price indexation of existing retirees' benefits and spending that does not respond to growth, although policymakers have historically kept revenue and many spending categories stable relative to GDP.

## What must be true for this to occur
- AI raises productivity growth by about half a point a year for three decades, about 45% above CBO's baseline rate.
- The gains reach workers across the distribution at the same rate as capital, with no displacement.
- Congress lets bracket creep and relative spending declines run, which history suggests it would not.

## Tensions and weak points
- The 39-point debt gain depends on the welfare state shrinking relative to GDP: 30 of the 39 points come from lower non-interest spending. The authors doubt this counts as "a continuation of current policies", so the stronger finances on the map are partly an artefact of the projection method.
- Proportional sharing is an assumption, not a result. The authors list AI reducing inequality and AI having little effect as alternatives they do not explore.
- Even here debt keeps rising relative to GDP, so "stronger public finances" means stronger than CBO's baseline, not strong.
- Close to `korinek-aug` and `gos-augmented-growth` on the labour map, but it reaches its position through a fiscal accounting exercise rather than a story about tasks. On the fiscal map it sits near `tbi` and `toloui-copilot-constructive`.

## Scoring notes
Scores are 4/–/3/3*/–. Work is 4 because the scenario states no fall in employment and labour income rising across the whole distribution, so effort pays somewhat more for every group; the gain is modest (about 16% after 30 years), so not 5. Safety net is 3: no job losses test it, and benefits keep their real value while shrinking relative to incomes, which the authors flag as a possible weakening but not a break. Government is 3, inferred: the budget improves under current law, leaving elected policymakers a real choice, but debt still rises and public appetite for tax rises or benefit cuts is limited, so institutions are stable rather than improving. Essentials and time are not addressed: the extract has no price path for housing, food, energy or care, and the leisure case is explicitly set aside.
