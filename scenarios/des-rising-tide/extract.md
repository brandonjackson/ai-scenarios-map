# A rising tide lifts all boats proportionally — extract

## Summary
AI is a rising tide that lifts all boats proportionally. It raises average annual total factor productivity (TFP) growth in the US nonfarm business (NFB) sector over the next 30 years by 0.5 percentage point, from the 1.1% CBO projects to 1.6%. The extra national income goes to capital owners and workers in proportion to their incomes without AI (pp. 1–2, 4; Tables 1–2). GDP ends up about 5% higher after a decade, 10% after two and 16% after three. Under current law and CBO's projection method, publicly held federal debt is 39 percentage points of GDP lower after 30 years, offsetting roughly half of the projected rise from 101% to 175% of GDP (pp. 4, 6). Even in this "relatively favorable scenario", policymakers must choose whether to accept the automatic results: retirees' benefits falling relative to workers' incomes, taxes rising relative to incomes, and many programmes shrinking relative to private spending. The alternative is to raise spending and cut taxes to keep closer to historical relationships with GDP (p. 9; Table 2).

## Key details
- **Source:** How Might Fiscal Policy Respond to the Rise of Artificial Intelligence?, Karen Dynan, Douglas Elmendorf and Louise Sheiner, Brookings Institution, August 2026. The paper was written for the Brookings Institution–University of Utah conference on "The Forces Shaping America's Fiscal Future" in June 2026 (cover). `sources/dynan-elmendorf-sheiner-fiscal-ai-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text (pp. 1–39, including references). Figure 1, the chart of debt paths, is image-only in the text extraction. Its values are taken from the prose and tables (p. 7).
- **Scenario set:** Four illustrative scenarios: #1 A rising tide lifts all boats proportionally (this one); #2 Income inequality increases; #3 Jobs are lost and new jobs emerge; #4 Capital wins amid major permanent job displacement. They are built from three drivers: the increase in annual TFP growth (0.5 pp in #1–#3, 1 pp in #4), the reduction in the number of workers (none, none, 2%, 6%) and the distribution of the extra income (proportional to income without AI; proportional to capital and labour but all to the top quintile; the same; all to owners of capital in the top quintile). The drivers are layered: each scenario adds an element to the one before (pp. 1–3; Table 1).
- **Geography:** United States, federal budget only. The authors exclude effects on other countries, international trade and financial flows, and state and local budgets (p. 1).
- **Authors' framing:** Thought experiment and planning scenario for fiscal policy. "The scenarios are not intended to be specific predictions, but rather illustrations of the types of effects that have garnered scholarly and popular attention in recent months" (p. 1). The scenarios are additional to the AI effects already in CBO's projections (p. 1).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned: "We do not attempt to assign probabilities to the different scenarios, which seems to us exceedingly difficult" (p. 1). *Applies across the set:* the four scenarios "include only a subset of the possible effects of AI" (p. 3). The conclusion says "immense uncertainty remains" (p. 32).

### Timeline
- Horizon: 30 years, matching CBO's long-term projections to 2056 (pp. 1, 6).
- 2025 — discretionary spending is 6.1% of GDP (p. 6).
- 2026 — federal debt held by the public is 101% of GDP in CBO's extended baseline (p. 6).
- Coming decade — CBO's projections already include AI adding 0.1 pp to annual NFB TFP growth. CBO has not quantified its AI assumptions for later years (p. 1).
- After 10 years — GDP is about 5% higher than without AI. CBO holds Medicare spending unchanged over the first 10 years of its alternative scenarios (pp. 4–5).
- After 20 years — GDP is about 10% higher (p. 4).
- 2056 (after 30 years) — GDP is about 16% higher. Debt is 39 pp of GDP lower than the baseline's 175%. Discretionary spending falls to 4.5% of GDP, against 4% under CBO's method (pp. 4, 6).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Off. The scenario's budget results assume no significant changes in fiscal policy, under current law and CBO's projection methodology. Responses are discussed as options, not built into the scenario (pp. 1, 4; Table 2).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Current law and CBO's projection methodology, with one change by the authors. Discretionary spending follows CBO's extended-baseline rule, rising with inflation for 10 years and then with GDP after a five-year transition. CBO's scenario-analysis rule would leave it unaffected by growth (pp. 5–6).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *This scenario:* the authors frame a choice rather than a recommendation. Policymakers could take no action, or "raise spending and cut taxes relative to current law and projection methodology to maintain closer historical relations to GDP" (Table 2, p. 4; p. 9). "The policy question is fundamentally whether faster economic growth should lead to a smaller government relative to GDP or instead support higher levels of public benefits and services alongside rising private incomes and spending" (p. 9). They present views on both sides on benefits, healthcare, discretionary programmes and taxes (pp. 9–10). *Applies across the set:* given uncertainty, "policies that are robust to different scenarios would be especially valuable" (p. 1). Waiting until AI's effects are clear lets policy be targeted, but problems can become entrenched first, as with the China shock (p. 32). An alternative is to adopt policies now that help in several scenarios and insure against the most disruptive effects. Examples are a modernised form of Trade Adjustment Assistance (TAA) on a limited scale, or modest public purchases of equities through a sovereign wealth fund, which could be scaled up later. A variant uses automatic triggers: TAA if unemployment persists above a threshold, equity purchases if the capital share rises above a level (p. 32). The costs of such insurance "might not be large" (pp. 32–33). Table 6 catalogues all responses across the set (p. 31). Combinations may suit, citing Marinescu (2025), Sperling (2026) and Korinek and Lockwood (2026) (p. 31).

## Key dynamics explored
*This scenario*

- Faster TFP growth in the NFB sector, about 75% of the economy, directly adds 0.38 pp to annual GDP growth. Extra capital investment and other adjustments lift the GDP level 16% after 30 years (p. 4).
- Faster growth raises revenue relative to GDP because income tax is progressive and its thresholds are indexed to inflation, not GDP, or not indexed. The effect is small: +0.14% of GDP after three decades per CBO (2025) (p. 5).
- Faster growth lowers non-interest spending relative to GDP, a large effect from programme rules and CBO's method (pp. 5–6):
  - Social Security benefits of new retirees are wage-indexed, but benefits of existing retirees are price-indexed. Total spending rises less than GDP (p. 5).
  - Medicare per-beneficiary spending grows with GDP per capita plus "additional cost growth" in the long run. CBO holds it unchanged for the first 10 years, so it rises less than GDP (p. 5).
  - Medicaid and CHIP fall short of GDP growth slightly more than Medicare because faster growth probably lowers poverty and eligibility (p. 5).
  - CBO assumes ACA marketplace subsidies and other mandatory spending (SNAP, veterans' benefits and so on) do not respond to growth. Because faster growth would likely cut means-tested eligibility, this probably slightly overstates spending (pp. 5–6).
  - Discretionary spending falls relative to GDP, less sharply under the authors' assumption than CBO's (p. 6).
- Existing debt shrinks relative to a larger GDP (p. 6).
- Faster growth raises interest rates on new and rolled-over Treasury debt, partly offsetting the other effects. CBO expects rates to rise by roughly the TFP increment. Foreign capital inflows could hold rates down, while ongoing AI investment in computing capacity and energy could push them up (pp. 6, 8).
- Accepting these automatic effects means retirees' incomes fall relative to workers', bracket creep raises tax burdens, and public services shrink relative to private spending. The authors say this raises the question of whether the result counts as "a continuation of current policies" (pp. 7–8).
- Because policymakers have historically kept revenue and several spending categories stable relative to GDP, the budget improvement "may prove at least partly illusory" (p. 8).

*Applies across the set*

- The effect on optimal national saving (Ramsey-style social planner) and on private saving is ambiguous. Households expecting faster income growth, or holding stocks that rise in anticipation, may consume more, while higher rates may encourage saving. Business saving could go either way. Ramsey analysis therefore "does not offer ready guidance" on whether AI justifies larger or smaller deficits, and this holds in all four scenarios (pp. 8–9).
- The pace of labour-market disruption matters: sharp drops in demand for skills mean more layoffs, while gradual drops are absorbed by retirements and new entrants. Faster change would increase public pressure for policy responses (p. 3).

## State of technology
AI is represented only as an increment to NFB TFP growth: 0.5 pp per year beyond the 0.1 pp already in CBO's projections for the coming decade (pp. 1, 4). An AI boom "might spur ongoing investment in computer capacity and energy production" (p. 8). AI capabilities, providers, market structure and pricing are not addressed.

## State of adoption
Not addressed for this scenario. *Applies across the set:* the authors do not explore an outcome where AI's spread is dampened by public resistance. Pew (2025) found 50% of Americans more concerned than excited about AI and 10% the opposite (p. 3).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
NFB TFP growth averages 1.6% a year over 30 years instead of CBO's 1.1%. The direct effect on annual GDP growth is +0.38 pp. GDP is about 5% higher after a decade, 10% after two and 16% after three (pp. 1, 4; Table 2).

### Labour market dynamics
No reduction in the number of workers (Table 1, p. 3). Labour income rises proportionally across the distribution (p. 4).

### Winners and losers
"All incomes higher": capital and labour incomes across the distribution rise proportionally (p. 4; Table 2). Under current law, retirees' federal benefits fall relative to workers' incomes, because existing beneficiaries' Social Security is price-indexed (pp. 5, 7–9). Rising equity prices from AI benefit many retirees (p. 9).

### Household incomes and living standards
All incomes are higher than without AI (Table 2). Taxes rise relative to incomes as people move into higher brackets (pp. 8–9). Some lower-income people no longer qualify for some benefits (p. 7). The authors note that median inflation-adjusted personal income is more than 50% higher than half a century ago, yet "rising incomes do not generally resolve fiscal challenges" (p. 10).

### Government finances
- Method: the authors apply CBO's (2025) scenario-analysis changes in GDP, interest rates and spending and revenue shares to CBO's (2026d) extended baseline. Discretionary spending is treated differently (p. 5).
- Debt held by the public is 39 pp of GDP lower than the extended baseline after 30 years. The baseline rises from 101% of GDP in 2026 to 175%, so the scenario offsets roughly half of that rise (p. 6; Table 2; Figure 1, p. 7).
- Decomposition of the 39 pp: lower non-interest spending relative to GDP, 30 pp; higher revenue relative to GDP, 2 pp; higher GDP and interest rates acting on existing debt, 7 pp. The spending contribution uses the higher interest rates; at baseline rates it would be "a bit higher" (p. 7).
- Revenue rises 0.14% of GDP after three decades for +0.5 pp TFP growth, per CBO (2025) (p. 5).
- Discretionary spending falls from 6.1% of GDP in 2025 to 4.5% in 2056 under the authors' approach, against 4% under CBO's scenario approach (p. 6). The authors find indefinite decline relative to GDP not "a compelling depiction of current policies". Defense and non-defense discretionary outlays have shown no trend relative to GDP in recent decades (p. 6).
- History: revenue has shown no trend relative to GDP for six decades, defense spending none for three, and non-defense discretionary and other benefit programmes (excluding Social Security and major health programmes) none for six (p. 8).
- If interest rates did not change, debt would be lower still, by an amount building to 12% of GDP after three decades (p. 8).
- Healthcare: faster healthcare cost growth would raise Medicare Part B premiums. Past rises in insurance costs have tended to bring larger public subsidies (Follette and Sheiner 2005) (p. 9).
- *Applies across the set:* even taking the projected improvement at face value, "federal debt would continue to rise significantly relative to GDP in the scenarios we analyzed" (p. 30). State and local governments' balanced-budget rules prevent large sustained changes in their saving (p. 9, n. 4).

### International economic patterns
Faster AI-driven growth might attract foreign capital and hold down interest rates (p. 8). Otherwise not addressed. The authors exclude international effects (p. 1).

### Market pricing and scarcity
AI spurs equity-price increases that are good for many retirees (p. 9). Stock values may rise immediately in anticipation of AI-driven growth (p. 8). Healthcare costs have historically risen faster than GDP (p. 9). In many cases the cost of providing public services rises as wages elsewhere rise (p. 10).

### Financial markets and stability
Higher interest rates on new Treasury securities (pp. 6, 8). Otherwise not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Not addressed.

### Affordable essentials
Healthcare costs have historically outpaced GDP and generated pressure for larger public subsidies. Faster healthcare spending growth would raise Medicare Part B premiums (p. 9).

### Reliable safety net
The authors ask whether lower-income people losing eligibility for benefits as incomes rise would look like "a continuation of the existing safety net or as a weakening of the redistribution inherent in our social insurance programs". They also ask whether retirees' incomes falling relative to workers' would be "an inappropriate breach of the generational social contract" (pp. 7–8). Some would accept relative declines on the view that programmes should give a minimum level of support. Others treat need as relative and would bolster benefits (p. 9).

### Time for what matters
*Applies across the set:* the authors do not analyse "the most benign and optimistic alternative", in which workers use higher earnings to choose more leisure and labour input falls appreciably. In that case there would probably be little demand for fiscal changes (p. 4).

### A say in your future
Public support for raising taxes or cutting benefits "is quite limited" (p. 10).

### A fair share of progress
Gains are shared proportionally (p. 4). The open question is whether public benefits and services should keep pace with rising private incomes (p. 9). *Applies across the set:* the authors expect "considerable pressure to ensure that gains from AI would be shared broadly across society, in terms of both incomes and opportunities for meaningful work" (pp. 30–31).

## Other dynamics
The paper deliberately excludes AI's effects on the environment, social interactions, national security, AI regulation, other countries, and state and local and foreign fiscal policy (p. 1). *Applies across the set:* alternatives not explored are (a) small effects from AI, because public resistance dampens its spread or because AI offsets weaker productivity from other sources (Jones 2026 shows output per person growing at a fairly stable rate for 150 years); (b) AI raising productivity more for workers outside the top quintile and reducing inequality; and (c) the leisure case (pp. 3–4). Major economic transformations have historically brought fiscal innovations, and AI "may become one of the central questions of our time" (p. 33).
