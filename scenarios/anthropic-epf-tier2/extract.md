# Tier 2: Recession-level disruption (~10% unemployment) — extract

## Summary
At about 10% unemployment, “disruption is a fact of life for tens of millions of American families” (p. 10). Professions no longer need the expertise they once demanded, careers end earlier and begin later, and job searches “stretch from months into years”. Families revise or abandon plans for homes, education and retirement, and communities see their employment base erode faster than new work replaces it (p. 10). Familiar pathways “from effort to advancement” become less reliable, and the question “shifts from whether to intervene to how fast existing systems can scale” (p. 10). The tier’s aim is to “Expand temporary income support and retraining”, using “the safety net as a launchpad”. Its priority is expanded unemployment insurance with automatic triggers, supplemented by sector-specific transition support and basic-needs relief. At this level the authors would also support uniform, firm-level regulations and incentives to manage the pace of displacement (pp. 4, 10–11).

## Key details
- **Source:** Anthropic’s Economic Policy Framework (PDF title page; running footer “A policy framework for AI’s impact on work”), Anthropic, Anthropic, June 2026. `sources/anthropic-economic-policy-framework-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** Anthropic’s web summary page, “Policy on the AI Exponential” / “Anthropic’s Economic Policy Framework” (`private/web-summary.txt`), restates the tiers and announces linked funding commitments. This is a separate publication from The Anthropic Institute’s “Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI” (Korinek, Jones, Sacher, Cotter and McCrory, Working Paper No. 2026-02, September 2026), which is already on the map as `anthropic-modest`, `anthropic-substantial` and `anthropic-extreme`. The two use different scenario sets: the Institute paper uses modelled capability and adoption paths, while this framework uses policy tiers set by unemployment level. *[Inference: the 5% / 10% / unprecedented tiers do not map one-to-one onto Modest / Substantial / Extreme, and neither source says they do.]*
- **Reading notes:** Full text of the 14-page PDF (pp. 1–14) and the web summary page. Page locators use the PDF’s printed page numbers; p. 1 is the cover page. The text extraction contains tables (pp. 9–11), which were read in full. Page 7 says measures that could entrench the link between income and employment are flagged “below”, but no explicit flags appear in the extracted text. *[Inference: any flag may have been a visual marker lost in extraction, or it may be the convergence-to-a-common-floor discussion on p. 12.]*
- **Scenario set:** Three tiers, set by “the severity of AI-driven labor market disruption, using the unemployment rate as the primary trigger” (p. 6): Tier 1, Baseline economy (~5% unemployment with churn); Tier 2, Recession-level disruption (~10% unemployment); Tier 3, Transformative disruption (unemployment exceeding historical peaks) (pp. 7, 10, 12). A second dimension runs across the tiers without defining separate scenarios: whether disruption is “a temporary shock, after which the labor market re-stabilizes” or “an enduring restructuring, in which the demand for human labor is significantly and persistently lower” (p. 7). Each tier “buys time for workers and the economy to adapt, and eventually judge whether the interventions in the next tier are needed” (p. 4).
- **Geography:** United States. The authors say “the principles underpinning this framework are intended to be global”, and the right policy mix in any country “will turn on its labor law, social contract, and where models are developed” (p. 4).
- **Authors' framing:** Prescription and planning scenario. Tiers are contingencies for calibrating policy, not forecasts: “We cannot know today which scenario will unfold, but we believe this uncertainty warrants preparation… our policy recommendations anchor on three possible scenarios” (p. 6). Tier 2 policies are among “the responses worth having ready if conditions worsen” (p. 3).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned to any tier. “We can’t forecast with confidence where AI capabilities will be even a few years from now” (p. 3). The web summary says that if AI delivers “even a fraction of its potential… it’s likely that AI will have acted as a general substitute for labor” (web summary).

### Timeline
No horizon year is given.
- 1970 — Extended Benefits UI programme established. Its triggers “can fire late and unevenly”, and Congress has repeatedly legislated emergency extensions on top of it (p. 11).
- Great Recession and COVID pandemic — extended benefits “did little to discourage people from looking for work, while helping families keep up with essential spending” (p. 11). Legacy UI systems buckled under pandemic claim volumes (p. 6).
- Recent years — entry-level workers in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen weaker employment growth (p. 1).
- June 2026 — framework published. Recommendations are calibrated to “AI capabilities that exist today or will arrive within roughly the next year” (p. 3).
- Within the tier — job searches “stretch from months into years” (p. 10).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** On. The tier is defined as a trigger for expanded government income support, retraining and possible management of rollout pace.
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The tiers set out what governments should do, not what they do inside a story. Existing policies the text treats as current: state-run UI on legacy systems (pp. 5–6), the Extended Benefits programme and its triggers (p. 11), existing safety-net programmes that “only partially fill the gap” after UI exhaustion (p. 11), and federal capital accounts seeded at birth (p. 8).
- **Policies the source recommends:**
  - *This tier:*
    - **Expand unemployment insurance** (p. 11). UI is called “the most direct, tested, and rapidly scalable mechanism for supporting displaced workers”. The authors propose reforming the Extended Benefits trigger “so that extension is automatic and uniform across states”, and extending UI duration during periods of elevated unemployment. The web summary calls expanded UI “our priority” in this scenario.
    - **Sector-specific transition support** (p. 11). Targeted assistance for workers in industries facing acute AI-driven displacement: extended income support, subsidised retraining, job-search allowances and relocation assistance. Intensive reemployment services produce “modest positive effects on earnings”.
    - **Basic needs relief** (p. 11). Monthly payments to displaced workers who have exhausted UI, or to labour-market entrants unable to find work after documented search, “ensuring continued access to food, housing, and healthcare”. Benefits are enhanced for those who opt into roles addressing shortages in healthcare, education, child and elder care, infrastructure, ecosystem restoration and public safety. This could be a new instrument or an expansion of existing programmes, structured “to reach a broader population”.
    - **Manage the pace of displacement** (p. 4). “If disruption happens beyond the economy’s ability to adapt, at the levels described in Tier 2, we would support government regulations and incentives at the firm level that manage the pace of displacement.” Uniform application is essential. The web summary says policymakers should consider “the pace of its rollout, including by incentivizing firms to manage displacement gradually”.
    - **Invest in growth areas** (p. 10). Using the safety net as a launchpad “will require investing, in parallel, in the opportunities and industries where meaningful work will be”.
  - *Carried from Tier 1 (the source does not say they continue in Tier 2; listed for completeness):* universal pre-distributive capital accounts, wage insurance, occupational licensing reform, retention tax incentives, workforce training grants and job-matching infrastructure (pp. 8–10). *[Inference: the escalating design (“Each tier buys time… judge whether the interventions in the next tier are needed”, p. 4) implies Tier 1 measures stay in place unless phased out.]* Capital accounts “must be paired with the income support and transition mechanisms in Tiers 2 and 3” (p. 8).
  - *Applies across the set (foundational measures, “valuable no matter what happens”, web summary; pp. 5–6):*
    - **Measurement.** Expanded investment in statistical agencies; AI-specific survey instruments; and reporting requirements for AI labs and firms, “including Anthropic”, on deployment patterns and workforce effects. Directly observable metrics include AI usage rates, intensity of use, and worker productivity by level of AI adoption (p. 5).
    - **A government unit focused on tracking AI’s effects**, modelled on the Council of Economic Advisers, to track AI sector by sector, stress-test institutions and flag early signals (p. 5).
    - **Delivery infrastructure.** Modernised UI that processes claims faster, flexes quickly when benefits change, and runs real-time identity and cross-state fraud matching. Investment in rails to expand coverage quickly, revenue collection, and digital public infrastructure (pp. 5–6).
    - **Broader signals.** Watch labour force participation, underemployment, wages and labour share alongside unemployment (p. 6).
    - **Phase-out.** Some Tier 1 and Tier 2 tools would be phased out if conditions move toward Tier 3, since some near-term measures “could make matters worse in an enduring restructuring by entrenching the link between income and employment” (p. 7).
    - **Firm conduct.** Firms should build training into deployment, avoid “defaulting to headcount reductions”, retrain and redeploy, and redesign early-career roles (p. 3). “Voluntary action isn’t a substitute for a government response that holds companies to the same standard” (web summary).

## Key dynamics explored
*This tier*
- Displacement at recession scale lengthens job searches into years and erodes community employment bases faster than new work can replace them. Families then revise or abandon major life plans (p. 10).
- Late, uneven Extended Benefits triggers force repeated emergency legislation, so automatic, uniform triggers would let support scale faster (p. 11).
- Extended UI sustains essential spending without much reducing job search, based on Great Recession and COVID evidence (p. 11).
- Consumption “drops sharply” when workers exhaust UI, and existing programmes only partly fill the gap. Basic needs relief is meant to cover that cliff (p. 11).
- Higher benefits for taking shortage-sector roles (care, health, education, infrastructure, ecosystem restoration, public safety) steer displaced workers toward unmet needs (p. 11).
- When disruption outruns the economy’s ability to adapt and redistributive tools are imperfect, theory suggests steering or sequencing rollout. Unilateral slowing fails because “any firm that slows down alone simply cedes the market to those that do not” (p. 4).

*Applies across the set*
- Interventions serve stability, adaptability or both. UI does both: it “stabilizes demand while giving displaced workers the time and tools to find a well-matched job”. As disruption increases, the balance shifts toward income support and redistribution (p. 7).
- A temporary shock calls for temporary supports. An enduring restructuring calls for permanent responses (p. 7).
- If AI becomes a general substitute, retraining, matching and mobility policy “may fail to deliver a stable, full-employment equilibrium” (p. 4).
- Firms can use AI to cut headcount or to expand output with the same people (p. 3).

## State of technology
*This tier:* Professions “no longer need the expertise they once demanded” (p. 10). The pace-management proposal is conditioned on AI becoming “a general substitute for human labor across most of the economy” (p. 4; web summary).

*Applies across the set:* Recommendations are calibrated to capabilities “that exist today or will arrive within roughly the next year” (p. 3). If capabilities keep advancing, AI “may become a general substitute for human labor” and could generate growth “on a scale the modern world hasn’t seen” (p. 1). Market structure, providers, open versus closed models, compute, energy and pricing are not addressed.

## State of adoption
*This tier:* Not addressed directly. Proposed firm-level regulation and incentives would manage the pace of displacement uniformly across firms (p. 4).

*Applies across the set:* “We’re still early in AI’s adoption” (p. 1). Claude is used more in the US than in any other country (p. 4). Pace and diffusion are uncertain (web summary). Firms choose how to deploy, and Anthropic offers customers training and enablement (pp. 3–4).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
*This tier:* Not addressed.
*Applies across the set:* AI could generate growth “on a scale the modern world hasn’t seen”. The central challenge is sharing the gains (p. 1). Economists’ models range from modest growth to output doubling while wages collapse (p. 1).

### Labour market dynamics
*This tier:* Unemployment is about 10%, at recession level (p. 10). Expertise is devalued. Careers “end earlier than planned and begin later than expected”, and job searches stretch from months into years (p. 10). Labour-market entrants may be unable to find work after documented search, and some workers exhaust UI (p. 11). Industries face “acute AI-driven displacement” (p. 11). Shortages persist in healthcare, education, child and elder care, infrastructure, ecosystem restoration and public safety (p. 11).
*Applies across the set:* Entry-level workers in exposed occupations have already seen weaker employment growth (p. 1). Pay, security and job quality may erode even for those who stay employed (p. 6).

### Winners and losers
*This tier:* Losers named: “tens of millions of American families” affected by disruption; workers in industries facing acute displacement; older workers whose careers end early; young people whose careers begin late; and communities “that depended on a stable base of steady employment” (pp. 10–11).
*Applies across the set:* Gains could concentrate narrowly or be broadly shared depending on near-term choices (web summary).

### Household incomes and living standards
*This tier:* Families revise or abandon plans “for homes, for children’s education, for retirements” (p. 10). Consumption drops sharply on UI exhaustion (p. 11). Basic needs relief aims to secure food, housing and healthcare (p. 11).
*Applies across the set:* The goal is “a society where people can live well” (web summary).

### Government finances
*This tier:* Spending rises through extended UI duration, sector transition support and new monthly basic-needs payments (p. 11). Congress has repeatedly had to legislate emergency extensions (p. 11). Funding sources for Tier 2 are not specified.
*Applies across the set:* Investment in statistical agencies and UI systems. Legacy UI paid out billions in improper payments during the pandemic (pp. 5–6).

### International economic patterns
*This tier:* Not addressed.
*Applies across the set:* Only 52% of the world’s population is covered by any social protection scheme, and many countries’ systems cannot absorb a fast shock (p. 6). The principles are intended to be global, with discussion sought at the G7 and the Geneva AI Summit (p. 4; web summary).

### Market pricing and scarcity
*This tier:* Labour shortages persist in care, health, education, infrastructure, ecosystem restoration and public safety (p. 11). Prices are not addressed.
*Applies across the set:* Not addressed.

### Financial markets and stability
Not addressed.

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
*This tier:* “Familiar pathways—from education to career, from effort to advancement, from work to stability—have become less reliable, and for many, impassable” (p. 10).
*Applies across the set:* “There is dignity in work… society should keep searching for ways to remain at full employment” (p. 2).

### Affordable essentials
*This tier:* Basic needs relief is designed to ensure “continued access to food, housing, and healthcare” for those who have exhausted UI or cannot find work (p. 11). Families revise plans for homes (p. 10).

### Reliable safety net
*This tier:* The question becomes “how fast existing systems can scale” (p. 10). Extended Benefits triggers fire “late and unevenly”, and reform would make extension automatic (p. 11). Existing programmes “only partially fill the gap” after UI exhaustion (p. 11). The safety net is meant to be “a launchpad to help workers pursue new vocations” (p. 10).
*Applies across the set:* UI “is not sufficiently prepared for a large labor market shock” (pp. 5–6).

### Time for what matters
*This tier:* Families revise or abandon plans for children’s education and retirements (p. 10).
*Applies across the set:* “People are more than their jobs”. Over a longer horizon, people may work far less (p. 2).

### A say in your future
*This tier:* Not addressed.
*Applies across the set:* Anthropic says it will engage workers, unions, small business owners and worker organisations directly (web summary).

### A fair share of progress
*This tier:* Not addressed.
*Applies across the set:* The central challenge is “making sure the gains are widely shared” (p. 1).

## Other dynamics
*Applies across the set:*
- The framework is published alongside Anthropic’s Advanced AI Framework (web summary).
- Anthropic announces $350 million in linked investments: a $200 million Economic Futures Research Fund and a $150 million national fellowship programme (web summary).
- Anthropic contributes data through the Anthropic Economic Index (p. 5).
- Values: “We are not seeking job displacement. We are working to prevent or minimize it” (p. 2).
- The framework is “necessarily incomplete”, and AI’s pace may make proposals inadequate before they can be implemented (web summary).
- Safety, geopolitics, culture, health, environment and the information ecosystem are not addressed in this document.
