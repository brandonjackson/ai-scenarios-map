# Something Big Is Happening — extract

## Summary

Rapid model releases make AI capable of completing increasingly long and complex digital projects with little supervision, as the author reports from his own software work (“I know this is real”). AI begins contributing to AI development, which he expects to speed up further improvements (“AI is now building the next AI”). The effects spread from coding to other screen-based occupations, with capability arriving before the full employment effects (“What this means for your job”).

- **Other sources for this scenario:** None

- **Authors’ view of likelihood:** Shumer is highly confident about rapid disruption in the next two to five years. He is less certain about when machines can match deep empathy or relationship-based trust (“What I know”, “What this means for your job”).

### Timeline

February 2026 — The author reports that new programming models complete work he previously handled. The technological capacity for substantial employment disruption may arrive by the end of 2026, with a lag before the full economic effects (“I know this is real”, “What this means for your job”).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** Off. The author describes a trajectory driven by frontier labs and user adoption, without a specified government response.
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Not addressed.
- **Policies the source recommends:** Not addressed. The author recommends individual preparation through practising with current models, saving where possible, and developing skills grounded in trust and accountability (“What you should actually do”).

## Key dynamics explored

- A small number of frontier companies improve broadly capable software, and users experience jumps when new models ship (opening, “How fast this is actually moving”).
- Current models help develop later models, creating a feedback loop the author expects to accelerate (“AI is now building the next AI”).
- AI can improve across many occupation-specific skills simultaneously, reducing options for retraining into another screen-based field (“What this means for your job”).
- Organizational adoption and labour-market change lag technical capability as people gain experience with the tools (“What this means for your job”, “What you should actually do”).

## State of technology

Frontier models can interpret specifications, write and test multi-step software work and revise it after feedback. The author describes apparent judgement about design choices, sustained task performance, legal and analytical work, and AI contributions to model development. Robotics lags digital work, but he expects physical tasks eventually to be affected (“I know this is real”, “AI is now building the next AI”, “What this means for your job”).

## State of adoption

The author says free products can lag advanced paid models, obscuring capability gains. Software teams, some lawyers and customer-service organisations already use the technology. He suggests testing substantial real assignments, learning iteratively, and adopting early within organisations (“How fast this is actually moving”, “What this means for your job”, “What you should actually do”).

### Labour market dynamics

Shumer expects fewer programming roles and broad disruption in legal work, financial analysis, writing, medical analysis and customer service. He explicitly attributes the estimate of 50% of entry-level white-collar jobs disappearing within one to five years to Dario Amodei. Physical presence, trusted relationships and licensed accountability can delay replacement (“What this means for your job”, “What you should actually do”).

### Winners and losers

Workers who understand and apply the tools early can become especially valuable during the transition. Screen-based work is exposed, while relationship, in-person and legally accountable work may be harder to displace (“What this means for your job”, “What you should actually do”).

### Household incomes and living standards

The author urges people to save where possible, moderate new debt commitments that assume stable income, and create financial room to adapt or change jobs (“What you should actually do”).
