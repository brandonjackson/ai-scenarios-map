# Uneven disruption — extract

## Summary
“In a less severe—though still disruptive—scenario, AI’s labor market effects remain highly uneven across sectors and occupations.” (“Uneven disruption” section). There may be significant job loss in sectors and roles where AI does most of the tasks, while jobs grow in new or existing roles that use AI to be more productive, “perhaps resembling labor market changes resulting from past periods of technological disruption” (“Uneven disruption”). Even if total jobs rise, the transition is hard for affected workers and communities: past displaced workers took years to find work and often earned much less when they did (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58). Educators and business leaders face the challenge, including a generational divide between employees new to AI and those who grew up with it (“Uneven disruption”, n. 59).

## Key details
- **Source:** *AI & the Labor Force: Scenarios for Stakeholders*, no named authors, The Conference Board (Solutions Brief, Policy Watch series), 15 September 2026. `sources/conference-board-ai-labor-force-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** The Conference Board press release “AI Could Reshape the US Workforce in 4 Very Different Ways” restates the four scenarios (listed in `source.json`; not read for this extract).
- **Reading notes:** Full text of the report as published on the HTML publication page (`private/fulltext.txt`); cookie, navigation, sign-in and registration text skipped; the downloadable PDF was not used. No page numbers: claims are cited by section heading, with the report's inline endnote number where one is given (`n. 36`). Figures 1–3 are images: only their captions, notes and source lines are in the text, no data values. The endnote list in the text file is unnumbered; numbers were matched by order (80 entries). The press release, the companion chart/infographic and the webcast were not read.
- **Scenario set:** Four scenarios in one framework: **Gradual augmentation**, **Concentrated gains**, **Significant displacement** and **Uneven disruption** (“Impact Scenarios” section). The report names no axes or drivers; each is defined in one line: “Gradual augmentation: AI primarily aids rather than replaces workers and impacts are slow to materialize.” / “Concentrated gains: AI has relatively modest employment impacts overall but leads to significant productivity gains for a limited subset of industries and occupations.” / “Significant displacement: AI leads to significant job loss across a broad range of occupations.” / “Uneven disruption: AI leads to significant displacement but in a limited subset of occupations.” (“Impact Scenarios” section). *[Inference: the four differ mainly in the size and breadth of job loss and in how concentrated the gains are.]* The report ranks severity only in places: Uneven disruption is “a less severe—though still disruptive—scenario” than Significant displacement (“Uneven disruption” section), and the policy section groups “scenarios with modest negative employment impacts” apart from “more significant impacts—such as the concentrated gains and uneven disruption scenarios” (“UI and public benefits” section).
- **Geography:** United States. A few data points are global: more than 1.2 billion people have used an AI tool (“Adoption trends”, n. 8); 25% of global employment is exposed to AI (“Significant displacement” section, n. 47).
- **Authors' framing:** Planning scenarios for stakeholders (policymakers, educators, CEOs), paired with recommendations. “Given the stakes, it is important to consider several possible scenarios and consider their potential implications for policymakers, educators, and employers.” (“Introduction”). “While the labor market effects of AI are uncertain, waiting to take action until the outlook is clear could leave policymakers and CEOs ill-prepared to respond to quickly changing conditions.” (“Recommendations”). “Despite widespread concerns about largescale disruptions, the evidence to date supports neither complacency nor alarm.” (“Conclusion”).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** Described as “less severe—though still disruptive” compared with Significant displacement (“Uneven disruption” section); no probability. No probabilities are given for any scenario. Across the set: “It remains unclear which labor market impact scenario will unfold as AI is integrated into the workplace and the technology evolves.” (“Public Policy Impacts”). So far “the net effect of AI on the labor market appears, at least so far, to be muted” (“Employment Impacts”, n. 24), but “this picture is far from conclusive, and the evidence is quickly evolving” (“Employment Impacts”), and impacts “may be more significant” as firm-level abilities increase (“Employment Impacts”, n. 35). The Conclusion says AI “may follow” the Solow pattern of delayed, uneven effects, “although its speed of adoption and reach into cognitive work could make the transition faster and more disruptive than previous technological shifts” (“Conclusion”).

### Timeline
No horizon year for the scenario. Evidence cited:
- Four years after displacement (trade-displaced workers study, undated) — only about 60% re-employed (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58)
- 2021–2023 — long-tenured workers displaced (BLS) (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58)
- 2024 — of those, about 16% unemployed and 18% out of the labour force; about 40% of the re-employed earned less than before (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58)

**Applies across the set (no horizon year is given for the set; background dates in order):**
- 1987 — Robert Solow: “You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics.” (“Introduction”, n. 1)
- Late 1990s — productivity growth rose after nearly 25 years of stagnation (“Introduction”, n. 2)
- November 2022 — public introduction of ChatGPT (“Introduction”)
- 2023 — Conference Board survey: more than half of employees used AI on the job; 4% expected it to replace their job (“Employment Impacts”, n. 26)
- 2025 — total factor productivity growth was 0.8% (“Productivity”, n. 21)
- End of 2025 — about 18% of US firms and 41% of US workers reported using AI (“Adoption trends”, n. 9)
- Early 2026 — job-trend analyses find no link between AI exposure and vacancies or aggregate labour demand (“Employment Impacts”, n. 25)
- 2026 — AI-related investment projected to exceed $1 trillion (“Introduction”)
- Mid-2026 — 47% of US employees say their organisation has adopted AI (“Adoption trends”, n. 10)
- 2030s — one projection: AI adds up to 0.2 percentage points a year to productivity growth (“Productivity”, n. 20)
- About 2045 — that effect fades to less than 0.04 percentage points as adoption saturates (“Productivity”, n. 20)

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** **off** — the scenario narrative describes no government action; the policy section names this scenario as one needing targeted interventions (“UI and public benefits”).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Not addressed.
- **Policies the source recommends:** Named explicitly: “Under more significant impacts—such as the concentrated gains and uneven disruption scenarios—targeted interventions may be needed to assist those in affected occupations, career stages, or regions experiencing severe shocks.” Options: support modelled on Trade Adjustment Assistance (created 1974 for offshoring-displaced workers); supplemental federal funding for extended unemployment benefits based on occupational displacement, regional unemployment or earnings losses rather than statewide unemployment rates (“UI and public benefits”, n. 63). The scenario section says the transition “would create challenges for both educators and business leaders”, who would need to bridge a generational divide in AI use (“Uneven disruption”, n. 59).

**Applies across the set: the report's recommendations** (“Recommendations” section; the report says to act now, whichever scenario unfolds):
- *Improve data collection and set up early-warning indicators.* Federal agencies should work with employers and educational institutions to use existing workforce and enrollment administrative data to see how AI is used and how it affects jobs, while protecting businesses' confidential information and individuals' privacy. Congress should consider more funding for the Department of Labor, Bureau of Labor Statistics and Census Bureau to expand data collection and economic research. Stakeholders should identify more indicators from AI-related changes to job postings, job loss, earnings and other factors “to enable rapid responses to changing economic conditions when needed”; the Conference Board's Help Wanted OnLine Index and AI and Automation Risk Tool are given as examples. CEOs should assess their firm's internal capacity to use AI productively (culture, workforce capabilities, data quality, processes) and study how employees use AI to learn which use cases raise productivity (“Recommendations: Improve data collection and establish early-warning indicators”).
- *Invest in worker training and education.* CEOs should expand employer-connected training, apprenticeships, internships and work-based learning in professional services, health care, technology, finance, advanced manufacturing and other fields where AI is likely to change job duties. Policymakers should give community colleges, universities and workforce organisations flexible funding to expand capacity quickly when disruption raises enrollment, favouring programmes with strong completion, employment and earnings outcomes “so that capacity constraints do not divert displaced workers into fields with limited demand”. CEOs should build pathways for highly educated and mid-career workers to move existing expertise into adjacent occupations. CEOs should reassess talent-pipeline, succession and knowledge-transfer strategies so that reduced entry-level hiring does not weaken the future supply of experienced workers and senior departures do not erode institutional knowledge (“Recommendations: Invest in worker training and education”).
- *Modernise unemployment insurance and public-benefit systems.* “Even a concentrated or temporary increase in displacement could strain unemployment insurance and other public-benefit programs”; systems should be improved before a major shock rather than during a crisis. Policymakers should replace or modernise outdated UI technology, adopt common data standards, strengthen cybersecurity, and build systems that can expand rapidly. The Department of Labor should work with states on surge-capacity plans (staffing, call centres, application processing, identity verification, fraud prevention, appeals, communication with claimants), tested through regular simulations. Agencies should use AI where appropriate for customer service, fraud detection and processing, keeping human governance and oversight. Policymakers should keep evaluating whether benefit programmes still help displaced workers while “providing strong incentives for work” (“Recommendations: Modernize unemployment insurance and public-benefit systems”).
- *Education and training design* (“Education and training” section): flexible programmes with more generous funding have large long-term effects on earnings (n. 75); programmes tied to employers that teach targeted occupational and workplace skills have shown benefits (n. 76); stakeholders “should be prepared to experiment with different types of reskilling programs, carefully study their results, and adjust as needed” (n. 77). Employers will need to plan workplace training so workers in AI-augmented roles can build skills to stay competitive.
- The Conclusion: policymakers, educators and employers “should evaluate potential impact scenarios and prepare workers, policies, and programs to capitalize on upsides and remain resilient in the face of downsides” (“Conclusion”).

**Responses the report ties to scenario severity** (“UI and public benefits” section):
- “Under scenarios with modest negative employment impacts, demand for unemployment benefits might resemble a traditional mild to moderate recession and not require specialized policy responses.” Congress may still consider targeted actions, as in past recessions: funding state unemployment systems or extending benefit duration. The report does not say which named scenarios count as “modest”.
- “Under more significant impacts—such as the concentrated gains and uneven disruption scenarios—targeted interventions may be needed to assist those in affected occupations, career stages, or regions experiencing severe shocks.” Options: support modelled on Trade Adjustment Assistance (created 1974 for workers displaced by offshoring, n. 63); basing supplemental federal funding for extended unemployment benefits on occupational displacement, regional unemployment or earnings losses rather than statewide unemployment rates.
- “The potential for high and persistent joblessness has also led to calls for consideration of more novel, controversial, and potentially costly steps including providing long-term wage subsidization for displaced workers, a universal basic income, or distributing a public dividend from AI model profits.” (n. 64). The report reports these calls; it does not endorse them, and it does not tie them to a named scenario.

## Key dynamics explored
- AI does most tasks in some sectors and roles, causing significant job loss there, while roles that use AI to be more productive grow (“Uneven disruption”).
- Aggregate jobs may rise, yet the transition is hard for affected workers and communities because displaced workers take long to find work and lose earnings (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58).
- The pattern may resemble past technological disruptions (“Uneven disruption”).
- Business leaders must bridge a generational divide between employees new to AI tools and those who grew up using them (“Uneven disruption”, n. 59).

**Applies across the set:**
- New technology shows up late in the statistics because effects come “through delayed, uneven, and institution-dependent processes” (“Conclusion”); “You can see the AI age everywhere but in the labor statistics.” (“Introduction”).
- Evidence lags because research is retrospective, takes time, and often uses data from an older economy and from older, far less capable models (“Introduction”; “Current Research on Economic Impacts”).
- AI has “broken down the barrier limiting machines to tasks following explicit rules”, which earlier computerisation respected (“Current Research on Economic Impacts”, n. 7).
- Adoption is “highly correlated” with exposure: AI is diffusing fastest into jobs where it can most reshape tasks (“Adoption trends”, n. 13).
- Impacts may become more significant as firm-level AI abilities increase; most firms are still in “early experimentation” (“Employment Impacts”, n. 35).
- Given the pace of AI advance and diffusion, “both positive and negative impacts may spread rapidly” (“Trusted Insights for What’s Ahead”).
- Job loss raises UI demand; consumer spending “drops sharply when UI benefits are exhausted” (“UI and public benefits”, n. 65).
- Displaced workers keep lower earnings after re-employment, especially in a weak job market; long-tenured workers see the largest earnings losses (“UI and public benefits”, n. 60–61).
- Labour disruption raises enrollment in Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid and SNAP, and these effects can persist after a recession ends (“UI and public benefits”, n. 69–71); it also strains agency administrative capacity and nongovernmental aid providers, including charitable and religious organisations (“UI and public benefits”).
- Job loss drives college enrollment, most of all at community and two-year colleges; capacity limits in high-return programmes (technical, health care) push students into lower-return programmes (“Education and training”, n. 73–74).
- Because AI could displace highly educated or specialised workers, unlike past recessions, “traditional reskilling pathways” may be ill-suited (“Education and training”).
- Labour disruption cuts tax revenue and raises mandatory and discretionary spending (“Fiscal impacts”).
- Reduced entry-level hiring can weaken the future supply of experienced workers; senior departures can erode institutional knowledge (“Recommendations: Invest in worker training and education”).
- The outcome “will depend not only on what the technology can do but also on the choices that businesses, educators, and policymakers make as it is deployed” (“Conclusion”).

## State of technology
AI completes most tasks in some sectors and roles; in others it is a productivity tool (“Uneven disruption”). Provision (market structure, pricing, compute): Not addressed.

**Applies across the set:**
- AI's capabilities “remain uneven and, in some cases, can lead to worse employee performance” (“Trusted Insights for What’s Ahead”). For tasks outside the model's frontier, consultants with AI were 19% less likely to reach correct solutions than those without it (“Productivity”, n. 19).
- AI is a “rapidly evolving technology”; current models are far more capable than those in the studies (“Introduction”).
- Use of AI for automation (the user gives a task and the model completes it with minimal human involvement) has increased relative to augmentation (the user asks for information and iterates) (“Adoption trends”, n. 14).
- One estimate of occupational exposure (the share of a job's tasks AI can complete) has not increased in three years, even as models have become more capable (“Employment Impacts”, n. 27). Other research finds exposure in web development, database design and software development “has increased significantly” as models improved (“Employment Impacts”, n. 34).
- Investment: AI-related investment “is projected to exceed $1 trillion in 2026” (“Introduction”).
- Named providers appear only as sources of evidence or statements: OpenAI, Anthropic (Claude usage data; its CEO's forecast). Market structure, number of providers, open vs closed models, compute, energy and pricing: Not addressed.
- Physical AI / robotics: Not addressed, beyond “potential advances in domains ranging from medicine to manufacturing” (“Introduction”).

## State of adoption
Uneven across sectors and occupations (“Uneven disruption”). Generational gap: employees new to AI tools vs those who grew up using them (“Uneven disruption”, n. 59). Other drivers or barriers: Not addressed.

**Applies across the set:**
- “By some estimates, AI is the quickest-adopted general-purpose technology in human history”, with more than 1.2 billion users (“Adoption trends”, n. 8; Figure 1 compares AI from 2022 with the PC from 1981 and the internet from 1995, no values in text).
- About 18% of US firms and 41% of US workers reported using AI through end-2025, more at larger firms and in professional services (including scientific and technical services), finance and insurance (“Adoption trends”, n. 9). A mid-2026 survey: 47% of US employees say their organisation has adopted AI (“Adoption trends”, n. 10).
- Higher adoption among younger workers, those with more education and higher earners (“Adoption trends”, n. 11).
- Regional readiness varies: about one-third of US metro areas are in the top tier of AI preparedness (talent, innovation infrastructure, adoption) (“Adoption trends”, n. 12).
- Most firms are in the “early experimentation” phase of AI maturity (“Employment Impacts”, n. 35).
- Adoption depends on firm factors, workforce readiness and other variables (“Introduction”); CEOs should assess culture, workforce capabilities, data quality and processes (“Recommendations”).
- Government use: agencies should use AI in benefit administration (customer service, fraud detection, processing) with human oversight (“Recommendations: Modernize unemployment insurance and public-benefit systems”).
- “Workplace adoption of AI has far outpaced that of other modern technologies.” (“Conclusion”)
- Other countries' adoption: Not addressed.

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
Not addressed beyond job growth in roles that “leverage AI to be more productive” (“Uneven disruption”).

**Applies across the set (evidence the report reviews):**
- Customer support: +14% issues resolved per hour, driven by novice and low-skilled workers (+34%), with minimal effect on experienced and highly skilled workers (“Productivity”, n. 15).
- Writing tasks: higher productivity and worker satisfaction, biggest gains for the weakest writers (“Productivity”, n. 16).
- Software developers: +26% task completion, with higher adoption and gains among less-experienced developers (“Productivity”, n. 17).
- Medical diagnosis: benefits across many specialties; could help relieve health care workforce shortages (“Productivity”, n. 18).
- Management consultants: faster and higher quality, but 19% less likely to be correct on tasks beyond the AI's frontier (“Productivity”, n. 19).
- Projection: AI adds up to 0.2 percentage points a year to productivity growth in the 2030s, fading to less than 0.04 points by about 2045 as adoption saturates; for reference, TFP growth was 0.8% in 2025 (“Productivity”, n. 20–21).
- Individual productivity gains have been “slower to materialize and harder to discern” (“Conclusion”; “Trusted Insights for What’s Ahead”).
- Historical analogy: after the 1987 Solow remark, productivity growth rose in the late 1990s after nearly 25 years of stagnation (“Introduction”, n. 2).

### Labour market dynamics
- Significant job loss in some sectors and roles; job growth in new or existing AI-using roles; “even if aggregate job levels increase” (“Uneven disruption”).
- One-line definition: “significant displacement but in a limited subset of occupations” (“Impact Scenarios”).
- Re-employment evidence: in one study only about 60% of trade-displaced workers were re-employed after four years, and they replaced “only slightly more than half” of prior earnings (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58).
- BLS: of long-tenured workers displaced in 2021–2023, about 16% were unemployed and 18% out of the labour force in 2024; about 40% of the re-employed earned less than before (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58). (The policy section cites the same survey as “only 62% earned as much or more” among those re-employed full time (“UI and public benefits”, n. 62).)
- Generational divide in AI use within workforces (“Uneven disruption”, n. 59).
- Which sectors or occupations: Not addressed in this section.

**Applies across the set (evidence the report reviews):**
- Net labour-market effect so far “appears, at least so far, to be muted”; analyses through early 2026 find no link between AI exposure and job vacancies and no evidence of lower aggregate labour demand (“Employment Impacts”, n. 24–25; Figure 2, no values in text).
- 2023 survey: more than half of employees used AI at work; 4% expected it to replace their job (“Employment Impacts”, n. 26).
- No significant negative wage effects found so far in AI-exposed occupations; possible wage gains for lower-skilled workers (“Employment Impacts”, n. 28–29).
- Data through 2023: tasks exposed to AI saw reduced labour demand, with modest overall employment effects (“Employment Impacts”, n. 30).
- Early-career workers: several studies link AI exposure to lower employment; others find no effect (“Employment Impacts”, n. 31–33).
- Major employers have warned AI could cause “significant and permanent job reductions”; many experts dispute this, “including, more recently, many who previously made them” (“Introduction”, n. 4, 6).
- Displaced long-tenured workers (pandemic period): 16% unemployed and 18% out of the labour force by 2024; of those re-employed full time, only 62% earned as much or more than before (“UI and public benefits”, n. 62).
- Only about 28% of laid-off workers collect UI (“UI and public benefits”, n. 66).
- A 1 percentage point rise in local unemployment raises first-time community-college enrollment by about 2% (“Education and training”, n. 73).
- AI will change the skills required in existing jobs and the mix of occupations employers demand (“Recommendations: Invest in worker training and education”).

### Winners and losers
- Winners: workers in new or existing roles that use AI to be more productive (“Uneven disruption”).
- Losers: workers and communities in sectors and roles where AI does most tasks; displaced long-tenured workers (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58).
- Age: a generational divide between employees new to AI and those who grew up with it (“Uneven disruption”, n. 59).

**Applies across the set:**
- Adoption is higher among younger, more educated and higher-earning workers (“Adoption trends”, n. 11).
- In the productivity studies, gains go mostly to novice, low-skilled or less-experienced workers (“Productivity”, n. 15–17).
- Early-career workers and those unable to use AI are at higher risk; the displacement section opens this point with “As in other scenarios” (“Significant displacement” section; the same groups appear in “Gradual augmentation”).
- When displaced, long-tenured workers may see the worst employment outcomes and the largest earnings losses (“UI and public benefits”, n. 61).
- Unlike past recessions, AI could displace large numbers of highly educated or specialised workers (“Education and training”).
- Regions: about one-third of metros are in the top tier of AI readiness (“Adoption trends”, n. 12).

### Household incomes and living standards
Displaced workers who find new jobs often earn much less: trade-displaced workers replaced only slightly more than half of prior earnings; about 40% of re-employed long-tenured displaced workers earned less (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58).

**Applies across the set:**
- UI covers about 28% of laid-off workers and replaces about 40% of average weekly earnings, “leaving a significant potential income gap for affected workers” (“UI and public benefits”, n. 66).
- Consumer spending drops sharply when UI is exhausted (“UI and public benefits”, n. 65).
- Even as employment recovers, displaced workers “would likely continue to see reduced earnings, particularly in a weak job market” (“UI and public benefits”, n. 60).

### Government finances
Not addressed in the scenario section.

**Applies across the set:**
- Disruption brings revenue losses plus higher mandatory and discretionary spending. Great Recession, first year: total federal receipts fell nearly 17%, mainly from a 20% fall in individual income tax receipts (“Fiscal impacts”, n. 79). Pandemic UI cost about $800 billion in its first year (“Fiscal impacts”; inline note marked 70, *[Inference: probably a numbering slip for n. 80, which is the UI source]*). Large disruptions “could also worsen the fiscal crisis facing Social Security” (“Fiscal impacts”).
- Fiscal effect depends on scenario (“Fiscal impacts”): “If AI mostly raises worker productivity and preserves employment, tax revenue should be neutral to positive overall: more output, more taxable wages, and some increase in business receipts. However, even in a scenario with positive productivity and employment effects for some workers, lower overall labor demand may still negatively impact government budgets. Likewise, scenarios that result in widespread unemployment or disproportionate displacement of higher-earning workers could have severe impacts on the tax base at federal, state, and local levels.” The report does not map these cases onto the named scenarios.
- Benefit programmes: Great Recession induced nearly 1 million extra SSDI applications and more than 400,000 extra beneficiaries in 2008–2012 (n. 70); payments for unemployment, disability, retirement and health care rose sharply in labour markets exposed to Chinese imports, 1990–2007; SNAP enrollment kept rising until 2013 though the recession ended in mid-2009 (n. 71) (“UI and public benefits”).
- Administration: in the pandemic, millions faced UI delays and payment errors rose from 9.2% to 18.9% (n. 67); UI fraud cost an estimated $100–135 billion (n. 68); disability determinations slowed from 121 to 219 days on average between 2019 and 2023 (n. 72) (“UI and public benefits”).
- Recommended spending: more funds for DOL, BLS and Census data work; flexible funding for colleges and workforce organisations; UI technology modernisation (“Recommendations”).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
Displaced workers often earn less after re-employment: trade-displaced workers replaced only slightly more than half of pre-separation earnings; about 40% of re-employed long-tenured displaced workers earned less (“Uneven disruption”, n. 58).

**Applies across the set:** Policymakers should check that benefit programmes keep “providing strong incentives for work” (“Recommendations”). Displaced workers who find new jobs often earn less: only 62% of re-employed long-tenured workers displaced in the pandemic earned as much or more (“UI and public benefits”, n. 62).

### Affordable essentials
Not addressed.

**Applies across the set:** Not addressed. (Medicaid and SNAP appear only as benefit programmes whose enrollment rises with job loss; see *Reliable safety net*.)

### Reliable safety net
The policy section names this scenario as needing targeted interventions: TAA-style support; extended benefits keyed to occupational displacement, regional unemployment or earnings losses rather than statewide rates (“UI and public benefits”, n. 63).

**Applies across the set:** The report's main policy concern. Under any scenario with job loss, UI demand rises first (“UI and public benefits”). UI reaches about 28% of laid-off workers and replaces about 40% of earnings (n. 66). Pandemic experience: delays for millions, error rate 9.2% → 18.9%, fraud $100–135 billion (n. 67–68). Disruption also raises enrollment in Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid and SNAP, strains agencies (disability determinations 121 → 219 days, 2019–2023) and burdens charitable and religious providers (“UI and public benefits”). “Even a concentrated or temporary increase in displacement could strain unemployment insurance and other public-benefit programs.” (“Recommendations”). Remedies: modernise UI technology and surge capacity before a shock; severity-linked options (targeted aid, TAA-style support, extended benefits keyed to occupational or regional displacement); calls for wage subsidies, UBI or an AI public dividend reported, not endorsed (“Recommendations”; “UI and public benefits”).

## Other dynamics
Not addressed for this scenario.

**Applies across the set:**
- Existential risk: “prominent AI developers have likewise claimed that AI poses a potential existential threat to humanity” (“Introduction”, n. 5); not developed further.
- Measurement: data and research limits “risk leaving policymakers, educators, and CEOs with little insight into how AI is impacting the economy” (“Trusted Insights for What’s Ahead”); hence the early-warning indicators recommendation (“Recommendations”).
