# Diplomacy: Licensed Utopia — extract

## Summary

Serious safety incidents trigger international coordination, tighter development oversight and research on scalable alignment (Diplomacy, main path). Secure early deployments are expensive. When research improves oversight, a treaty lets private developers advance under adjustable capability thresholds, audits, fees and a 25% AI tax (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, 2029–31). Licensed firms supply advances in science, health and productivity and become more powerful. Global annual GDP growth reaches 7% in the ending, while rising unemployment in regions with weaker protections leads governments to shift from retraining to redistribution (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, 2031–32).

## Key details

- **Source:** Advanced AI: Possible futures, Bengüsu Özcan, Daan Juijn, Jakob Graabak, Sam Bogerd, Centre for Future Generations, 2025-07-10. `sources/cfg-advanced-ai-possible-futures-2025/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** The publisher’s scenario PDF and companion methodology, technical context and governance analysis are listed in the source manifest.
- **Reading notes:** Full publisher scenario text, narrative PDF, and companion methodology and governance analysis.
- **Scenario set:** The study crosses speed of capability progress with concentration of frontier development. The five core narratives are Plateau, Big AI, Diplomacy, Arms Race, and Take-off, each with two alternative endings (“How to navigate the scenarios?”; narrative PDF §1). This row follows Diplomacy and its first ending, Licensed Utopia. The alternative ending is Unstable Pause, where cooperation stalls and safety progress falls short.
- **Geography:** Global, with particular attention to the US, China and Europe (scenario narratives).
- **Authors' framing:** Plausible foresight scenarios for stress-testing policy under uncertainty, constructed from expert consultation and structured identification of key parameters. The numbers for training compute and R&D automation are directional (“Key Takeaways”; “AI futures in the numbers”; Methodology).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned. The authors selected plausible, deliberately diverse cases for decision-making (“Key Takeaways”; “How to navigate the scenarios?”).

### Timeline

- Mid-2025 to late-2026 — AI use and internal capabilities advance faster than oversight. The UK and Canada convene a security summit with work on common verification (Diplomacy, “Mid 2025 – late 2026”).
- Late-2026 to late-2027 — U.S. evaluations delay an advanced release. Staged deployment of Nova in September 2027 produces both enthusiasm and concerns about jobs and control. The Nova breach later that year prompts calls for a deployment pause (Diplomacy, “Late 2026 – late 2027”; “Late 2027”; “Late 2027 – early 2028”).
- 2028 to early 2029 — a Washington summit funds joint safety research and a Global AI Security Institute in London. Europe earmarks compute. Frontier work resumes, but the US and China agree to limit public release of new capabilities while retaining internal work (Diplomacy, “Early 2028 – Mid 2028”; “Mid 2028 – early 2029”).
- 2029–30 — safety improves, and restrictions partially relax for secure sites. The October 2029 interpretability breakthrough enables further oversight advances shared within six months (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, early 2029 to late 2030).
- Late 2030 to late 2031 — the US, EU, China and dozens of other countries agree to a licensing regime with IAEA oversight (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, late 2030 to late 2031).
- 2031–32 — health and productivity gains broaden. The threshold rises in September 2032, then a security incident causes a temporary licence freeze and debate over hosting frontier models in public infrastructure (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, late 2031 to late 2032).

### Policy stance

- **Stance:** On. Licensing, oversight, joint safety research, and redistribution govern access and gains in the story (Diplomacy, main path and “Licensed utopia”).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** The US and China first restrict public frontier release. Under the treaty, countries annually review a capability threshold, raising it by supermajority. Developers submit to standardized alignment, cybersecurity monitoring and on-site audits. Lump-sum fees and a 25% AI tax are redistributed among treaty nations by population size and development needs. The IAEA takes on verification. Countries later suspend new licences temporarily and tighten safeguards (Diplomacy, main path and “Licensed utopia”, 2030–32).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *Applies across the set.* Stress-test policy across different trajectories. Invest in adaptive labour-market and education institutions, information resilience and cyber defence. Coordinate among institutions and countries and develop credible verification for international agreements. Build scalable safeguards alongside innovation rather than relying solely on access restrictions (“Key Takeaways”; governance analysis §§2–5 and conclusion).

## Key dynamics explored

- Internal AI accelerates R&D faster than public tools reveal. The gap between capability and control produces crises that build public pressure and justify coordination (Diplomacy, “Baseline assumptions”; main path).
- Shared compute, institutional research and better interpretability assist efforts to detect problems and use AI supervision to improve alignment. Secure infrastructure and unified standards then make treaty-governed development acceptable (Diplomacy, “Early 2028 – Mid 2028”; “Licensed utopia”, 2029–31).
- Licensing limits participation and adds a safety backstop, while strengthening the few firms able to qualify. Fees, tax and later redistribution are mechanisms for spreading some of the gains (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, 2030–32).

## State of technology

Agents first augment AI R&D, then advance science outside AI. Internal systems lead public deployments and safety progress initially lags. An interpretability breakthrough helps detect deception. AI-assisted oversight, shared techniques and secure facilities enable superhuman systems under human control in the ending (Diplomacy, main path and “Licensed utopia”, 2029–31).

## State of adoption

Software changes first, followed by consulting, finance and desk research. Enterprise deployments initially have strong returns, but public releases lag internal capabilities and access to newly relaxed models is limited and very costly. Some consumers and firms choose human-only alternatives on grounds of ethics and privacy. By 2032 higher licensing thresholds release systems easier to integrate across knowledge work, and most industries use agents (Diplomacy, main path and “Licensed utopia”).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity

Automation appears in macroeconomic data by early 2029. Developed-country growth rises to 4–5% annually with advances in biotech, materials and energy. With agents embedded in most industries, global GDP growth reaches 7% annually by late 2032 (Diplomacy, “Mid 2028 – early 2029”; “Licensed utopia”, late 2031 to late 2032).

### Labour market dynamics

Senior programmers shift to architecture, and junior programmers work more as AI managers. Consultants, analysts and desk researchers also supervise agents. Unemployment rises in places with weaker labour protections once cognitive automation is widespread. Education turns toward critical thinking, emotional intelligence and mental health as traditional skills lose relevance (Diplomacy, main path and “Licensed utopia”).

### Winners and losers

Few companies qualify for licences and gain outsized commercial value and bargaining power. Treaty members share tax and fee revenues. People in regions with weak labour protections experience higher unemployment, even as many countries gain access to better treatments and other innovations (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, 2030–32).

### Household incomes and living standards

Governments redirect efforts from retraining to large-scale wealth redistribution when the labour adjustment accelerates. Families gain leisure and reshape community structures, and access to health services improves (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, late 2031 to late 2032).

### Government finances

An EU “CERN for AI” coordinates public research compute and 35% of European Gigafactories’ capacity is pledged to safety/security. The treaty later redistributes revenues from lump-sum fees and a 25% AI tax among treaty participants, according to population and development needs. The account separately describes governments expanding wealth redistribution (Diplomacy, “Early 2028 – Mid 2028”; “Licensed utopia”, 2030–32).

### International economic patterns

Joint research brings private developers and institutes together. After an initial U.S.–China compromise, the treaty includes the EU and many other countries. Exclusion remains a security challenge. The EU proposes offering only API access from public infrastructure, while some states and licensed firms oppose that step on innovation grounds (Diplomacy, main path and “Licensed utopia”).

### Market pricing and scarcity

Limited facilities make access to early secure model deployments extremely expensive (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, early 2029 to late 2030).

### Financial markets and stability

At Nova’s launch, financial analysts predict large productivity gains, while labour economists warn of employment shocks. The story gives no market-price or credit path (Diplomacy, “Late 2026 – late 2027”).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded

Not addressed.

### Affordable essentials

AI-assisted diagnostics, personalized treatments and new medicines improve access and health outcomes. The scenario says much less about household prices for those services (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, late 2031 to late 2032).

### Reliable safety net

Governments move from retraining programmes to large-scale wealth redistribution under faster displacement. The narrative does not specify benefit design (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, late 2031 to late 2032).

### Time for what matters

Families adapt to increased leisure time through new community structures and norms (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, late 2031 to late 2032).

### A say in your future

Public fear and safety activism create pressure for action and trust improves with coordination. People express concern about the growing power of the small set of licensed firms. Some organize around preserving human-led services and AI-minimal spaces (Diplomacy, main path and “Licensed utopia”).

### A fair share of progress

The treaty distributes fee and tax revenues among member countries. Health, cancer research, materials, superconductors and carbon capture are examples of advances. Access to models and commercial control remain uneven (Diplomacy, “Licensed utopia”, 2030–32).

## Other dynamics

*Applies across the set.* The authors argue that even existing AI capabilities can disrupt labour, education, media and political institutions if widely deployed. Resistance can impede beneficial uses. The pace of R&D automation, control of supply chains and the ability to verify safety commitments are critical uncertainties (“Key Takeaways”; governance analysis §§1–5). Control and cybersecurity incidents prompt a pause, coordination, and later a temporary freeze in new licences. Initial alignment gains slow training compute growth after the 2028 crisis, then controlled progress in automated research approaches the benchmark’s upper range (Diplomacy, main path and “Licensed utopia”; Methodology, Appendix 2).
