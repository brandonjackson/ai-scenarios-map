# Capital wins amid major permanent job displacement — extract

## Summary
AI has "more profound effects". Annual total factor productivity (TFP) growth in the US nonfarm business (NFB) sector is 1 percentage point higher than without AI, twice the increment in Scenarios #1–#3. Major permanent job displacement phases in over three decades, raising unemployment by 2 points and cutting labour force participation by 4 points (pp. 2, 18–19; Table 5). Extra capital investment replaces the lost labour, so GDP is about 9% higher after a decade, 21% after two and 35% after three. All the extra income goes to capital owners in the top quintile, and the capital and labour shares "nearly reverse" (pp. 19, 22). Remaining workers' living standards are unchanged apart from more government services, and displaced workers' living standards presumably fall (p. 19). Because capital is taxed more lightly than labour, federal debt falls by the same 49 percentage points of GDP as in Scenario #2 despite double the growth (p. 21). The authors extend the menu of responses: fostering jobs, a universal basic income (UBI), higher taxes on capital and public ownership of businesses (pp. 23–30; Table 5).

## Key details
- **Source:** How Might Fiscal Policy Respond to the Rise of Artificial Intelligence?, Karen Dynan, Douglas Elmendorf and Louise Sheiner, Brookings Institution, August 2026. The paper was written for the Brookings Institution–University of Utah conference on "The Forces Shaping America's Fiscal Future" in June 2026 (cover). `sources/dynan-elmendorf-sheiner-fiscal-ai-2026/`
- **Other sources for this scenario:** None
- **Reading notes:** Full text (pp. 1–39, including references). Figure 1, the chart of debt paths, is image-only in the text extraction. This scenario is its long-dashed line, and values are taken from the prose and Table 5 (pp. 7, 21).
- **Scenario set:** Four illustrative scenarios: #1 A rising tide lifts all boats proportionally; #2 Income inequality increases; #3 Jobs are lost and new jobs emerge; #4 Capital wins amid major permanent job displacement (this one). They are built from three drivers: the increase in annual TFP growth (0.5 pp in #1–#3, 1 pp in #4), the reduction in the number of workers (none, none, 2%, 6%) and the distribution of the extra income (proportional to income without AI; proportional to capital and labour but all to the top quintile; the same; all to owners of capital in the top quintile) (pp. 1–3; Table 1).
- **Geography:** United States, federal budget only (p. 1).
- **Authors' framing:** Thought experiment and planning scenario for fiscal policy. "The scenarios are not intended to be specific predictions, but rather illustrations of the types of effects that have garnered scholarly and popular attention in recent months" (p. 1). The scenarios are additional to the AI effects already in CBO's projections (p. 1). The conclusion calls the disruptive possibilities here, "an ongoing economy-wide shortfall of jobs or a dramatic shift in income toward capital", "fundamentally new developments" (p. 30).
- **Authors' view of likelihood:** No probability is assigned: "We do not attempt to assign probabilities to the different scenarios, which seems to us exceedingly difficult" (p. 1). *Applies across the set:* the scenarios "include only a subset of the possible effects of AI" (p. 3). "Immense uncertainty remains" (p. 32).

### Timeline
- Horizon: 30 years, matching CBO's long-term projections to 2056 (pp. 1, 6).
- 2026 — federal debt held by the public is 101% of GDP in CBO's extended baseline (p. 6).
- Coming decade — CBO already includes AI adding 0.1 pp to NFB TFP growth (p. 1). CBO's marginal tax rates on new investment are about 6% for equipment and, after the next few years, about 21% for structures (p. 21).
- After 10 years — GDP is about 9% higher than without AI (p. 19).
- After 20 years — GDP is about 21% higher (p. 19).
- Over three decades — the unemployment rise (2 pp) and participation fall (4 pp) phase in gradually as productivity rises, at a moderate pace (pp. 2–3, 19).
- 2056 (after 30 years) — GDP is about 35% higher, and debt is 49 pp of GDP below the baseline's 175%, leaving aside interest-rate changes (pp. 6, 19, 21).
- Historical references: the 1930s (unemployment above 14% every year from 1931 to 1940, the Civilian Conservation Corps and the Works Progress Administration, and Social Security's origins) (pp. 24–25). The full Social Security retirement age has risen from 65 to 67 over past decades (p. 25).

### Policy stance
- **Stance:** Off. The scenario's budget results assume no significant changes in fiscal policy, under current law and CBO's projection methodology. The large responses discussed are options, not part of the scenario (pp. 1, 4, 21; Table 5).
- **Policies assumed in the scenario:** Current law and CBO's projection methodology, with the authors' extended-baseline treatment of discretionary spending (pp. 5–6). The current tax treatment of capital applies: preferential rates, deferral until realisation, step-up in basis at inheritance, a 21% corporate rate, and some evasion (p. 21).
- **Policies the source recommends:** *This scenario:* options, with trade-offs (Table 5, pp. 19–20; pp. 23–30):
  - All earlier options apply: keep closer historical budget-to-GDP relationships, increase progressivity, and support and retrain displaced workers through unemployment insurance, wage insurance and training (p. 23). The fall in employment would cut employer-sponsored health insurance and increase pressure to expand public insurance (p. 23). Social Security's benefit formula may need adjusting for people without work for years (p. 23, n. 9).
  - **Foster employment opportunities** (pp. 24–25):
    - Support private jobs through more training and job matching, or direct subsidies. Eliminating payroll taxes would cut the cost of employment by about 15%, perhaps only for earnings below a threshold. Earnings records would still need crediting, and this would undermine Social Security and Medicare's earned-benefit nature (p. 24, n. 10). "Large negative tax rates" on work are another possibility (Weinzierl 2025) (p. 24).
    - Provide public jobs that are scalable without much training or capital, give workers a sense of purpose and do not crowd out private jobs. Traditional public works fail these tests, but personal care for older people and children might pass (pp. 24–25).
    - Encourage earlier retirement and shorter workweeks, for example by lowering the full Social Security and Medicare ages and enabling phased retirement. This reverses recent policy (p. 25).
    - Have the public sector support or coordinate the training of junior workers, since training has positive externalities and AI may supplant junior workers' immediate output (p. 25).
  - **Institute UBI.** It is very costly: $15,000 a year per person would cost over $5 trillion annually. It would shift transfers toward childless, non-elderly, non-disabled and middle-income households (Hoynes and Rothstein). Labour-supply effects are uncertain but may matter less with permanently insufficient labour demand (pp. 25–26).
  - **Tax capital at higher rates.** This would change debt substantially or fund other priorities, and improve the relative position of non-elite people. But it reduces saving, investment and growth, and capital mobility weakens it without global coordination (pp. 26–27). Routes: higher rates within the existing structure (Liscow 2026: pre-2017 top rate, historical corporate rate, end step-up basis), wealth taxes (constitutionally unclear federally; California's proposed 5% billionaire tax over five years, per Saez and Zucman 2026), consumption or VAT taxes with an exemption for moderate consumption (Korinek and Lockwood 2026), or taxes on AI and digital activity (a "compute tax", data taxes, a tax on AI use) (p. 27).
  - **Establish some public ownership of businesses.** It may be more robust to political swings, more likely seen as earned rather than redistributed income, and harder to avoid than taxes (p. 28). Options are a sovereign wealth fund, with design questions on financing, private placements, scope, active versus passive investment and budget scoring (pp. 28–29), or equity in individual accounts, ranging from non-tradable notional claims annuitised at older ages to supplementary IRAs holding index funds (pp. 29–30).

  *Applies across the set (and especially here):* "policies that are robust to different scenarios would be especially valuable" (p. 1). In this scenario waiting is especially costly: "waiting to observe that outcome definitively might foreclose some policy options altogether as vested interests become stronger" (p. 32). The authors suggest starting modest public equity purchases through a sovereign wealth fund, or a modernised TAA, now on a limited scale. A variant uses automatic triggers, with equity purchases beginning if the capital share rises above a preset level (p. 32). Risk "would be limited as long as equity purchases remained small" (pp. 32–33). Combinations may suit: Marinescu (2025) proposes TAA-like aid plus a UBI funded by a digital-economy tax, Sperling (2026) job creation plus support for contributors, and Korinek and Lockwood (2026) a UBI financed by consumption taxes (p. 31). Table 6 catalogues all responses (p. 31).

## Key dynamics explored
*This scenario*

- AI adds 1 pp to annual TFP growth. Extra capital investment replaces lost labour input, so GDP growth rises by the full TFP increment (pp. 2, 19).
- All extra income goes to top-quintile capital owners, who have high saving rates (Dynan, Skinner and Zeldes 2004). National saving rises (p. 19).
- Higher national saving means lower interest rates, so debt would compound more slowly than the authors estimate. Interest-rate effects are left out of this scenario's debt estimate because calibration "would be quite challenging" (p. 20).
- Higher saving also raises the risk that demand falls short of potential supply persistently, not just in recessions. Monetary policy would be constrained by the effective lower bound for longer, increasing pressure for countercyclical fiscal policy. This is not pursued further (p. 20).
- The extra income is taxed at a capital rate of 15%, against 25.9% for top-quintile labour and capital combined. The additional tax from growth is therefore 42% lower, and debt falls only as much as in Scenario #2 despite double the growth (pp. 20–21).
- Displaced workers lose income, purpose and social contact. Surveys and experiments show that work matters for dignity (Pope Leo XIV 2026; Ramamurti 2025; Hussam et al. 2022) (p. 22).
- The importance of work applies far more to working-age people than to retirees: the long-term unemployed report large life-satisfaction gains on retiring (Hetschko, Knabe and Schöb 2011). Social Security and Medicare enjoy political protection as earned social insurance (pp. 22–23). If displacement spreads, social norms about not working might change (Yglesias 2025), and so might support for policies (p. 23).
- The capital share could rise even higher (Trammell and Patel 2025) or stabilise well below 100% even with nearly complete automation (Aghion, Jones and Jones 2019) (p. 22).
- Capital owners who gain a much larger income share "would presumably gain even more political power" (p. 28). Trammell and Patel (2025) argue that political power follows economic power (p. 28).
- If junior workers' immediate services are supplanted by AI, uncoordinated hiring under-provides junior positions and training, which carries a positive externality (p. 25).
- The fall in employment cuts employer-sponsored health insurance (p. 23).

*Shared with Scenarios #1–#3 (repeated here)*

- Faster growth slightly raises revenue relative to GDP and markedly lowers non-interest spending relative to GDP under current law and CBO's method, through Social Security indexation, Medicare and Medicaid rules, unresponsive other mandatory spending and slower discretionary growth. It also shrinks existing debt relative to GDP (pp. 5–6).
- Concentrating income at the top raises personal income tax, lowers payroll tax and keeps more people eligible for benefits (p. 11).
- The improvement "may prove at least partly illusory" if policymakers keep historical budget-to-GDP relationships (p. 8).

*Applies across the set*

- Faster labour-market change would be more disruptive and increase pressure for responses. In this scenario job losses come at a moderate pace (p. 3).
- Ramsey-style analysis gives no clear deficit guidance in any scenario (pp. 8–9).

## State of technology
AI adds 1 pp to annual NFB TFP growth beyond CBO's baseline (pp. 2, 18). It permanently displaces large numbers of workers, and capital substitutes for labour (pp. 2, 19). "AI-enabled machines will provide an increasing amount of personal care", but the physical and emotional aspects of care are hard to automate fully, so demand for human care stays high (p. 25). AI may supplant many of the immediate services junior workers provide (p. 25). Much AI wealth is generated in private markets: privately held firms' share of corporate capital rose from 8% in 2000 to 19% today (Trammell and Patel 2025) (p. 29). Digital-sector businesses may be especially able to use tax-avoidance provisions (p. 28). Defining an "AI firm" for taxation would be daunting (p. 29). Number of providers, openness, compute and pricing are not addressed. *Applies across the set:* an AI boom might spur investment in computing capacity and energy (p. 8).

## State of adoption
Displacement phases in gradually over three decades as productivity rises (pp. 3, 19). Sectors, firm sizes and adoption barriers are not addressed. *Applies across the set:* the authors do not explore a case where public resistance dampens AI's spread. Pew (2025) found 50% of Americans more concerned than excited, 10% the opposite (p. 3).

## How the economy works

### GDP growth and productivity
TFP growth is 1 pp per year higher than without AI. GDP is about 9% higher after a decade, 21% after two and 35% after three (pp. 18–19). The authors cite CBO's 1.1% baseline NFB TFP growth for the other scenarios (p. 1). Higher capital taxes would reduce saving, investment and growth (p. 26).

### Labour market dynamics
Unemployment rises 2 pp and participation falls 4 pp, phasing in over three decades (pp. 2, 18–19). The number of workers falls by 6% (Table 1, p. 3). Displacement is permanent, and labour demand is ongoingly weak, "differ[ing] sharply from typical experience" (p. 25). The capital and labour shares nearly reverse from their traditional positions (p. 22). Junior positions may be under-provided (p. 25). Demand for human care work remains high (p. 25).

### Winners and losers
All the extra income goes to capital owners in the top quintile (p. 19; Tables 1, 5). People who keep their jobs have unchanged living standards apart from more government services (p. 19). "Workers who are permanently displaced face huge obstacles both professionally and personally" (Table 5). Their living standards presumably fall (p. 19). Displaced low-wage workers may face an income tax rate close to or below the capital rate (p. 20, n. 7).

### Household incomes and living standards
For workers who keep their jobs, living standards are unchanged except that government services increase, by less than GDP (p. 19). For displaced workers, living standards presumably fall, and they lose purpose and social contact as well as income (pp. 19, 22). People without work for years would earn fewer Social Security credits and receive smaller benefits without formula changes (p. 23, n. 9). Loss of employer-sponsored health insurance (p. 23).

### Government finances
- Debt held by the public is 49 pp of GDP lower than the extended baseline after 30 years, the same as Scenario #2, leaving aside interest-rate changes. The baseline rises from 101% of GDP in 2026 to 175% (pp. 6, 21; Table 5; Figure 1, long-dashed line).
- Method: double the Scenario #2 effects, then adjust because all extra income is capital income taxed at lower rates (pp. 20–21):
  - CBO marginal tax rates on new investment earning the opportunity cost: about 6% for equipment and about 21% for structures. The authors use a 10% weighted average (p. 21).
  - Existing assets and supernormal returns: an effective capital gains rate of 9% (about one-third of 25.9%, reflecting deferral and step-up basis) and a 21% corporate rate, combining to 28% (1 − [1 − .09] × [1 − .21]) (p. 21).
  - A 50/50 split gives 19%, reduced to 15% for tax evasion (p. 21).
  - 15% is a little over half of 25.9%, so the additional tax from income growth is cut by 42% (p. 21).
- Lower interest rates from higher saving would make debt compound more slowly than estimated (p. 20).
- Alternative version: if the extra top-quintile income went to both capital and labour, taxed at 25.9%, debt would fall by 94% of GDP after three decades, ending "smaller relative to GDP than it is today" (p. 21).
- Shifting income from displaced workers' labour to capital might change revenue only a little and is not accounted for (p. 20, n. 7).
- Response costs: UBI of $15,000 per person a year would cost over $5 trillion, nearly all federal spending other than defense and interest (p. 26). Eliminating payroll taxes would require general-revenue funding of Social Security and Medicare (p. 24). Higher capital taxes "would make a substantial difference in the trajectory of government debt" (p. 26). Explicit public ownership could yield more than comparable tax rates because avoidance is harder (p. 28). A debt-financed sovereign wealth fund would be a leveraged bet, like Japan's (Chien, Du and Lustig 2025). Scoring expected equity returns without a risk adjustment could make the budget look rosier than it is (pp. 28–29).
- Shared with Scenarios #1–#3: the Scenario #1 decomposition (30 pp spending, 2 pp revenue, 7 pp GDP and interest on existing debt) (p. 7). Discretionary spending declining relative to GDP (p. 6).
- *Applies across the set:* even at face value, "federal debt would continue to rise significantly relative to GDP in the scenarios we analyzed" (p. 30).

### International economic patterns
Capital's global mobility means that, without international coordination, higher capital taxes raise less revenue and harm growth more than in a closed economy (pp. 26–27). Businesses use the global location of income and costs to cut tax liabilities (p. 28). A sovereign wealth fund investing abroad would diversify but weaken the "we're all in this together" ethos (p. 29). Expected outmigration of taxpayers under California's wealth tax is noted (p. 27). Otherwise not addressed (p. 1).

### Market pricing and scarcity
Interest rates are lower because of higher national saving (p. 20). AI income may be capitalised into share prices before companies list publicly (p. 29). Many people will probably want eldercare and be unable to afford it (p. 25). A survey question cited by the authors posits "very few human jobs but plenty of goods and services" (Ramamurti 2025) (p. 22).

### Financial markets and stability
Higher saving raises the risk of ongoing demand shortfalls and longer periods at the effective lower bound on interest rates (p. 20). A sovereign wealth fund financed by debt would be a leveraged bet on business returns. Venture-type investments could bring prominent losses (pp. 28–29). Individual accounts with marketable assets expose people to the risk of bad financial decisions (p. 30).

## Impact on the social contract

### Effort is rewarded
"When people who want to work cannot find suitable jobs, the harm they experience is not just a loss of income but also a loss of purpose and social contact" (p. 22). In Ramamurti (2025), 67% of 12,000 Americans call a world without traditional jobs "mostly a bad thing—people need jobs to have purpose and dignity" (p. 22). Pope Leo XIV (2026): the goal is "to enable each person to live with dignity through his or her own work" (p. 22).

### Affordable essentials
The fall in employment would cut employer-sponsored health insurance, increasing pressure to expand public insurance (p. 23). Many people will probably want eldercare and be unable to afford it, so public care jobs could complement private provision (p. 25).

### Reliable safety net
Options include UBI (pp. 25–26), expanded unemployment and wage insurance, and training (p. 23). UBI substituted for existing benefits would redirect transfers from the poor to middle-income households, or require much higher spending (p. 26). Social Security and Medicare's earned-benefit status gives them political protection, which payroll-tax elimination could undermine (pp. 23–24). People out of work for years would receive smaller Social Security benefits unless the formula changes (p. 23, n. 9). Asked how to ensure income if AI does almost all paid work, 39% of survey respondents chose a guaranteed public job programme, 31% UBI, 6% no assistance and 24% not sure. Younger people more strongly preferred jobs (p. 22).

### Time for what matters
Policy might encourage earlier retirement and shorter workweeks: lowering the full Social Security age (raised from 65 to 67) and the Medicare age (65), phased reductions in hours, and helping retirees find meaning and social interaction (p. 25). Whether this meets a social need depends on whether early retirees enjoy the time more than the jobless would have (p. 25). Yglesias (2025) suggests a dignified non-working "English gentleman mindset" may need to develop (p. 23).

### A say in your future
Capital owners with a much larger income share "would presumably gain even more political power" (p. 28). Trammell and Patel (2025): "once the masses lose … economic power, due to AI, the political power will follow" (p. 28). Waiting might foreclose options "as vested interests become stronger" (p. 32). Public ownership could be more robust to swings in political sentiment than tax rates (p. 28). Higher capital taxes would enhance the political power of people outside the top strata (p. 26). Amodei (2026) warns billionaires they may "get a bad version designed by a mob" (p. 23).

### A fair share of progress
All gains go to top-quintile capital owners (p. 19). Fink: "There's a real risk artificial intelligence could widen wealth inequality if ownership does not broaden alongside it" (p. 23). Smith (2026): "everyone in America deserves a stake in the AI that their nation created" (p. 28). *Applies across the set:* the authors expect "considerable pressure to ensure that gains from AI would be shared broadly across society, in terms of both incomes and opportunities for meaningful work" (pp. 30–31).

## Other dynamics
Macroeconomic stabilisation: persistent demand shortfalls and the effective lower bound raise pressure for countercyclical fiscal policy, not pursued here (p. 20). Culture and norms: whether social attitudes to not working change if displacement becomes widespread (p. 23). Some advocates want an AI or digital tax to slow AI's advance so that society has time to adjust (p. 27). Industrial policy and self-dealing risks if a sovereign wealth fund invests actively (p. 29). Historically, major economic transformations have brought fiscal innovations (p. 33). The paper excludes the environment, social interactions, national security, AI regulation and other countries (p. 1). *Applies across the set:* alternatives not explored are small AI effects, AI reducing inequality, and the leisure case (pp. 3–4).
